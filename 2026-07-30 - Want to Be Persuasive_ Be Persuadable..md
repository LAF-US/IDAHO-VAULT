Chad Ford was working with Israelis and Palestinians as an international conflict mediator when he got asked a question he couldn’t answer.

“Why are you here?”

Ford was shocked. “I’m like, ‘What are you talking about? I’ve been working here for two decades. It’s primarily the place I’m working on. It’s never needed more conflict resolution than we need right now.’ And they’re like, ‘No, no, no. We look at America right now and we’re asking, why are you working here? You need to be there.’”

“I thought about that all the way home on the airplane,” Ford told attendees at NCSL’s Legislative Summit in Chicago. “I’ve been so focused on this area of the world that had become my expertise, I was missing stuff that was happening in my own backyard. Are we at the same place they are? No. Are we on the same trajectory and path that they are on? All the signs say yes.”

> 
> —Chad Ford, associate professor of religious studies, Utah State University

Conflict isn’t inherently bad, says Ford, a Utah State University associate professor of religious studies who works with the nonprofit [Disagree Better](https://www.nga.org/disagree-better/). “I think it’s actually really important. It’s built into our government. It’s built into our legislative system. I think it’s good that people come together with different viewpoints and ideas and are struggling over different things together.”

When it’s done poorly, however, it comes down to one key principle, Ford says: “We are there to persuade and not to be persuaded. To be persuasive, you have to be persuadable.”

What does that look like? “It starts with you earning cross-party support by offering it first,” he says. “It turns out that in conflict, one of the things that often we get stuck with is, ‘Well, I’ll make a concession if they make a concession. I’ll do something if they do it first. I will disagree better or show respect, but first they need to show me respect.’ But most of the really great work that happens is because someone decides to go first. Someone decides, ‘I’m going to be the person that’s going to reach across the aisle here. I’m going to be the person that’s going to be curious.’”

Getting curious is a first step toward resolution, Ford says. “Don’t just focus on the position. Think about what sits underneath that position. Why are people taking the positions that they’re taking? We have to be able to figure out, is this just self-interest? Is there a deeper value or worldview that’s underlying it that is sincerely held by someone? And we have to figure out what that is and how to address it. And so when someone says, ‘I’ll never vote for that,’ we have to ask why? Are you protecting your constituency? Did you make a promise that you wouldn’t do that? Is there a value or worldview that this legislation holds that you feel will compromise who you are as a person? We need to understand the deeper issues of why.”

Ford calls it “the magic of the seven whys. Keep asking people why. You can use different words than why, but you get the gist. Maybe why, why, why, why, why will annoy some people. And every time, you’ll get a slightly different answer.”

When you’re trying to navigate conflict, it turns out debate isn’t particularly helpful, Ford says. “Because what most people say is, ‘As I’m debating, the only things I’m listening for are the things I disagree with so that I can push back.’ Instead of trying to deeply understand why it is that people feel the way that they do. So how are they seeing the issue? What’s driving their position? What’s at risk for them?”

The only way to find out: Listen.

“We interrupt. We don’t reflect back what we heard. We do the yeah, yeah, yeah. I get it. I get it. I get it. Now let me get back to my point. We don’t validate—‘Oh, I can understand why you might feel that way. Oh, I could see how that would be really hard for you. Oh, I never really thought about how that might impact you. I'm really sorry. When I was framing this, I didn't see that.’”

When people feel heard, they’re more willing to negotiate, Ford says.

“Even if the solution isn’t what they had hoped it would be,” he says. “They are more willing to live with that wasn’t what I wanted it to look like, when they feel listened to and heard and understood.”
