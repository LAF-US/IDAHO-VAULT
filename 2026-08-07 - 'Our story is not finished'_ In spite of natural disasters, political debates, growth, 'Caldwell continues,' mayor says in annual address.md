About seven months into his mayoral career, Caldwell Mayor Eric Phillips experienced a full circle moment on Thursday.

Phillips delivered his first state of the city address from the auditorium of Vallivue High School, his alma mater. State of the city speeches tend to touch on city happenings and accomplishments over the previous year. Phillips won election in November 2025, and took office in early January 2026.

While highlighting the city’s progress during his term, Phillips thanked employees for their dedication and commitment. He also described his optimism for where the city is headed and its people’s ability to come together despite differing opinions on key issues.

After reflecting on his first months in office, Phillips said he would sum it up with the phrase, “Caldwell continues.”

“Our story is not finished. In many ways, it’s just beginning,” Phillips said. “Caldwell continues, and I can’t wait to write the next chapter with all of you in here.”

## On trains, crosswalks, storms, and democracy

One issue that surfaced early in Phillips’s tenure was revamping the city’s railroad crossings to create a “quiet zone” in which train conductors don’t need to use their horns. Establishing a quiet zone requires constructing changes to crossings, typically raised medians, to prevent drivers from going around lowered railroad arms when a train is nearby.

Though business owners were largely in favor of a quiet zone, some community members and the council said the city did not need such a zone and feared that it could lead to pedestrian fatalities. Ultimately, the council voted to move ahead with implementing the zone, [as BoiseDev reported](https://boisedev.com/news/2026/03/26/caldwell-moves-forward-with-railroad-quite-zone-with-one-dissenting-vote/). Looking back, Phillips said the differences of opinion expressed during discussions on the quiet zone and other challenging topics reflect a functional community.

“Democracy isn’t built on unanimous agreement — it’s built on participation, listening, and respecting one another enough to keep talking even when we disagree,” Phillips said. “When the meetings end, and the headlines fade, we’re still neighbors.”

Phillips also noted the city’s swift action on mobilizing to install a crossing signal near Caldwell High School. [As Idaho EdNews reported](https://www.idahoednews.org/top-news/caldwell-leaders-lobby-lawmakers-for-ways-to-make-streets-safer-for-kids/), the impetus for the crossing followed three separate incidents over 45 days of students being struck by vehicles in the city while walking to school.

In June, a storm brought some of the heaviest hail the city had ever seen, causing flooding and displacing dozens of residents. Phillips said the city’s response included first responders helping with the immediate aftermath, working with Canyon County’s emergency operations manager, and working with other local, state, and federal partners. Community members stepped up to offer help, he said.

“People didn’t ask whose responsibility it was,” Phillips said. “They asked, ‘how can I help?’”

## ‘Investing in tomorrow’

Phillips said that the city is close, or has surpassed, 80,000 residents, depending on the source.

As the city considers future growth and mitigating its effects, Phillips acknowledged that it’s a sore subject for many residents.

“Growth can be exciting, but growth is also very hard,” he said. “Change can test our patience. Trust me, I know. But we’re also investing in tomorrow, not just maintaining yesterday, to preserve what makes Caldwell special, and preparing for the generations that will call this city home long after we’re gone.”

Phillips said that though Caldwell’s median income is about $70,000 annually, “many households have not reached that income level.” But as commercial areas grow along Karcher Rd. and Highway 20/26, Phillips predicted additional jobs will come to the city.

“Those jobs are going to put people here to work, and create less drive time, hopefully, on our Treasure Valley Roads (as we) keep those jobs close to home.”

## Air traffic control tower, fire stations

Phillips said that a number of new city developments are in the works, or already underway.

He said that the city received a $2 million grant from the Federal Aviation Administration to construct an air traffic control tower for the Caldwell Executive Airport.

“It’s a major investment that’s going to enhance safety and support future growth,” Phillips said. At the moment, the airport is playing a needed role in wildfire response efforts, he said.

[As BoiseDev reported](https://boisedev.com/news/2025/03/18/caldwell-airport-march-18-2025/), the airport does not have a control tower, despite being the busiest in Idaho.

The city is also in the process of building a new Fire Station 1 on the site of its former city hall at 411 Blaine St., Phillips said. The city is scheduled to break ground on a new Fire Station 4, planned near the intersection of Karcher Rd. and Lake Ave.

The city is also considering the possibility of spinning off its fire department into a separate district, a question that must go before voters, Phillips said.

The city has continued to see a decrease in crime, with overall crime down 20%, Phillips said. The city’s police department has expanded its traffic enforcement program, including increased traffic stops.
