Anthony Salazar, 48, stood by a rawhide drum, as his wife, Stephanie, helped adjust and tighten the many straps and laces of his regalia.

“The colors of it are white, black, red and yellow, the colors of the four directions,” he explained, as a steady morning breeze made the yarn frills on his sleeves billow gently. “All the regalia is connected to nature.”

The Salazars chatted with each other as couples do, while a group of men approached the drum. They arranged chairs and tested the microphone, as another man swirled a stick of burning sage as visitors passed through toward a shaded section of chairs.

At first glance, it could pass for any other summer powwow getting underway.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_7843-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1493&ssl=1)

Anthony Salazar (right) dances before a group of roughly 80 guests during the 2026 OSCI Many Nations Powwow in Salem. (Photo by Brian Bull)

“I’m in here for two counts of murder, and I got a life sentence. I’ve been in 24 years,” said Salazar, who is Apache, originally from San Bernardino, California. He and several other dancers stood in a grassy field circled by large coils of barbed wire and imposing guard towers.  
  
“I’m very fortunate to be here, it means a lot. This is actually the first powwow where I’ve actually dressed up in regalia,” he said. “And I’m going to dance.”

Before his wife Stephanie left to sit in the visitor’s section, she told Underscore Native News that this is the first powwow she has attended anywhere.

No one present knew exactly when the first Many Nations Powwow was held at the Oregon State Correctional Institution (OSCI), an all-men’s medium-security prison with an inmate population nearing 900. But Charlie Tladatsi, of Mescalero, Tsalagi and white descent, said it’s been over three decades, back when he was first incarcerated at OSCI.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_7711-scaled.jpg?resize=2000%2C2667&ssl=1)

Having fitted and arranged his regalia, Anthony and Stephanie Salazar pause for a photo before the start of the 2026 OSCI Many Nations Powwow on July 18, 2026. (Photo by Brian Bull)

“It’s been my experience over the last 30 years or so that when people connect with their culture—the things that are already in their blood and their DNA—it helps perspective,” said Tladatsi, massaging his graying goatee. “So if we change the way we think, we change the way we behave. And a lot of us are here because of the way we behave. So the idea is, if we get well here, we’re well when we go home.”

All the incarcerated people interviewed for this story are “lifers,” meaning they’ve been convicted of serious crimes and are looking at spending most—if not the rest of their existence—inside this concrete and steel fortress on Salem’s north side.

It’s a fact none of them glosses over. But all the men expressed that being able to practice Native traditions, even in prison, is empowering and gives them hope that redemption and connection are still possible.

“I come from a home of addiction and depression,” said Nestor Diaz-Miller, of Cow Creek Band of Umpqua heritage. Sentenced in 2008 to 25 years to life for murder, he gestured towards the wooden frame of the OSCI sweat lodge as well as the drummers sitting down near him. “I’ve dealt with suicidal thoughts and actions in my past, and being able to focus on the sweat lodge and that drum literally saved my life.”

Diaz-Miller said he grew up in a Hispanic household but knew he was Native American, and felt it in his heart. He says at 37, younger inmates who want to participate in the annual powwow look to him for guidance.

“How do you sing this song? How do you drum? How do you dance? How do you practice our ancestral ways?” he recalled, remembering the many questions he too once asked. “And I’m able to carry that on because it was carried down to me.”

**A year’s worth of preparation**

The prison’s Many Nations Culture Club (MNCC) organizes its summer powwow, with participants practicing dances and songs throughout the year. Regalia is handed down by those who’ve designed and constructed them, with their new users making modifications or additions.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_7911-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1536&ssl=1)

Drummers sing at the 2026 Many Nations Powwow at the Oregon State Correctional Institution. (Photo by Brian Bull)

“The only one thing I pieced together was my prayer stick right here,” said Salazar, running his fingers across it. “I got a feather right here. My brother passed away, so this is in honor of him.”

The MNCC has worked to help causes important to the Native community. At last year’s powwow the club announced [a scholarship honoring Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women](https://www.oregon.gov/doc/family-connections/Documents/osci-mmiw-scholarship.pdf), with funds collectively gathered from the Siletz Charitable Contribution Fund and personal contributions from inmates’ relatives and friends.

One of the primary organizers and representative of the club is Shawn Jeffers-Monro, who said he’s been adopted by the Klamath Modoc people and has walked the “red road” for 20 years now. Like many others, he was brought into the club and taught the ways of powwow and ceremony.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_7729-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1487&ssl=1)

Several drummers help Stephen Mathew Lister-Galiza with his regalia ahead of the OSCI Many Nations Powwow on July 18, 2026. (Photo by Brian Bull)

“I’m one of the drummers, and to be able to share that in ceremony with all the honored guests from the outside is amazing,” said Jeffers-Monro. “It’s very moving, just to be able to see the community aspect and the healing aspect of it, too. A lot of brothers, we practice all year.”

Like many of the men participating in the Many Nations Powwow, Jeffers-Monro was excited to have the event where outside guests are allowed to visit and participate. Intertribal dances, a feast with frybread, deer and elk meat, and even a powwow version of “musical chairs” saw incarcerated people holding hands and laughing with loved ones and friends. The impact of even having one day together means a lot to those doing time behind bars.

“My mother was dying of cancer three years ago,” said Jeffers-Monro, “and I was able to have this ceremony with her and last rites. And to be able to say goodbye to her.”

**Honored guests**

The OSCI allows inmates to invite people to the powwow, which requires a background check, strict restrictions against certain objects or clothes, and a prohibition against phones past the main gate and inspection site. But if all goes well, guests can be invited back time and time again.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_8001-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1536&ssl=1)

Aliza Kaplan, a law professor at Lewis and Clark Law School, says she’s been a return guest several times for the OSCI Many Nations Powwow. (Photo by Brian Bull)

“I run a law clinic at Lewis and Clark Law School called the Criminal Justice Reform Clinic,” said law professor Aliza Kaplan. By her count, she’s been invited with her students to the Many Nations Powwow about five times.

“I feel really honored to be invited to events like this,” said Kaplan. “It keeps me and my law students connected. Not just in a legal way, but in a human way.”

Kaplan added that not all prisons accommodate cultural organizations for a number of reasons, but said OSCI does a “really good job” at letting their inmates explore their identities, their histories, and “be in community.”

“The more people get to know truly who they are, an identity like their Native roots, having community, these are all really, really important factors towards rehabilitation.”

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_7885-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1536&ssl=1)

Ed Edmo, visiting storyteller, works a hand puppet while sharing a Native legend during the 2026 OSCI Many Nations Powwow. (Photo by Brian Bull)

Another visitor on the “honored guests” roster was storyteller Ed Edmo, a Shoshone-Bannock tribal member with Yakima and Nez Perce lineage. Perched in a chair and surrounded by hand puppets, Edmo told the crowd stories about Coyote and other characters from Native folklore, cracking jokes and weaving in historical insights about places like Celilo Falls and other important places to regional tribes.

**Liberation within the walls**

As the powwow’s grand entry kicked off, a lone red-tailed hawk circled the space quietly. It was a subtle but powerful image for the people below who’d worked to make the event happen.

“It’s a very special event,” said Kaplan. “It’s the one that feels maybe the most free of all the events that I go to around the state, with people dancing, singing, hugging and just really being in community.”

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/IMG_7736-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1536&ssl=1)

High above the powwow grounds, a red-tailed hawk circled above as the event’s grand entry began. (Photo by Brian Bull)

For Diaz-Miller, the ability to practice the powwow tradition is also a unifying event where no one is subject to hierarchy or judgment, calling it a “gathering of nations” brought together to celebrate life.

“We focus on the things that bring us positivity, bring us hope, bring us joy,” said Diaz-Miller. “This is for me what has saved my life.”

As Salazar made the final adjustments to his regalia, he said that the powwow is something that resonates well past walls, guard towers and barbed wire.

“A lot of this helps keep the culture alive, so our people are not forgotten. The world remembers us.”
