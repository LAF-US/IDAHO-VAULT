Summer Twerago and Niki Baxter spend most workdays weighing loads, writing invoices, and helping customers at the Valley County Transfer Station.

But ask many of the transfer station’s regular visitors what they’re known for, and the answer is much simpler – dog treats.

“Beans knows when we’re driving up the road,” McCall resident Pierce Ramsay said of his two-year-old corgi. “As soon as he sees the window and the Transfer Ladies, he’s excited and ready for his treat.”

Several years ago, Twerago and Baxter started buying treats with their own money to hand out to dogs accompanying their owners on the weekly trip to the dump. Their generosity has earned them a reputation as “The Transfer Ladies” — a name the women joked they happily embrace since the alternative would be “The Dump Ladies.”

“They’re always kind, even when I’ve been dumb, like putting 2×4’s in the brush pile,” Ramsay said.

![](https://valleylookout.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/08/valleylookout.com-transfer-ladies-turn-trips-to-the-dump-into-treats-img-2306.jpeg)

Summer Twerago feeds a treat to Beans from the Valley County Transfer Site gatehouse. Photo: Courtesy Pierce Ramsay

From the transfer station’s small gatehouse, Baxter and Twerago process invoices, answer questions, keep traffic moving, and greet a steady stream of customers. Along the way, they’ve come to know many customers — and their pets — by name.

“I can hear one dog barking with excitement before they’re even all the way up the road,” Twerago said. “A couple of people bring their cats, so we now stock cat treats, too!”

Handing out treats has become part of the job, but the women are careful to ask every owner before offering one.

“We always ask first,” Baxter said. “A few dogs have dietary restrictions, or are older and missing teeth, so they don’t get treats. And sometimes it’s a puppy, or a working dog, that’s only given treats for training.”

Some customers return the favor by donating dog treats to the Transfer Ladies, while others drop off homemade cookies and other baked goods.

Baxter and Twerago estimate that about half the vehicles coming through the gate have one or more dogs inside. Seeing those familiar faces, they said, is one of the best parts of the job.

“It makes our day seeing all the amazing dogs and handing them treats,” Twerago said. “A couple of times, though, we ran out of treats. The dogs were so sad.”

## Generational connection

Both women have worked at the transfer station for nearly a decade, though Baxter’s connection to the facility goes back much longer.

Her father and grandfather both worked at the facility when she was a child.

“I remember sometimes bringing lunch for him and Grandpa, watching them use the bulldozer and loader to push trash into the landfill,” Baxter said.

![City of McCall - Visiting? 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/57592d17-0c69-4d28-a6e9-c5d629733da1.png)

Baxter’s grandfather once owned the property where the facility now sits, leasing the land to the county when it operated as a landfill. The county later purchased the property, covered the landfill, and built the transfer station that serves residents today.

“Working here allows me to feel close to grandpa, who’s no longer with us,” she said.

The transfer station at 240 Spink Ln. near Lake Fork collects about 40 million pounds of garbage each year and is open Monday through Saturday from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m.
