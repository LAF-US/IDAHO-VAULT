# Alchemy

**Alchemy** is an ancient body of practice and philosophy — at once
proto-chemistry, cosmology, and spiritual discipline — aimed at the
**transformation of matter and self**. Its most famous goals were the
*philosopher's stone* (a substance said to perfect base metals into gold and to
confer healing or longevity via the *elixir of life*), but its deeper claim was
that the same laws govern the workbench and the soul: to purify a metal and to
purify a person were, to the alchemist, the same operation read at two scales.

This note builds the subject **from first principles** — the axioms, then the
notation, then the historical lineage, then the cycle of the Great Work — and
closes by relating it to the vault's other symbolic language, the
[[Language of flowers]]. It is an original synthesis grounded in the sources
listed at the end, not a verbatim copy.

## First principles

Alchemy is not arbitrary mysticism; it is built on a small set of axioms that,
granted, make its operations follow logically.

1. **The unity of matter — *prima materia*.** All substances are held to share
   one underlying "first matter." If matter is one beneath its forms, then in
   principle any substance can be reduced to that ground and re-formed as
   another. Transmutation is not magic but *re-cooking* the same dough.
2. **The four elements and four qualities (Aristotelian physics).** Earth,
   water, air, and fire are not the modern elements but combinations of two
   primary *qualities* — hot/cold and wet/dry. Fire is hot + dry, air hot +
   wet, water cold + wet, earth cold + dry. Because the elements differ only by
   quality, change a quality and you change the element — the theoretical
   license for transmutation.
3. **The three principles — *tria prima*.** Systematized in the Arabic
   tradition (sulfur + mercury) and extended by **Paracelsus** to a third:
   - **Sulfur** — the principle of combustibility, soul, the volatile/active.
   - **Mercury** — the principle of fusibility/metallicity, spirit, the fluid.
   - **Salt** — the principle of solidity, body, the fixed.
   Every substance is a particular balance of the three; the Work rebalances
   them.
4. **Correspondence — "as above, so below."** From the Hermetic *Emerald
   Tablet* attributed to Hermes Trismegistus (see [[Hermeticism]]): the
   macrocosm (heavens) and the
   microcosm (matter, body) mirror one another. This is why the **metals are
   bound to the planets** and why operating on a metal is also operating on a
   fate and a faculty of the self.

## The notation — a symbolic language

Because the work was secret, dangerous, and contested, alchemists wrote in a
dense glyph-language rather than plain prose. Two of its core lexicons:

**The classical elements** were written as triangles: fire △, water ▽, air △
with a bar, earth ▽ with a bar — up-pointing for the "ascending" (hot)
elements, down-pointing for the "descending" (cold) ones.

**The seven metals and their planets.** The seven metals known since antiquity
were each fused with a classical planet (the associations had stabilized by the
15th century):

| Planet | Metal |
| --- | --- |
| Sun ☉ | Gold |
| Moon ☽ | Silver |
| Mercury ☿ | Mercury (quicksilver) |
| Venus ♀ | Copper |
| Mars ♂ | Iron |
| Jupiter ♃ | Tin |
| Saturn ♄ | Lead |

To name lead was to invoke Saturn — heaviness, age, melancholy — and the goal
of perfecting lead "up" to gold was equally a movement from Saturn to the Sun.

## Historical lineage

- **Hellenistic Egypt (1st–3rd c. CE).** Alchemy crystallized in Alexandria
  where Greek natural philosophy, Egyptian metallurgy, and Hermetic spirituality
  met. **Zosimos of Panopolis** is among the earliest named alchemists,
  recording distillation apparatus and symbolic dream-visions of death and
  rebirth.
- **The Islamic Golden Age.** Scholars preserved and vastly extended the
  Greek corpus. **Jābir ibn Hayyān** ("Geber" in Latin) systematized theory and
  laboratory technique and advanced the sulfur–mercury theory of the metals;
  **al-Rāzī** pushed practical, classificatory chemistry. The very word
  *alchemy* reaches English through Arabic *al-kīmiyāʾ*.
- **Latin medieval & Renaissance Europe.** Translation of Arabic works
  reignited the art in the West; figures real and pseudonymous (pseudo-Geber,
  the *Rosarium philosophorum*, etc.) circulated. **Paracelsus** (16th c.)
  redirected alchemy toward medicine (*iatrochemistry*) and fixed the *tria
  prima*.
- **The Scientific Revolution and the parting of ways.** Alchemy was
  respectable enough that **Isaac Newton** and **Robert Boyle** both practiced
  it in earnest. Yet Boyle's insistence on experiment and mechanical
  explanation — *The Sceptical Chymist* (1661) — helped dismantle both the
  four-element and three-principle schemes, and over the following decades the
  empirical residue hardened into **chemistry** while the esoteric program fell
  out of repute by the 1720s.
- **The psychological afterlife.** In the 20th century **Carl Jung** re-read
  alchemy as a projected map of inner transformation — the *opus* as
  individuation — which is the lens through which much popular alchemy is now
  received. See the companion node [[Jung and alchemy]].

## The Great Work — the cycle (*Magnum Opus*)

The heart of alchemy is the **Magnum Opus**, the staged process of producing
the stone. It is conventionally described as a color cycle — matter (and, by
correspondence, the practitioner) is broken down and rebuilt:

1. **Nigredo — the blackening.** Dissolution, putrefaction, the *massa
   confusa*. The raw material is reduced to undifferentiated black matter.
   Psychologically: crisis, the *melancholia*, Jung's confrontation with the
   shadow.
2. **Albedo — the whitening.** *Ablutio* — the washing-away of impurities.
   Light and clarity return to the purified first matter; the silver/lunar
   stage.
3. **Citrinitas — the yellowing.** The dawning, the solar light beginning to
   gild the work — the "peacock's tail" of iridescence into gold. (After the
   15th century this stage was often folded into the rubedo, giving a
   three-stage cycle: black → white → red.)
4. **Rubedo — the reddening.** *Coagulatio* — the work takes final, permanent
   form: the red stone, the unification of opposites (the *coniunctio*), the
   completed Great Work.

## At the end of the cycle: the rose, and the relationship to floriography

The cycle ends in **red** — *rubedo*, the reddening, the perfected stone — and
red is where alchemy's symbolic language and the [[Language of flowers]] touch.
The completed Work was repeatedly figured as a **rose** or **red flower**: the
*Rosarium philosophorum* ("Rosary of the Philosophers") makes the rose-garden
the emblem of the opus, and the later **[[Rosicrucianism|Rosicrucian]]** current bound rose to
cross. In floriography, that same bloom — the **red rose** — is the plainest
sign in the lexicon: *love*, the union of two.

So the two systems rhyme. Each is a language in which **the object is the
message** rather than plain speech:

- Floriography encodes **sentiment** in a *flower* and the *gesture* of giving
  it.
- Alchemy encodes **operation and correspondence** in a *glyph*, a *metal*, a
  *color*, a *planet*.

Both also keep the family of *secret-speech* practices — alchemy's deliberate
obscurity shades into the wider esoteric vocabulary, including the distinction
between **theurgy** ("god-work": ritual aimed at union with or invocation of
the divine) and **thaumaturgy** ("wonder-working": the practical working of
marvels). And both leave a telling idiom in ordinary language: the rose that
ends the alchemical cycle is also the rose of *sub rosa* — "under the rose,"
i.e., in confidence. The cycle closes where the flowers begin.
