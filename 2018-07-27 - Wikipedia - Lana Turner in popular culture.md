Turner photographed in 1944

Since her rise to fame in the 1940s, American film actress [Lana Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lana_Turner "Lana Turner") (1921–1995) has appeared and been referenced in numerous works across literature, film, art, and music. Her [glamorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glamour_\(presentation\) "Glamour (presentation)") persona and publicized personal troubles have contributed to her recurring prevalence in [popular culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_culture "Popular culture").[^1] [^2] [^3]

Turner first entered the popular culture lexicon under the nickname "the [Sweater Girl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweater_girl "Sweater girl") " due to her feature film debut in *[They Won't Forget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/They_Won't_Forget "They Won't Forget")* (1937), in which she appeared in a form-fitting sweater that accentuated her bust. She was the first known person to be given the nickname, which went on to be applied to numerous actresses throughout the 1940s and 1950s. Her subsequent rise to fame at [Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer "Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer") and establishment as a [sex symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_symbol "Sex symbol") enhanced Turner's profile, and she became a popular [pin-up model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin-up_model "Pin-up model") throughout [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II").

While she enjoyed significant popularity as one of MGM's biggest stars, she was also subject to vociferous media attention over her numerous romantic affairs, eight total marriages, and her daughter's [1958 killing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lana_Turner#1958%E2%80%931959:_Johnny_Stompanato_homicide_scandal "Lana Turner") of Turner's lover, [Johnny Stompanato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_Stompanato "Johnny Stompanato"), during a domestic struggle. The media focus on her storied personal life (particularly the Stompanato homicide) contributed to her cultural prevalence at the time, even sometimes serving as the basis for novels and films.

In her later career and after her death, Turner would go on to appear depicted in numerous artistic works, as well as be studied by social critics and academics in discussions surrounding Hollywood, [film theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_theory "Film theory"), [gay icons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_icon "Gay icon"), and [camp aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_\(style\) "Camp (style)").

## Timeline

### 20th century

#### Discovery story

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/61/Lana_Turner_circa_1942.jpg/250px-Lana_Turner_circa_1942.jpg)

Lana Turner, 1942

The story of Turner's chance discovery by [William Wilkerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_R._Wilkerson "William R. Wilkerson") at a Hollywood [malt shop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda_shop "Soda shop") at age 16 was widely circulated during her lifetime, and went on to become a part of Hollywood mythology.[^4] [^5] Varied accounts as to where the discovery occurred were recounted in publication and via [word of mouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_of_mouth "Word of mouth") during her early career in the late 1930s and early 1940s, with a popular retelling having the meeting occur at [Schwab's Pharmacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwab's_Pharmacy "Schwab's Pharmacy").[^6] [^7] Shortly after Turner's rise to fame, the owner of the malt shop in which the discovery took place erected a metal plaque on the seat which Turner had occupied when she met Wilkerson, and soon the shop became inundated with young women hoping to experience the same chance occurrence.[^5] Wilkerson's son, journalist William Wilkerson III, wrote on the lasting cultural impact of Turner's discovery in 1995:

> On that January day, a Hollywood myth sprang to life. In one brief, glittering moment, a schoolgirl was plucked from a soda fountain counter and catapulted into the eternal firmament of stardom. But, curiously, while Hollywood's history is littered with dozens of noteworthy discovery stories, this one stuck. Lana's much quoted "discovery" became one of the town's most enduring legends. It became famous as "the Hollywood Cinderella Story" because it combined a young girl, [Cinderella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinderella "Cinderella"); a powerful publisher, the handsome prince; and a letter to agent [Zeppo Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeppo_Marx "Zeppo Marx"), the glass slipper that transformed her life.[^5]

Scholar Kim Fields similarly wrote about the lasting cultural relevance of Turner and her discovery story in her 2007 book *An Intimate Affair: Women, Lingerie, and Sexuality*, noting that it is emblematic of "the [American Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Dream "American Dream") fulfilled... Because of her, being discovered at a soda fountain has become almost as cherished an ideal as being [born in a log cabin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log_cabin#Symbolism "Log cabin")." [^2]

#### "The Sweater Girl"

Following her feature film debut in [Mervyn LeRoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mervyn_LeRoy "Mervyn LeRoy") 's *[They Won't Forget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/They_Won't_Forget "They Won't Forget")* (1937), Turner was the first woman to receive the nickname the " [Sweater Girl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweater_girl "Sweater girl") " due to her form-fitting attire which accentuated her bust.[^8] [^9] Though she herself detested the nickname, it was one with which she became synonymous over the duration of her career,[^10] and it would come to be applied to several actresses throughout the 1940s and 1950s.[^11] Several years after the release of *They Won't Forget*, *[Modern Screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Screen "Modern Screen")* journalist Nancy Squires wrote an extensive article on Turner, in which she noted that she had "made a sweater look like something [Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleopatra "Cleopatra") was saving for the next visiting [Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar")." [^12]

Turner's rise to fame and establishment as a Hollywood [sex symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_symbol "Sex symbol") [^13] following *They Won't Forget* was concurrent with [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), and she became a prominent [pin-up model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin-up_model "Pin-up model") at the time, appearing painted on the noses of U.S. fighter planes bearing the title "Tempest Turner." [^14] MGM appointed her to tour the United States [West Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_of_the_United_States "West Coast of the United States") and sell [war bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_bonds "War bonds") in various cities in the summer of 1942.[^15] During the tour, Turner began giving out kisses to the highest buyers, and by the end of the 10-week tour, had sold $5.25 million (equivalent to $103.4 million in 2025) in war bonds.[^15]

#### Depictions in art

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/Lana_Turner_and_Frank_Sinatra%2C_1944.png/250px-Lana_Turner_and_Frank_Sinatra%2C_1944.png)

Turner with Frank Sinatra c. 1944

One of the earliest [popular culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_culture "Popular culture") references to Turner is in [Frank Sinatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Sinatra "Frank Sinatra") 's 1944 recorded version of " [Nancy (with the Laughing Face)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_\(with_the_Laughing_Face\) "Nancy (with the Laughing Face)")." [^16] During this time, Turner and Sinatra had been carrying on a romantic affair.[^17] She would later be named in [Nina Simone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nina_Simone "Nina Simone") 's 1958 version of the song " [My Baby Just Cares for Me](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_Baby_Just_Cares_for_Me "My Baby Just Cares for Me")," which appeared on her album *[Little Girl Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Girl_Blue_\(album\) "Little Girl Blue (album)")*.[^18]

### 21st century

In 2002, Turner was commemorated by artist Eloy Torrez in an outdoor [mural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mural "Mural"), *Portrait of Hollywood*, painted on the auditorium of [Hollywood High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood_High_School "Hollywood High School"), her alma mater, alongside numerous other famous alumni.[^27] [Beyoncé Knowles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beyonc%C3%A9_Knowles "Beyoncé Knowles"), as a member of Destiny's Child, for a while adopted a look and fashion sense reminiscent of Turner. American singer-songwriter Elizabeth Grant (b. 1985), better known as [Lana Del Rey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lana_Del_Rey "Lana Del Rey"), devised her stage name inspired by Turner and the [Ford Del Rey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Del_Rey "Ford Del Rey") sedan.[^28] [^29] A 2011 article on Del Rey in *The New Yorker* described Turner as a "titan of red-lip, Old Hollywood glamour." [^28]

## Gay icon

Turner's daughter [Cheryl Crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheryl_Crane "Cheryl Crane"), privately [came out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coming_out "Coming out") as a [lesbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesbian "Lesbian") to her parents at the age of thirteen, and they remained supportive of her.[^30]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/L%C3%ADmitation_of_Life-_Lana%27s_First_Modeling_Gig_%288634048811%29.jpg/250px-L%C3%ADmitation_of_Life-_Lana%27s_First_Modeling_Gig_%288634048811%29.jpg)

Turner portrayed in drag in a 2013 a Chicago production of Límitation of Life, based on Imitation of Life (1959)

Beginning in the 1970s, it was observed by journalist [Sally Quinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sally_Quinn "Sally Quinn") of *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* that Turner had accumulated a following of [gay men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_male_culture "Gay male culture"),[^31] similar to her contemporaries [Judy Garland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judy_Garland "Judy Garland"), [Joan Crawford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Crawford "Joan Crawford"), [Bette Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bette_Davis "Bette Davis"), and others.[^32] One attendee at a 1975 gala celebrating her career told Quinn: "She represents... homosexuals' fantasies at a certain age in their lives. They identify with her and she's a survivor. It's like they're saying, 'Let's go see how she's survived.'" [^31] Subsequent associations with Turner as a [gay icon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_icon "Gay icon") have centered on her personal struggles and triumphs, as well as her glamorous persona and the perceived [camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_\(style\) "Camp (style)") aesthetics of several of her films.[^33] Scholars such as [Susan Sontag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Sontag "Susan Sontag") noted the camp qualities of such films as *[The Prodigal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prodigal_\(film\) "The Prodigal (film)")* (1955),[^34] a Biblical costume drama in which Turner portrayed a pagan temptress.[^35] [^36]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Lana_Turner_in_The_Postman_Always_Rings_Twice_-_publicity_portrait.jpg/250px-Lana_Turner_in_The_Postman_Always_Rings_Twice_-_publicity_portrait.jpg)

Turner in The Postman Always Rings Twice (1946)

Scholar Georges-Claude Guilbert notes Turner's impact as a gay icon as far back as her appearance in *[The Postman Always Rings Twice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Postman_Always_Rings_Twice_\(1946_film\) "The Postman Always Rings Twice (1946 film)")* (1946): "\[Upon the release of *[Imitation of Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imitation_of_Life_\(1959_film\) "Imitation of Life (1959 film)")*,\] Turner's gay iconocity quotient had already reached a very high level 12 years previously with the release of *The Postman Always Rings Twice* Even 70 years later one never comes out unscathed from a viewing. The head wrap! The shoes! The shorts! That movie is a lesson in style and in it, Turner is a lesson in stylishness." [^37] Additionally, Guilbert notes Turner's personal scandals (such as the 1958 Stompanato killing), frequent romances and eight-total marriages as additional factors in her consideration as a gay icon.[^37] Scholar Robin Griffiths also considers Turner a "camp icon" due to her "alternately deadpan and overblown performances." [^38] Author Sam Staggs noted of Turner in *Imitation of Life*:

> To Hollywood, Lana *was* her tight sweater. To [Ross Hunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Hunter "Ross Hunter"), a closeted gay man unaroused by female flesh, Lana without the sweater meant the opportunity to dress her up in two dozen stunning outfits, like a live [Barbie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbie "Barbie") doll. Even that famous sweater, in its day, had symbolized a certain kind of heterosexual camp, redolent of locker rooms, and [burlesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlesque "Burlesque"). Hunter, hugely abetted by [Douglas Sirk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Sirk "Douglas Sirk"), created an entirely new goddess, a Lana Turner who, like [Myra Breckinridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myra_Breckinridge "Myra Breckinridge"), embodies heterosexual and homosexual camp while remaining oddly sexless.[^39]
