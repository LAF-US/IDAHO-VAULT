A few days after the Willow fire, the Tennessee Pass Cafe in nearby Leadville committed to giving free meals to first responders.

That was about a month ago.

“We didn’t think that it would last this long, but they appreciate it, and we appreciate them,” said Shannon Vitale, the restaurant’s manager.

The free-food policy is a small example of how the historic mining community and outdoor hub is learning to live with a long-lived wildfire next door.

Fire crews have gained the upper hand against the fire burning on Mount Massive outside of town.

Recent precipitation has helped crews corral the fire behind increasingly fortified containment lines. Local organizers have also started to reschedule major events, such as the [Leadville Trail 100](https://www.denver7.com/news/mountains/could-the-leadville-100-save-the-town-from-their-tourism-slump-businesses-residents-hope-so), now set for late August.

But after a record [snow drought](https://www.cpr.org/show-segment/high-hopes-for-monsoon-climate-change-compounds-snow-drought/) last winter, many experts overseeing the fire response don’t expect the moisture will fully extinguish the blaze anytime soon.

“To be honest, this thing is not going out,” said Joe Goudsward, a National Weather Service meteorologist assigned to the Willow fire. “You’re going to see puffs of smoke on this thing probably until the snow flies,” he said.

It’s a reality sinking in across the Mountain West as fires smolder well into the summer. Officials overseeing other blazes around Colorado — such as the [Aspen Acres fire](https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/28/flooding-beulah-aspen-acres-burn-scar/) near Pueblo and the [Gold Mountain fire](https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/27/ouray-flooding-gold-mountain-fire-burn-scar/) near Ouray — also don’t expect the monsoon can erase the deficit left by a bone-dry winter. If those predictions play out, those fires could linger for months, or roar back to life under the right conditions.

The situation could further challenge already [strained firecrews](https://www.cpr.org/2026/06/25/colorado-lack-fema-support-wildfire/) and mountain towns [missing their normal influx of summer tourists](https://www.cpr.org/2026/07/28/colorado-tourism-growth-falling/). As [climate change drives snow droughts](https://www.minesnewsroom.com/news/climate-change-made-years-snow-drought-western-us-four-times-more-likely-new-colorado-school), the long-lived fires might also offer a preview of a new normal in the coming decades.

## The rise of marathon fires

Anyone who’s made a campfire knows that thicker logs burn longer. The same concept can help explain why rain might not fully extinguish some of Colorado’s biggest wildfires.

Jonathan Coop, a professor studying fire and forest ecology at Western State University, said the fires are burning through bigger fuels, like large logs and entire trees.

“These things are burning in high-elevation forests that have tons of fuel, and heavy fuels like beetle-kill tree trunks and deep litter and duff,” Coop said. “These things can smolder for a long time.”

In Colorado, outbreaks of [mountain pine beetle](https://www.cpr.org/2026/03/04/colorado-pine-beetle-task-force-wildfires/) and other tree diseases have plagued entire hillsides. Whether those conditions increase fire risk or fire severity remains a topic of [intense debate](https://www.summitdaily.com/news/colorado-wildfire-risk-beetle-killed-forests/), and it’s unclear how much of a role the outbreaks are playing in the state’s current wildfire season.

What’s more apparent is the impact of the recent snow drought on heavier vegetation, known as “1,000-hour fuels” among wildland firefighters. The term refers to how quickly different wildfire fuels gain or lose moisture. In wood between three and eight inches in diameter, it can take a prolonged period of wet weather to make the material significantly less burnable, according to the [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/monitoring/dyk/deadfuelmoisture).

The ongoing drought has left much of that fuel very dry. [Data maintained](https://gacc.nifc.gov/rmcc/nfdrs.php) by the National Interagency Fire Center shows moisture levels in 1,000-hour fuels hovering near record lows.  
  
Those circumstances are why many forecasters aren’t counting on recent moisture to end Colorado’s wildfire season. Peter Goble, the assistant state climatologist for the Colorado Climate Center, said any rain helps, but fire fuel could quickly dry out again during hot, dry periods.

“I'm cautiously optimistic that things will start to get better, but just worried that this moisture is going to be fleeting and not make much of a dent in the deficits that we have,” Goble said.

## Hints of longer-lasting fires

It’s clear that wildfires are growing [larger](https://dfpc.colorado.gov/sections/wildfire-information-center/historical-wildfire-information), [faster](https://www.colorado.edu/today/2026/07/23/wildfires-are-moving-faster-heres-what-means-colorado) and [more destructive](https://dfpc.colorado.gov/sections/wildfire-information-center/historical-wildfire-information) in Colorado, but are they also lasting longer?

Coop investigated that question for CPR News by analyzing fire-tracking satellite data. He found that between 2012 and 2023, the lifespan of an average fire burning in the western U.S. and Canadian mountains increased by more than half a day each year. In California and the Pacific Northwest, the increase was closer to a day and a half.  
  
[A 2025 study](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2024GL112539) took a slightly different approach by tracking when fires were first reported and then fully contained, using data from 1992 to 2020. In the western U.S., it found an increase of nearly a full day for summer fires, and about half a day for fires burning in the fall.

“That might not sound like much,” Coop said. “But over decades that adds up to fires that are burning for quite a bit longer.”

Recent blazes confirmed that Colorado wildfires can burn late into the year. In 2020, the [Cameron Peak fire](https://www.cpr.org/tag/cameron-peak-fire/page/2/) only briefly slowed its spread following a September snowstorm. Once conditions dried out, it continued to gain ground until firefighters fully contained the flames in early December.
