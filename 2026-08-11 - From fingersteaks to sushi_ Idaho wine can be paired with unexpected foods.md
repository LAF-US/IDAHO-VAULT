It’s often thought that wine goes best with elevated food, like high-end charcuterie boards and fancy cheeses or premium steaks and fish.

Wine is balanced well by all of those food items, but Idaho wine pairings are less about the specific foods and more about balancing the key food ingredients with the wine’s components.

BoiseDev Eats spoke with Claire Coppi, a sommelier who holds a Level 2 certification from the Court of Master Sommeliers and a Level 3 from the Wine & Spirit Education Trust, about the science of wine pairings and how anyone can use these approaches to pair wine with their own meals, whether it’s a burger and fries or Thai food.

“I think it is a huge misconception that for some reason wine pairings should only be with high-end wine and high-end food. I think inherently in the name wine pairing, it’s just suggesting you’re pairing a wine with food,” Coppi said.

**Strategies for pairing wine with food**

Coppi said the first step in wine pairings is identifying the base components of the food.

![Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/6f251bf1-00e6-48d7-8365-570820fe299b.gif)

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

“When I work in restaurants and wine bars, I’m looking at what the guest has ordered… it’s looking at structural components of the food, so we’re talking fat, acidity, sugar,” Coppi said. “Is there any spice component to it? Are there any green components?”

Next, there are two strategies you can use: identifying a wine that either matches the food’s structure or complements it.

“I’m going to want a wine that either matches it or complements it. If I have a super high acid dish, I’m going to want a super high acid wine. Like tomatoes in a dish, I want something that’s going to be able to stand up to that,” she said. “Same thing with fat. If I have a super fatty dish, I don’t want a super delicate wine. I need something that’s going to be able to stand up to that fat and cut through it.”

![Corwin Ford Nampa - July 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/02b5ccc2-e925-42c6-9e39-de8bd0223814.jpg)

Corwin Ford Nampa - July 2026 - 2:1

Key wine components that blend with specific ingredients include:

- Wines with high acidity can cut through dishes with a lot of cream or fat
- Wines with sweetness can complement the heat of spicy foods
- Wines with high tannins, like cabernets, will have the structure to cut through high-protein, fatty foods like beef

**Sparkling wine — the jack of all trades**

Coppi said that sparkling wine can be paired with almost any type of food.

![Corwin Ford Nampa - July 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/02b5ccc2-e925-42c6-9e39-de8bd0223814.jpg)

Corwin Ford Nampa - July 2026 - 2:1

“Sparkling wine is like the jack of all trades as far as wine genres go,” Coppi said. “There are so many different grapes you can turn into sparkling wines with so many different sweetness levels, but they all tend to be really high acid, moderate alcohol, which means they’re super food friendly with anything.”

Coppi said that because of the high acid, sparkling wine partners well with high-protein and high-fat foods, including finger steaks, potato chips, French fries, mac and cheese, or fried chicken.

“Talking about fat with fried food, sparkling wine is so good with like any fried food. Like Idaho finger steaks, get the freaking sparkling wine to drink with that,” she said. “It would be really, really good.”

**Cheeseburger with a Syrah**

For a classic cheeseburger, Coppi also recommends a light red, like a Syrah.

![A1 Heating - 2:1 - Beat the Heat](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/89752a40-7ebf-454b-9e1f-898264293d49.jpg)

A1 Heating - 2:1 - Beat the Heat

“Depending on what’s on the burger, Idaho also does a really great job with Rhône varietals like Syrah,” she said. “… Syrah inherently has a fun, kind of smoked-meat, peppery quality to it that would really complement the meatiness of the burger, and that’s another one that Idaho is doing really well with.”

She also said an Idaho rosé would be a great burger pairing.

“Let’s also not forget rosés because rosés are really, really versatile. They’re high acid, they’re fresh, and they have a little more texture than a white wine, because they do have some skin contact. That’s what makes them pink,” Coppi said. “So that’s a super pairable genre, and we have a lot of great rosés coming out of Idaho as well.”

**Thai food and Riesling**

If a spicy Thai curry is on the menu, Coppi said Riesling’s blend of high acid, sweetness, and moderate to low alcohol content makes it the perfect match.

“Sommeliers love pairing Riesling because it’s one of the highest acid grapes out there… With that little bit of residual sugar as well, it eases the heat. Again, with hot foods you want to avoid high alcohol at all costs,” she said. “Riesling tends to be moderate to lower alcohol, especially if you have that sugar in there. So it balances that out really nicely.”

**Sushi and wine**

Coppi said that one food in particular most people don’t realize can pair well with wine is sushi.

“For whatever reason, people think you can only do beer and sake; it’s very limiting,” she said. “Wine with sushi is killer because you have all these different kinds of fish and all these different formats they’re served, whether it’s just simple soy sauce and wasabi, or you can start getting into really cool things like yuzu and all this citrus.”

![A1 Heating - 2:1 - Beat the Heat](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/89752a40-7ebf-454b-9e1f-898264293d49.jpg)

Coppi recommends a Riesling as a wine that goes well with sushi, and then a Pinot Noir or Grenache if you are focused on tuna. She also said that sparkling wine will do well with any type of sushi.
