# Waiting for another run

## Group has big plans to restore the Bovill Opera House to its former glory

ELIZABETH RUDD of the Tribune

![The projectionist’s seat sits idle and as it was when the Bovill Opera House closed.](https://public-assets-prod.pubgen.ai/brand_4fc3eb37-4c8a-4951-9324-365f6e4b53fd/asset_cab51148-9113-5ffa-b94f-f15d153894ea.jpg?w=3840)

BOVILL - To the unfamiliar eye, the structure that sits on the corner of Pine Street and Second Avenue is just an old, wooden building.

But to a select few residents, the Bovill Opera House is both the past and future of this small north central Idaho town.

The Bovill Opera House was once the center of activity in town, and has survived a fire that torched all of downtown, the Great Depression and a dwindling population. So when the Bovill Improvement Group was approached about restoring the theater, it was seen as an opportunity.

In August, Greg Hall, whose father, Lloyd, owned the opera house at the time, contacted the group and offered to donate the building to them to restore. The Bovill Improvement Group consists of about 10 residents who work to improve the town with various renovation and remodel projects.

Chris Childers, president of the group, said after the idea was brought to his attention, he and Mayor Brad Dorendorf were talking about the opera house's possibilities. The mayor told him it is the only building in town that - if they fix it up - will bring people into Bovill.

"Bovill itself in the last few years has declined, declined and declined to one bar, a post office and our city hall is open a day and a half a week, so it would be a reason for people to come to town," Childers said. "The bar's not a reason, the post office isn't a reason and city hall isn't a reason."

Dust covers the inside of the opera house, coating its original wood floors, rows of seats, a stage still hung with curtains and various other trinkets and furniture. Sunlight sneaks in through cracks in the brick and wooden walls, broken plaster from the ceiling covers the floor and paintings from an unknown time period run along the length of the walls.

"We want to restore it to the original," said Kellie Funke, vice president for the group.

The opera house was officially signed over to the group in February and they began work in early June, said Sheila Loomis, a member of the group. They have since contacted the Latah County Historical Society and are in the early stages of working with the organization to apply for grants to fund the renovation project.

The improvement group will also hold its first fundraiser for the project selling ice cream with huckleberry sauce and opera house post cards Saturday during Bovill Old Timers Days.

So far, the group has spent about 20 hours just removing random items that Lloyd Hall had stored in the opera house and sweeping some areas of the floor. The project is really just starting, said Diane Holt, another member of the group. Loomis said they have also dug several items out from under the stage and there's still more.

"There's been a lot of hours put into it," Holt said.

And there will be a lot more.

"It's going to (cost) quite a bit and it's going to take a long time," Funke said.

As of now, she said they are waiting for direction from the historical society, working to get insurance and grant money to begin renovations. Childers said they are not sure exactly what will need to be done to the building until they are able to have an architect look at it.

"We will be working with the historical society, and when you work with them they want it pretty original and we have to (do that) to get the grants and volunteers," Funke said.

Even so, the group - made up of all women with the exception of Childers - wants to keep the building's character intact, like the signatures and commentary written on the walls in the projection room. Kenny White, Loomis' brother, worked as a projection operator for several years and signed the ceiling of the room at least twice. Loomis said he talks about how he could still run the film projector to this day and would do it for them if he wasn't living in California.

"This was big time back in the day," she said.

Lewiston resident Bob Denevan, who is in his 90s, confirmed that the opera house was the place to be in Bovill when he was younger.

"I used to sit down in the front row when I was 4 and watch silent shows and they had a piano player and violin player," he said.

Denevan grew up in Bovill and lived there for 35 years. His grandfather, Lee, purchased the opera house when he moved with his wife to Idaho from South Dakota and owned it until the early 1930s. Denevan said his family showed silent films and hosted dances every Saturday on the upstairs dance floor.

"In the early days it was the center of activity in Bovill," he said.

Traveling shows would also makes stops in Bovill to perform at the opera house, Denevan said, and eventually his grandfather showed the first talking movies that came with a soundtrack on a regular record.

"You'd have to synchronize the record with the movie so it would be right," he said.

In 1932, there was a fire in the projection booth that cost his grandfather five film rolls and caused him to go broke. The Depression also made it difficult for him to keep an entertainment business running. Lee Denevan's last effort to create a profit from the opera house was to put a miniature golf course on top of the dance floor and charge 10 cents per game.

"And he didn't make any money on that either," Bob Denevan said.

So his grandfather walked away from the opera house, and later Denevan's mother reinstated Saturday night dances before the family sold it for good, he said.

The opera house is more than a century old, but the improvement group doesn't know an exact date of construction and much of the history is lost to them. Funke said they are hoping more people will contact them with information, memorabilia and stories to help with the restoration process. The group can be contacted through its Facebook page, Bovill Improvement Group aka BIG.

"We really want to hear the history of it," Funke said.

The group is also excited for what the opera house can be used for in the future.

"We're hoping to put this back in use," Funke said.

Loomis said they have heard the University of Idaho and Washington State University are interested in hosting performances in the opera house once it's restored. Funke added they would like to get the Bovill schools to hold plays and concerts in it too, and even start showing movies again.

"There's a lot of things," Funke said. "It's endless, really," Loomis added.
