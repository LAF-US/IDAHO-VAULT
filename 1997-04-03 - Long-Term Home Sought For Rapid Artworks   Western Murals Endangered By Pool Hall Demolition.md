Bob Templin knew Joe Breckenridge as a fast-working, good-hearted, hard-drinking artist.

Breckenridge probably got paid with Scotch for the three murals he painted inside Don’s Pool hall, the businessman said Wednesday.

“I’ll bet that whole thing didn’t take him three or four hours.”

That’s darned close. Rex Symons, who was there in 1961 to see Breckenridge create one 30-foot mural, said it took the artist three hours and earned him $30. The two seven-foot paintings took 23 and 25 minutes.

Symons was the last owner of Don’s. Templin is the resort owner who’s tearing down the sawmill workers’ hangout. He wonders if it’s possible to move the Western landscapes to a public building where they can be preserved.

“Or maybe nobody cares,” said Templin.

To which Elaine Carver, executive director of the Post Falls Arts Council, responded: “Maybe nobody knows.”

Carver looked up at the murals for the first time Wednesday afternoon. They’re just below the ceiling, and 38 inches deep. Breckenridge called them “The Four Seasons.”

Time and smoke have dulled the blues, greens and browns. But in the light of day, the landscapes invite close inspection.

There’s a bear on this wall, a moose on that one. The only person portrayed is a man fishing from a canoe.

Symons thinks the landscape looks like the Montana country where Breckenridge grew up.

“He knew (artist) Charlie Russell. Their farms weren’t far apart,” Symons said.

Breckenridge, who died in 1972, served in the Marines in World War I. He later made his living going to fairs and festivals, painting on pieces of wood. In a competition at Yale University, he was proclaimed the world’s fastest freehand artist.

He normally used four-inch brushes. He used a six-inch brush, Symons said, to paint the sky in the pool hall mural.

The two seven-foot paintings are in the front of the room, above the plate-glass windows. They’re painted on plywood, and wouldn’t be tough to move.

But the 30-foot section on the north wall was painted on lathe and plaster. Symons doubts it can be saved.

Templin holds out hope.

“We’d have to cut the wall out, get hold of the studding in there. We’d have to cut it in pieces.”

There are companies that specialize in moving murals, said Laura Thayer, curator of collections at Spokane’s Cheney Cowles Museum.

“Depending on how much money people want to spend, almost anything is possible,” she said.

Carver will discuss the mural’s fate with the arts council board of directors. As for a new home, she pointed out that City Hall is expanding, and an old church is being renovated. The historical society is looking to build, Templin said.

One thing’s for sure: The pool hall will soon be gone.

It’s one of five decrepit, turn-of-the-century buildings on Spokane Street that are coming down. Templin hopes to put shops and offices in their place.

Some of the few remaining artifacts, such as signs and bottles, will be offered for sale to the public from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Friday and Saturday at the site. Also for sale will be concrete construction blocks made at the plant founded by Frederick Post, for whom Post Falls is named.

As for the mural, Templin said, “perhaps there will be somebody in the community who has some ideas.” He can be reached at 773-1537.
