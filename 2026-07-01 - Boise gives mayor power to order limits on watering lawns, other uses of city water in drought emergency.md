The Boise City Council took action Tuesday to allow for the regulation of drinking water during drought conditions.

The council unanimously passed an ordinance that would allow the city’s mayor to issue an advisory or mandatory emergency drought response order amid drought conditions, which would in turn provide the power to issue orders to reduce or cease the use of potable water. [According to the ordinance](https://boisecityid.iqm2.com/Citizens/Detail_LegiFile.aspx?ID=25976&highlightTerms=ord-28-26), the mayor could issue the order and the council could ratify or rescind it. The ordinance applies to those connected to public drinking water systems in Boise; public drinking water providers are Veolia and Capital Water. Gardens and pastures irrigated with drinking water to grow food or other commodities would be exempt from any orders. Orders would also not apply to water provided by irrigation districts or private wells.

Specifically, orders could regulate the use of potable water/drinking water for irrigating lawns, gardens, pastures, parks, greenbelts, golf courses, and open space. Orders could also call for reducing or stopping the use of potable/drinking water for non-essential or discretionary uses, including irrigation, commercial, and recreational or aesthetic purposes. The ordinance says recreation and aesthetic uses of drinking water are privately-owned swimming pools and hot tubs, water fountains or features that don’t support aquatic life, and the use of hoses or sprinklers for leisure or optional activities, including noncommercial car washing or hosing down sidewalks.

Simply put, the ordinance would give the mayor the ability to order residents and businesses who use city water to stop watering their lawns, hosing down their sidewalks, or filling their swimming pools, among other restrictions.

Steve Hubble, a senior manager with the city’s public works department, told the council at a Tuesday afternoon workshop that current conditions do not warrant the need for any kind of emergency drought response from the city. However, he said staff wanted to prioritize putting the ordinance in place before the council’s summer recess so that an order could be issued if needed.

The city moved the ordinance through on a single vote, using a provision in Idaho code that allows it to bypass the typical process requiring three readings on three separate dates.

Ahead of the council’s vote at its Tuesday evening meeting, Council Member Jimmy Hallyburton reemphasized that “(there’s) no emergency related to drought to be concerned about today, but (it’s) something that we need to make sure that we’re always thinking about steps to make sure that we’re using water wisely, and that we have something in place if an emergency were to occur.”

## A ‘phased approach’

Hubble emphasized that the ordinance specifies a “phased approach” to water-reduction measures, ranging from advisory to mandatory.

An advisory order would be educational in nature and could provide guidelines and recommendations for conserving or lessening potable water use, such as fixing leaks and reducing consumption.

![Watkins Carter - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/f002ca13-0527-4831-9254-99ce5fa33461.jpg)

Watkins Carter - 2:1

For a voluntary reduction order, the mayor could specify measures to reduce potable water consumption. The ordinance says such an order “shall include recommended days and watering times for irrigation purposes.”

Under a mandatory reduction order, the mayor could require residents to reduce potable water use, and would establish mandatory irrigation watering days and times. It could also prohibit the use of potable water for certain recreational and aesthetic purposes, and could limit the days and times water can be used for certain commercial purposes.

Under a mandatory cessation order, the mayor could order the stoppage of certain uses of potable water. It could consist of prohibiting the use of potable water for irrigation purposes, or enacting measures that restrict such uses. Restrictions could be for irrigation purposes that are in certain areas or have certain features, like golf courses. For uses still allowed, it could also establish watering days and times, or mandate water delivery methods, like hand-watering or drip irrigation. For commercial uses, it could specify required activities, such as only serving customers drinking water by request, restricting new landscaping work, or prohibiting the use of water for dust control.

Hubble said that Boise’s water utilities are private and would “be challenged to enact any water conservation measures on their own without support from the city.” Veolia reviewed the draft ordinance language and provided some feedback on it, he said.

## Considering local conditions

The ordinance’s language is rooted in the idea that mayors can take immediate action to preserve public health in the face of emergencies.

“Severe or prolonged drought is a public health issue and may cause significant impacts to the quantity and quality of water for human consumption, increase the incidence of illness and disease, cause adverse mental health outcomes, and overall increase mortality rates,” the ordinance text says. It notes that the State of Idaho recognizes cities’ authority to restrict residents’ water use during emergencies.

![Watkins Carter - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/f002ca13-0527-4831-9254-99ce5fa33461.jpg)

Watkins Carter - 2:1

Hubble said that the cities of Burley and Rupert have created water conservation ordinances, while the City of Caldwell has called for a voluntary reduction in irrigation. He said Boise’s ordinance is similar to those, and is intended to be proactive.

The ordinance language says that drought severity factors in Ada County shall be considered when deciding whether to issue an order. The ordinance lists worsening climate conditions, consecutive years of drought in Ada County, above-normal temperatures, and below-normal precipitation as such factors.

It also says indications of a deteriorating water supply shall be considered, including an early drop in Boise River flows and early curtailment or shutoffs for irrigation water deliveries compared to the normal irrigation season.

The ordinance specifies that in addition to considering the above factors, the mayor shold only issue orders commensurate with the present category of drought for Ada County, as indicated by the U.S. Drought Monitor’s Drought Index: For example, in a moderate drought, the mayor can issue an advisory or voluntary reduction order. Hubble said that an advisory order might be focused on community education and outreach around changing drought conditions. In an exceptional drought, the mayor could mandate the cessation of drinking water use for irrigation and other non-essential uses outlined in the ordinance.

As of Wednesday, about 31% of Ada County, including parts of Boise’s north, west, and southwest areas, qualified as “Abnormally Dry.” Abnormally Dry is not technically a drought classification. About 67% of the county is in the “Moderate Drought” category, which is just above Abnormally Dry, and is the lowest drought category on the scale. Just a fraction of the county is in Severe Drought; none of the county is in Extreme Drought or Exceptional Drought.

## ‘Hopefully it’s not something that we need’

During the workshop, council members generally expressed support for the ordinance.

![Watkins Carter - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/f002ca13-0527-4831-9254-99ce5fa33461.jpg)

Watkins Carter - 2:1

“I grew up in a drought environment in my early elementary school years,” Council President Meredith Stead said, “and we used to have a bucket in the shower to catch extra water to then water the plants. I think that there’s a lot of little things that a lot of us can be doing to be more mindful, so I appreciate the opportunity to have a community conversation around it, and hopefully it’s not something that we need to enact, but (I’ll be) glad it’s in place in case we do.”

Council Member Colin Nash asked how residential water use in Boise compares to that of other types of water use. Hubble said agriculture is the most significant user of local water resources.

Council Member Jordan Morales asked whether the city has talked with neighboring municipalities on creating similar ordinances. He also asked if there is a need to work with the state to ensure that homeowners associations couldn’t fine residents for grass drying out as a result of the curtailed use of drinking water for irrigation.
