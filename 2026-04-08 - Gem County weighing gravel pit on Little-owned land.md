## Gem Co. weighs nearly 400-acre gravel pit on Little family-owned land

![](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/gem-county-weighing-nearly-400-acre-gravel-pit-on-little-family-owned-land-IMG-3868-2-Large.jpeg)

The site of a nearly 400-acre property owned by Governor Brad Little's family business that could be the site of a gravel pit southwest of Emmett. Photo: Margaret Carmel/BoiseDev

It will be another month and a half before Gem County decides the fate of an application for a new gravel pit southwest of Emmett owned by a well-known local family.

Since last year, an application from Boise-based Granite Excavation to turn a 393-acre property near 2595 Sales Yard Road into a gravel pit has divided Emmett. The project, which would turn a longtime industrial egg production operation into a gravel extraction site for roughly 13 years, is on land owned by Governor Brad Little’s family business. It was initially recommended for approval of a special use permit in August and appealed to the Gem County Board of Commissioners in late December, who pushed the hearing back over concerns about late submittals of information that neither side was able to review.

Commissioners took up the application again on April 6 and 7th, hearing back-to-back nights of three hours of testimony each in front of a packed room. Testimony from Granite Excavation, their employees, and supporters of the business argued Emmett’s economy needs Granite to find a new site to keep supporting local jobs and keep gravel affordable for local development projects. They also argued that the noise and health impacts from airborne silica dust from rock crushing are over-exaggerated by neighbors and not supported by evidence.

And on the other hand, the majority of those testifying urged the commissioners to deny the project to preserve the quiet way of life for those living near the site in unincorporated Gem County. They said Granite’s operation would add to the impact of nearby gravel pits already operating in the county, impact their air quality, cut home values, and draw down their wells due to the pumps dewatering the excavation area.

Commissioner Earl DeFur recused himself from the vote on Monday night because he had previously testified against the project before being appointed to the board in January. This leaves the decision with Commission Chairman Kirk Wille and Commissioner Mark Rekow. After closing the public hearing, Wille asked staff to gather more information on what would be required to complete the improvements to Star Lane proposed by Granite Excavation and to determine whether the Idaho Department of Water Resources could add wells around the gravel pit to its monitoring schedule.

This pushed the deliberation and vote on the project back to May 26 at 6 p.m.

## Economic engine? Or health hazard?

Whether gravel pits are a boost to Gem County or a hindrance depends on who you ask.

During this week’s public hearings, Granite Excavation and the project’s proponents argued this property is considered “prime mining lands” and the right to extract minerals on the site is protected by Idaho land use code. The company said it would use only 170 acres of the property for gravel extraction and would dig in a 20-acre area at a time, leaving the rock crusher hundreds of feet from neighboring homes. Josh Leonard, an attorney representing the company, said the company hired experts to show gravel pit operations, especially when surrounded by a 20-foot berm, and that there is no evidence that silica dust from gravel mining is building up around the county or causing health impacts.

He said the company is also proposing to add a turn lane and rebuild Star Lane at its expense to mitigate damage caused by gravel trucks traveling through the area. Leonard accused the Southwest Gem Community Association, the group of residents fighting the project, of aiming to shut down gravel pits across Gem County, which he said would damage the local economy.

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

“They don’t want you to consider the facts,” Leonard said. “They don’t want fairness.”

The project is on a 393-acre property owned by Little Enterprises LLLP, which bought the property with the plans to turn it into a gravel pit and then later develop it into a subdivision with homes and ponds after the gravel is removed. Both Adam and David Little, sons of Governor Brad Little, testified in support of the project on Monday. They said they knew gravel could be mined on the site after a study was done during the Great Recession and that the Little’s were open about their plans, including participation in Gem County’s public process to develop a zoning ordinance governing where gravel pits could be developed in 2011 and 2012.

“We’ve spent a lot of time and effort and improvements to try to make farming economical out there, and it can’t be done,” Adam Little said. “It’s not economical to farm it, and irrespective of what happens here with this approval, the property won’t be going back into production.”

Neighbors in opposition were not convinced. 117 letters of opposition were filed with the commissioners against the project, and during the testimony period, residents pleaded with the commissioners to block it due to its close proximity to nearby homes. Landon Brown, an attorney representing the Southwest Gem Community Association, said adding this gravel pit will add a third to this small area, which will compound the health impacts of gravel mining and rock crushing that residents of the area are already experiencing, like chronic breathing problems, skin conditions, dry eyes, and other problems.

“This will create a nonstop gravel pit corridor in an agricultural zone,” he said.

Residents said they weren’t informed by the county or realtors when buying property in the area that a project like this could be built on the next property over from their homes, which they said would limit their children’s ability to play outside, disturb them with constant noise of excavation, and impact the safety of children waiting for the bus on a busy road full of gravel trucks.

“I have nothing against corporate growth or someone being able to operate a business, but not at the cost of the community,” nearby resident David Moody testified, likening the gravel pits near Emmett to craters you’d see in a “war zone.”
