## A rural doctor’s perspective on protecting Idaho’s physician pipeline

When doctors who would like to be in Idaho decide to train elsewhere, they are far less likely to return home, writes guest columnist Dr. Frank Batcha.

As I prepare to retire after nearly three decades practicing medicine in the Wood River Valley, including almost 10 years as assistant clinical dean for the [University of Washington School of Medicine’s Idaho WWAMI program](https://www.uwmedicine.org/school-of-medicine/md-program/wwami/idaho), I’ve been reflecting on what makes rural health care work, and what’s at risk if we fail to protect it.

I never set out to come to Idaho. I grew up in a small town in eastern Ohio and always knew I wanted to be a doctor. During medical school, I applied — almost on a whim — to a family medicine residency in Idaho that included wilderness medicine. I came out for an interview, tossed in a ski trip to Sun Valley, and my life changed for the better.

I fell in love with the Wood River Valley, so I shifted my career path, moved west, and landed in Hailey. Nearly 30 years later, I’m grateful to have built a life in a community I love, practicing rural medicine and mentoring the next generation of physicians on the privilege of caring for generations of families. Rural doctors are part of a team that builds a tight-knit fabric of community relationships. This fabric also exposes how fragile access to quality health care can be outside of urban centers.

Here in Blaine County, we’re fortunate to have a strong health care system. But we are also a regional hub. Patients routinely travel from more remote communities like Stanley, Mackay, and beyond because services aren’t available closer to home. For many Idahoans, accessing care still means long drives, delayed treatment, or going without.

That reality reflects a larger problem: Idaho ranks dead last in the nation for physicians per capita. Rural communities face the greatest shortages, and hospitals across the state are [under increasing strain](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2026/03/19/the-clock-is-ticking-for-idahos-rural-hospitals-as-legislature-considers-federal-health-funding/), sometimes forced to reduce services or [close departments altogether](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2025/06/13/expensive-and-complicated-most-rural-hospitals-no-longer-deliver-babies/).

One of the most effective solutions we have is WWAMI. I joined the medical school partnership in the assistant clinical dean role in 2017 after working closely with WWAMI medical students for more than 30 years. I knew if I wanted to see more effective rural health care in Idaho, WWAMI doctors could make it happen.

The program has quietly become a cornerstone of Idaho’s physician workforce, and it continues to rely on support from taxpayers, residents, and lawmakers in Boise. About 11% of practicing physicians in Idaho graduated from the University of Washington School of Medicine, and roughly 42% of the state’s family physicians trained within the WWAMI region. Just as importantly, WWAMI students from Idaho are far more likely to return to practice in rural communities like ours.

Through programs like TRUST and WRITE, Idaho WWAMI medical students train in communities like Hailey, working alongside local physicians and building the relationships that often bring them back.

I’ve had the privilege of teaching many of these students over the years. What sets them apart is their commitment to the patients and places they serve.

[Idaho’s physician pipeline](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2026/01/16/idaho-needs-1400-more-medical-professionals-just-to-catch-up-to-national-average/) is not guaranteed. When policies create uncertainty around medical education or make practicing in Idaho less attractive, we risk losing these future physicians to other states. When doctors who would like to be in Idaho decide to train elsewhere, they are far less likely to return home. For a state already facing severe shortages, especially in rural communities, that’s a risk we can’t afford.

Over the course of my career, I’ve seen remarkable advances in medicine, alongside growing challenges for rural health care systems. Through it all, one truth remains: communities need physicians who are trained here, connected here, and committed to staying for the long haul.

As I step into retirement, my hope is that Idaho continues to invest in programs like WWAMI that strengthen the pipeline of physicians who will care for communities like the Wood River Valley and the many rural towns that depend on it.
