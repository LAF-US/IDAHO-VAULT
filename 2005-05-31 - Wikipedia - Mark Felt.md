**William Mark Felt Sr.** (August 17, 1913 – December 18, 2008) was an American law enforcement officer who worked for the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation "Federal Bureau of Investigation") (FBI) from 1942 to 1973 and was the informant known as " [Deep Throat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Throat_\(Watergate\) "Deep Throat (Watergate)") " in the [Watergate scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watergate_scandal "Watergate scandal").

After working in several FBI field offices and ascending through the ranks, Felt became the bureau's [deputy director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Director_of_the_Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation "Deputy Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation") following [J. Edgar Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover "J. Edgar Hoover") 's death in mid-1972. While his only superior was on sick leave, he provided two *[Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Post "Washington Post")* reporters information about the Watergate scandal under the pseudonym "Deep Throat". He resigned in mid-1973 under suspicions that he was the informant. In 1980, he was convicted of having violated the civil rights of people thought to be associated with members of the [Weather Underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_Underground "Weather Underground"), having ordered FBI agents to [break into their homes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_bag_operation "Black bag operation") and search the premises as part of an attempt to prevent bombings. He was ordered to pay a fine, but was pardoned by President [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan "Ronald Reagan") during his appeal.

In 2005, at age 91, Felt revealed to *[Vanity Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_Fair_\(magazine\) "Vanity Fair (magazine)")* magazine that during his tenure as Deputy Director of the FBI he was Deep Throat, who provided *The Washington Post* reporters [Bob Woodward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Woodward "Bob Woodward") and [Carl Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Bernstein "Carl Bernstein") critical information about the Watergate scandal, ultimately leading to the resignation of President [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon "Richard Nixon") in 1974. Woodward, who had long vowed not to reveal Deep Throat's identity while the source was still alive, quickly confirmed Felt's claim. Though Felt's identity as Deep Throat was suspected, including by Nixon himself,[^1] it had generally remained a secret for 30 years. Felt finally acknowledged that he was Deep Throat after being persuaded by his daughter to reveal his identity before his death.[^2]

Felt published two memoirs: *[The FBI Pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_FBI_Pyramid "The FBI Pyramid")* in 1979 (updated in 2006) and *A G-Man's Life*, written with John O'Connor in 2006. In 2012, the FBI released Felt's personnel file covering the period from 1941 to 1978. The agency also released files pertaining to an extortion threat made against Felt in 1956.[^3]

## Early life and career

Born on August 17, 1913, in [Twin Falls, Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Falls,_Idaho "Twin Falls, Idaho"),[^4] [^5] [^6] Felt was the son of Rose R. Dygert and Mark Earl Felt, a carpenter and building contractor.[^7] His paternal grandfather was a [Free Will Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Will_Baptist "Free Will Baptist") minister.[^8] His maternal grandparents were born in Canada and Scotland. Through his maternal grandfather, Felt was collaterally descended from [Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War "American Revolutionary War") general [Nicholas Herkimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Herkimer "Nicholas Herkimer") of [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_\(state\) "New York (state)").[^8]

After graduating from [Twin Falls High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Falls_High_School "Twin Falls High School") in 1931, Felt attended the [University of Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Idaho "University of Idaho") in [Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow,_Idaho "Moscow, Idaho").[^9] He was a member and president of the Gamma Gamma chapter of the [Beta Theta Pi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_Theta_Pi "Beta Theta Pi") [fraternity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraternities_and_sororities "Fraternities and sororities"),[^10] and earned a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") degree in 1935.[^9] [^11]

Felt then went to Washington, D.C., to work in the office of [Democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_\(United_States\) "Democratic Party (United States)") US Senator [James P. Pope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_P._Pope "James P. Pope"). In 1938, Felt married Audrey Robinson of [Gooding, Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gooding,_Idaho "Gooding, Idaho"), whom he had known when they were students at the University of [[Idaho]].[^12] She had come to Washington to work at the [Internal Revenue Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Service "Internal Revenue Service"). Their wedding was officiated by the chaplain of the [United States House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives "United States House of Representatives"), the Rev. [James Shera Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Shera_Montgomery "James Shera Montgomery").[^13]

Felt stayed on with Pope's successor in the Senate, [David Worth Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Worth_Clark "David Worth Clark") (D-Idaho).[^14] He then attended the [George Washington University Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_University_Law_School "George Washington University Law School") at night, earning his [J.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor "Juris Doctor") degree in 1940 and being admitted to the [District of Columbia Bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia_Bar "District of Columbia Bar") in 1941.[^15]

Upon graduation, Felt took a position at the [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission "Federal Trade Commission") but did not enjoy his work. His workload was very light, and he was assigned to investigate whether a [toilet paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toilet_paper "Toilet paper") brand, called "Red Cross", was misleading consumers into thinking it was endorsed by the [American Red Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Red_Cross "American Red Cross"). Felt wrote in his memoir:

> My research, which required days of travel and hundreds of interviews, produced two definite conclusions:
> 
> 1\. Most people *did* use toilet tissue.
> 
> 2\. Most people *did not* appreciate being asked about it.
> 
> That was when I started looking for other employment.[^16]

He applied for a job with the FBI in November 1941 and was accepted. His first day at the bureau was January 26, 1942.

## Early FBI years

FBI Director [J. Edgar Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover "J. Edgar Hoover") often moved Bureau agents around so they would have wide experience in the field. This was typical of other agencies and corporations of the time. Felt observed that Hoover "wanted every agent to get into any field office at any time. Since he \[Hoover\] had never been transferred and did not have a family, he had no idea of the financial and personal hardship involved." [^17]

After completing 16 weeks of training at the [FBI Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Academy "FBI Academy") in [Quantico, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantico,_Virginia "Quantico, Virginia"), and [FBI Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Headquarters "FBI Headquarters") in Washington, Felt was assigned to [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas "Texas"), spending three months each in the field offices in [Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston,_Texas "Houston, Texas") and [San Antonio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio,_Texas "San Antonio, Texas"). He returned to FBI Headquarters, where he was assigned to the Espionage Section of the Domestic Intelligence Division, tracking down spies and saboteurs during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). He worked on the Major Case Desk. His most notable [counterintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterintelligence "Counterintelligence") work was on the "Peasant" case. Helmut Goldschmidt, operating under the codename "Peasant", was a German agent in custody in England. Under Felt's direction, his German masters were led to believe that "Peasant" had made his way to the US, and thus were fed [disinformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_deception "Military deception") on Allied plans.[^18]

The Espionage Section was abolished in May 1945 after [V-E Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-E_Day "V-E Day"). After the war, Felt was assigned to the [Seattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle,_Washington "Seattle, Washington") field office. After two years of general work, he spent another two as a firearms instructor and was promoted from agent to supervisor. Upon passage of the [Atomic Energy Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Act_of_1946 "United States Atomic Energy Act of 1946") and the creation of the [US Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Atomic_Energy_Commission "US Atomic Energy Commission"), the Seattle office became responsible for completing background checks of workers at the [Hanford plutonium plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanford_Site "Hanford Site") near [Richland, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richland,_Washington "Richland, Washington"). Felt oversaw those investigations.[^19] In 1954 Felt returned briefly to Washington as an inspector's aide. Two months later, he was sent to [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans") as Assistant Special Agent-in-Charge of the field office. When he was transferred to [Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles "Los Angeles") fifteen months later, he held the same rank there.[^20]

## Investigates organized crime

In 1956, Felt was transferred to [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City "Salt Lake City") and promoted to Special Agent-in-Charge.[^21] The Salt Lake City office included [Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevada "Nevada") within its purview, and Felt oversaw some of the bureau's earliest investigations into [organized crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_crime "Organized crime"), assessing [the Mob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mafia "American Mafia") 's operations in the [Reno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reno,_Nevada "Reno, Nevada") and [Las Vegas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Vegas_Valley "Las Vegas Valley") [casinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casino "Casino").[^20] (It was Hoover's, and therefore the bureau's, official position at the time that there was no such thing as the Mob.) [^22] In February 1958, Felt was assigned to [Kansas City, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City,_Missouri "Kansas City, Missouri") (which he dubbed "the Siberia of field offices" in his memoir),[^20] where he directed further investigations of organized crime. By this time, Hoover had come to believe in organized crime, in the wake of the infamous [conclave of underworld bosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apalachin_meeting "Apalachin meeting") in November 1957 in [Apalachin, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apalachin,_New_York "Apalachin, New York").[^23]

## Middle career

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b0/Hoover-JEdgar-LOC.jpg/250px-Hoover-JEdgar-LOC.jpg)

J. Edgar Hoover, director of the FBI, photographed in 1961. Hoover appointed Felt the third-ranking official in the bureau in 1971.

Felt returned to Washington, D.C., in September 1962. As assistant to the bureau's assistant director in charge of the Training Division, Felt helped oversee the FBI Academy.[^24] In November 1964, he was promoted to an assistant director of the bureau, as Chief Inspector of the bureau and Head of the Inspection Division.[^25] This division oversaw compliance with Bureau regulations and conducted internal investigations.

On July 1, 1971, Felt was promoted by Hoover to Deputy Associate Director, assisting Deputy Director [Clyde Tolson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Tolson "Clyde Tolson").[^26] Hoover's right-hand man for decades, Tolson was in failing health and unable to fulfill his duties. Richard Gid Powers wrote that Hoover installed Felt to rein in [William C. Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Sullivan "William C. Sullivan") 's domestic spying operations, as Sullivan had been engaged in secret unofficial work for the [White House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House "White House").[^27] In his memoir, Felt quoted Hoover as having said, "I need someone who can control Sullivan. I think you know he has been getting out of hand." [^28] In his book, *The Bureau*, Ronald Kessler said that Felt "managed to please Hoover by being tactful with him and tough on agents".[^29] [Curt Gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curt_Gentry "Curt Gentry") described Felt as "the director's latest fair-haired boy", who had "no inherent power" in his new post, the real number three being [John P. Mohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_P._Mohr "John P. Mohr").[^30]

### Weather Underground investigations

Among the criminal organizations that the FBI investigated in the early 1970s was the [Weather Underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weatherman_\(organization\) "Weatherman (organization)"). Their case was dismissed by the court because it concluded that the FBI had conducted illegal activities, including unauthorized [wiretaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiretap "Wiretap"), break-ins, and mail interceptions. The lead federal prosecutor on the case, William C. Ibershof, claims that Felt and Attorney General [John Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_N._Mitchell "John N. Mitchell") initiated these illegal activities that tainted the investigation.[^31]

## After Hoover's death

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8d/Patrickgrey.jpeg)

L. Patrick Gray, acting director of the FBI from May 1972 to April 1973

Hoover died in his sleep and was found on the morning of May 2, 1972. Tolson was nominally in charge until the next day, when Nixon appointed [L. Patrick Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._Patrick_Gray "L. Patrick Gray") as Acting FBI Director. Tolson submitted his resignation, which Gray accepted. Felt succeeded to Tolson's post as Deputy Director, the number-two job in the bureau.[^32] Felt served as an honorary pallbearer at Hoover's funeral.[^33]

On the day of Hoover's death, Hoover's secretary for five decades, [Helen Gandy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Gandy "Helen Gandy"), began destroying his files. She turned over twelve boxes of the "Official/Confidential" files to Felt on May 4, 1972. These contained 167 files and 17,750 pages, many of them containing derogatory information about individuals whom Hoover had investigated. He used this information as power over them. Felt stored the files in his office.

The existence of such files had long been rumored. Gray told the press that afternoon that "there are no dossiers or secret files. There are just general files and I took steps to preserve their integrity." [^34] Felt earlier that day had told Gray, "Mr. Gray, the Bureau doesn't have any secret files", and later accompanied Gray to Hoover's office. They found Gandy boxing up papers. Felt said Gray "looked casually at an open file drawer and approved her work", though Gray would later deny he looked at anything. Gandy retained Hoover's "Personal File" and destroyed it.[^34]

When Felt was called to testify in 1975 by the House of Representatives about the destruction of Hoover's papers, he said, "There's no serious problems if we lose some papers. I don't see anything wrong and I still don't." [^35] At the same hearing, Gandy claimed that she had destroyed Hoover's personal files only after receiving Gray's approval. In a letter submitted to the committee in rebuttal of Gandy's testimony, Gray vehemently denied ever giving such permission. Both Gandy's testimony and Gray's letter were included in the committee's final report.[^35]

In his memoir, Felt expressed ambivalence about Gray. Gray, as acting director, had no background in the agency, but he had been a submarine captain in the Navy, an Assistant Attorney General in the Justice Department, and was nominated to be Deputy Attorney General, the second highest-ranking position, at the time of Hoover's death. While noting that Gray did work hard, Felt was critical of how often Gray was away from FBI headquarters. Gray lived in [Stonington, Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonington,_Connecticut "Stonington, Connecticut"), and commuted to Washington. He also visited all of the bureau's field offices except Honolulu; a permanent Resident Agency was established in Guam in 1975, becoming the FBI's westernmost investigative office. His frequent absences led to the nickname "Three-Day Gray".[^36] These absences, combined with Gray's hospitalization and recuperation from November 20, 1972, to January 2, 1973,[^37] meant that Felt was effectively in charge for much of his final year at the bureau. Bob Woodward wrote "Gray got to be director of the FBI and Felt did the work." [^38] Felt wrote in his memoir:

> The record amply demonstrates that President Nixon made Pat Gray the Acting Director of the FBI because he wanted a politician in J. Edgar Hoover's position who would convert the Bureau into an adjunct of the White House machine.[^39]

Gray's defenders would later argue that Gray had practiced a management system that differed from Hoover's. Gray's program of field office visits was something that Hoover had not done since his early years as director; some believed that Gray's visits helped raise the morale of the field agents. Gray's form of leadership seemed to continue what he had learned as a captain in the [US Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Navy "US Navy"), in which the executive officer concentrates on the basic operation of the ship while the captain concentrates on its position and heading. Felt believed Gray's methods were an unnecessary distraction from the work of the FBI and showed a lack of leadership. He asserted that he was not the only career manager at the FBI who disapproved of Gray's methods, all of whom had served only under Hoover.[^39]

## Watergate

As deputy director, Felt saw everything compiled on [Watergate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watergate "Watergate") before it was given to Gray. The Agent in Charge, [Charles Nuzum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Nuzum "Charles Nuzum"), sent his findings to Investigative Division Head Robert Gebhardt, who passed the information on to Felt. From the day of the burglary, June 17, 1972, until the FBI investigation was mostly completed in June 1973, Felt was the key control point for FBI information. He had been among the first to learn of the investigation, being informed the morning of June 17.[^40] [Ronald Kessler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Kessler "Ronald Kessler"), who spoke to former bureau agents, reported that throughout the investigation they "were amazed to see material in Woodward and Bernstein's stories lifted almost verbatim from their reports of interviews a few days or weeks earlier".[^41]

### "Deep Throat" informant

[Bob Woodward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Woodward "Bob Woodward") first describes his source, nicknamed "Deep Throat", in *[All the President's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_the_President's_Men "All the President's Men")*, as a "source in the Executive Branch who had access to information at CRP (the [Committee to Re-elect the President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Re-elect_the_President "Committee to Re-elect the President"), Nixon's 1972 campaign organization), as well as at the White House." [^42] In the book, Deep Throat is described as an "incorrigible gossip" who was "in a unique position to observe the Executive Branch", a man "whose fight had been worn out in too many battles".[^43] Woodward had known the source before Watergate and had discussed politics and government with him.

In 2005, Woodward wrote that he first met Felt at the White House in 1969 or 1970. Woodward was working as an aide to Admiral [Thomas Hinman Moorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hinman_Moorer "Thomas Hinman Moorer"), [Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Joint_Chiefs_of_Staff "Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff"), and was delivering papers to the White House Situation Room. In his book *The Secret Man*, Woodward described Felt as a "tall man with perfectly combed gray hair... distinguished looking" with a "studied air of confidence, even what might be called a command presence".[^44] They stayed in contact and spoke on the telephone several times. When Woodward started working at the *Washington Post*, he phoned Felt on several occasions to ask for information for articles in the paper. Felt's information, taken on a promise that Woodward would never reveal its origin, was a source for a few stories, notably for an article on May 18, 1972, about [Arthur Bremer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Bremer "Arthur Bremer"), who shot [George Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_C._Wallace "George C. Wallace").

When the Watergate story broke, Woodward called on Felt. Felt told Woodward on June 19 that [E. Howard Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Howard_Hunt "E. Howard Hunt"), who had connections to Nixon, was involved: the telephone number of his White House office had been listed in the address book of one of the burglars. Initially, Woodward's source was known at the *Post* as "My Friend". *Post* editor [Howard Simons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Simons "Howard Simons") tagged him as "Deep Throat", after the widely known pornographic film [*Deep Throat*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Throat_\(film\) "Deep Throat (film)").[^45] According to Woodward, Simons thought of the term because Felt had been providing information on a [deep background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_background "Deep background") basis.

When Felt revealed his role in 2005, it was noted that "My Friend" has the same initial letters as "Mark Felt". Woodward's notes from interviewing Felt were marked "M.F.", which Woodward says was "not very good tradecraft".[^46]

### Code for contacting Woodward

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1f/Watergate_complex.jpg/250px-Watergate_complex.jpg)

Felt saw all files on the FBI's investigation of the burglary at the Watergate complex in 1972

Woodward explained that when he wanted to meet Deep Throat, he would move a flowerpot with a red flag on his apartment balcony; he lived at number 617, Webster House, 1718 P Street, Northwest. On occasions when Deep Throat wanted a meeting, he would circle the page number on page twenty of Woodward's copy of *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* (delivered to his building) and draw clock hands to signal the hour.[^47]

[Adrian Havill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Havill "Adrian Havill") questioned these claims in his 1993 biography of Woodward and Bernstein. He said Woodward's balcony faced an interior courtyard and was not visible from the street. Woodward said that the courtyard had been bricked in since he had lived there. Havill also said *The Times* was not delivered in copies marked by apartment, but Woodward and a former neighbor disputed this claim.[^48]

Woodward said:

> How \[Felt\] could have made a daily observation of my balcony is still a mystery to me. At the time, the back of my building was not enclosed so anyone could have driven in the back alley to observe my balcony. In addition, my balcony and the back of the apartment complex faced onto a courtyard or back area that was shared with a number of other apartment or office buildings in the area. My balcony could have been seen from dozens of apartments or offices. There were several embassies in the area. The Iraqi embassy was down the street, and I thought it possible that the FBI had surveillance or listening posts nearby. Could Felt have had the counterintelligence agents regularly report on the status of my flag and flowerpot? That seems unlikely, but not impossible.[^49]

### Haldeman informs Nixon about Felt's leaks

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c7/Nixon-depart.png/250px-Nixon-depart.png)

Felt's leaks to Woodward spurred investigations that led to Nixon's resignation

Days after the burglary, Nixon and [White House chief of staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House_chief_of_staff "White House chief of staff") [H. R. Haldeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._R._Haldeman "H. R. Haldeman") talked about putting pressure on the FBI to slow down the investigation. The District of Columbia police had summoned the FBI because they found the burglars had [wiretapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiretapping "Wiretapping") equipment. Wiretapping is a crime investigated by the FBI. Haldeman told President Nixon on June 23, 1972, that Felt would "want to cooperate because he's ambitious".[^50] These tapes were not declassified and revealed for some time.

Haldeman later initially suspected lower-level FBI agents, including [Angelo Lano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelo_Lano "Angelo Lano"), of speaking to the *Post*.[^51] But in a taped conversation on October 19, 1972, Haldeman told the president that sources had said that Felt was speaking to the press.

> You can't say anything about this because it will screw up our source and there's a real concern. [Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_N._Mitchell "John N. Mitchell") is the only one who knows about this and he feels strongly that we better not do anything because... if we move on him, he'll go out and unload everything. He knows everything that's to be known in the FBI. He has access to absolutely everything.[^52]

Haldeman also said that he had spoken to White House counsel [John W. Dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Dean "John W. Dean") about punishing Felt, but Dean said Felt had committed no crime and could not be prosecuted.

When Acting FBI Director Gray returned from his sick leave in January 1973, he confronted Felt about being the source for Woodward and Bernstein. Gray said he had defended Felt to [Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Attorney_General "United States Attorney General") [Richard G. Kleindienst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_G._Kleindienst "Richard G. Kleindienst"): "You know, Mark, Dick Kleindienst told me I ought to get rid of you. He says White House staff members are concerned that you are the FBI source of leaks to Woodward and Bernstein".[^37] Felt replied, "Pat, I haven't leaked anything to anybody." [^37] Gray told Felt:

> I told Kleindienst that you've worked with me in a very competent manner and I'm convinced that you are completely loyal. I told him I was not going to move you out. Kleindienst told me, "Pat, I love you for that." [^37]

### Nixon passes over Felt again

On February 17, 1973, Nixon nominated Gray as Hoover's permanent replacement as director.[^53] Until then, Gray had been in limbo as acting director. In another taped conversation on February 28, Nixon spoke to Dean about Felt's acting as an informant and mentioned that he had never met Felt. Gray was forced to resign on April 27 after it was revealed that he had destroyed a file that had been in the White House safe of E. Howard Hunt.[^54] Gray recommended Felt as his successor.

The day Gray resigned, Kleindienst spoke to Nixon, urging him to appoint Felt as director of the FBI. Nixon instead appointed [William Ruckelshaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ruckelshaus "William Ruckelshaus") as its acting director. Stanley Kutler reported that Nixon said, "I don't want him. I can't have him. I just talked to Bill Ruckelshaus and Bill is a Mr. Clean and I want a fellow in there who is not part of the old guard and who is not part of that infighting in there." [^55] On another White House tape, from May 11, 1973, Nixon and White House Chief of Staff [Alexander Haig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Haig "Alexander Haig") spoke of Felt leaking material to *The New York Times*. Nixon said, "he's a bad guy, you see." He said that William Sullivan had told him of Felt's ambition to be director of the bureau.[^56]

## Clashes with Ruckelshaus and resignation

Felt called his relationship with Ruckelshaus "stormy".[^57] In his memoir, Felt describes Ruckelshaus as a "security guard sent to see that the FBI did nothing which would displease Mr. Nixon".[^58]

In mid-1973, *The New York Times* published a series of articles about the [Nixon wiretaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixon_wiretaps "Nixon wiretaps"), wiretaps that had been ordered by Hoover during his tenure at the FBI. Ruckelshaus believed that the information must have come from someone at the FBI.

In June 1973, Ruckelshaus received a call from someone claiming to be a *New York Times* reporter, telling him that Felt was the source of this information.[^59] On June 21, Ruckelshaus met privately with Felt and accused him of leaking information to *The New York Times*, a charge that Felt adamantly denied.[^51] Ruckelshaus told Felt to "sleep on it" and let him know the next day what he wanted to do. Felt resigned from the bureau the next day, June 22, 1973, ending his 31-year career.

In a 2013 interview, Ruckelshaus noted the possibility that the original caller was a hoax. He said that he considered Felt's resignation "an admission of guilt" anyway.[^59]

Ruckelshaus, who had served only as acting director, was replaced several weeks later by [Clarence M. Kelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_M._Kelley "Clarence M. Kelley"), who had been nominated by Nixon as FBI director and confirmed by the Senate.

## Trial and conviction

In the early 1970s, Felt had supervised Operation [COINTELPRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COINTELPRO "COINTELPRO"), initiated by Hoover in the 1950s. This period of FBI history has generated great controversy for its abuses of private citizens' rights. The FBI was spying on, infiltrating, and disrupting the [Civil Rights Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Movement "Civil Rights Movement"), [Anti-War Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_to_United_States_involvement_in_the_Vietnam_War "Opposition to United States involvement in the Vietnam War"), [Black Panthers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Panthers "Black Panthers"), and other [New Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Left "New Left") movements. By 1972, Felt was heading the investigation into the [Weather Underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weatherman_\(organization\) "Weatherman (organization)"), which had planted bombs at the [Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Capitol "United States Capitol"), [the Pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pentagon "The Pentagon"), and the [State Department building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S_Truman_Building "Harry S Truman Building"). Felt, along with [Edward S. Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_S._Miller "Edward S. Miller"), ordered FBI agents to break into homes secretly in 1972 and 1973, without a [search warrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_warrant "Search warrant"), on nine separate occasions. These kinds of FBI operations were known as " [black bag jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_bag_jobs "Black bag jobs") ". The break-ins occurred at five addresses in [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_\(state\) "New York (state)") and [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey"), at the homes of relatives and acquaintances of Weather Underground members. They did not contribute to the capture of any fugitives. The use of "black bag jobs" by the FBI was declared unconstitutional by the [US Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Supreme_Court "US Supreme Court") in the *[Plamondon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_\(Pun\)_Plamondon "Lawrence (Pun) Plamondon")* case, 407 U.S. 297 (1972).[^60]

Many agents were investigated after the [Citizens' Commission to Investigate the FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens'_Commission_to_Investigate_the_FBI "Citizens' Commission to Investigate the FBI") and the subsequent [Church Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Committee "Church Committee") of Congress revealed the FBI's illegal activities. In 1976, Felt publicly stated he had ordered break-ins and recommended against punishment of individual agents who had followed orders. Felt also stated that Gray had also authorized the break-ins, but Gray denied this. Felt said on the CBS television program *[Face the Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_the_Nation "Face the Nation")* he would probably be a "scapegoat" for the bureau's work.[^61] "I think this is justified and I'd do it again tomorrow," he said on the program. While admitting the break-ins were "extralegal", he justified them as protecting the "greater good". Felt said:

> To not take action against these people and know of a bombing in advance would simply be to stick your fingers in your ears and protect your eardrums when the explosion went off and then start the investigation.

[Griffin Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffin_Bell "Griffin Bell"), the Attorney General in the [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter "Jimmy Carter") administration, directed investigation of these cases. On April 10, 1978, a federal grand jury charged Felt, Miller, and Gray with conspiracy to violate the constitutional rights of US citizens by searching their homes without warrants.

The indictment charged violations of Title 18, Section 241 of the [United States Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Code "United States Code") and stated Felt and the others:

> Did unlawfully, willfully, and knowingly combine, conspire, confederate, and agree together and with each other to injure and oppress citizens of the United States who were relatives and acquaintances of the Weatherman fugitives, in the free exercise and enjoyments of certain rights and privileges secured to them by the Constitution and the laws of the United States of America.[^62]

Felt told his biographer Ronald Kessler: "I was shocked that I was indicted. You would be too if you did what you thought was in the best interests of the country and someone on technical grounds indicted you." [^63]

Felt, Gray, and Miller were arraigned in Washington, D.C., on April 20. Seven hundred current and former FBI agents were outside the courthouse applauding the "Washington Three", as Felt referred to himself and his colleagues in his memoir.[^64] Gray's case did not go to trial and was dropped by the government for lack of evidence on December 11, 1980.[^65] [^66] [^67]

Felt and Miller attempted to plea bargain with the government, willing to agree to a misdemeanor guilty plea to conducting searches without warrants—a violation of [18 U.S.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_18_of_the_United_States_Code "Title 18 of the United States Code") [§ 2236](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2236). The government rejected the offer in 1979. After eight postponements, the case against Felt and Miller went to trial in the [US District Court for the District of Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Columbia "US District Court for the District of Columbia") on September 18, 1980.[^68] On October 29, former President Nixon appeared as a rebuttal witness for the defense.[^69] [^70] He testified that in authorizing the bureau to conduct break-ins to gather foreign intelligence information "he was acting on precedents established by a number of Presidential directives dating to 1939." It was Nixon's first courtroom appearance since before Watergate. He had avoided appearing in any legal proceedings during it and had been pardoned by President Gerald Ford after his resignation. Nixon also contributed money to Felt's defense fund, since Felt's legal expenses were running over $600,000 by then. Also testifying were former Attorneys General Mitchell, Kleindienst, [Herbert Brownell Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Brownell_Jr. "Herbert Brownell Jr."), [Nicholas Katzenbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Katzenbach "Nicholas Katzenbach"), and [Ramsey Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey_Clark "Ramsey Clark"), all of whom said warrantless searches in national security matters were commonplace and understood not to be illegal. Mitchell and Kleindienst denied they had authorized any of the break-ins at issue in the trial. The bureau used a national security justification for the searches because it alleged the Weather Underground was in the employ of [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba "Cuba").[^71]

The jury returned guilty verdicts on November 6, 1980, two days after the [presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_United_States_presidential_election "1980 United States presidential election").[^72] [^73] [^74] The charge carried a maximum sentence of ten years in prison and a $10,000 fine; on December 15, Judge [William B. Bryant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_B._Bryant "William B. Bryant") fined Felt $5,000 and Miller $3,500, but imposed no jail time for either.[^63] [^75] [^76] Writing an op-ed piece in *The New York Times* a week after the conviction, attorney [Roy Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Cohn "Roy Cohn") claimed that Felt and Miller were being used as scapegoats by the [Carter administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carter_administration "Carter administration") and it was an unfair prosecution. Cohn wrote the break-ins were the "final dirty trick" of the Nixon administration, and there had been no "personal motive" to their actions.[^77] *The New York Times* praised the convictions, saying "the case has established that zeal is no excuse for violating the Constitution." [^78]

Felt and Miller appealed their verdicts.[^75] [^76] [^79]

## Pardon

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Official_Portrait_of_President_Reagan_1981.jpg/250px-Official_Portrait_of_President_Reagan_1981.jpg)

President Ronald Reagan pardoned Felt and Miller.

In a phone call on January 30, 1981, [Edwin Meese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Meese "Edwin Meese") encouraged President [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan "Ronald Reagan") to issue a pardon. After further encouragement from Felt's former colleagues, President Reagan pardoned Felt and Miller. The pardon was signed on March 26, but due to the [assassination attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempted_assassination_of_Ronald_Reagan "Attempted assassination of Ronald Reagan") on March 30, was not announced to the public until April 15, 1981.[^80] [^81] [^82]

In the pardon, Reagan wrote:

> During their long careers, Mark Felt and Edward Miller served the Federal Bureau of Investigation and our nation with great distinction. To punish them further—after 3 years of criminal prosecution proceedings—would not serve the ends of justice. Their convictions in the U.S. District Court, on appeal at the time I signed the pardons, grew out of their good-faith belief that their actions were necessary to preserve the security interests of our country. The record demonstrates that they acted not with criminal intent, but in the belief that they had grants of authority reaching to the highest levels of government.[^83]

Nixon sent Felt and Miller bottles of [champagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champagne_\(wine\) "Champagne (wine)") with the note "Justice ultimately prevails." [^84] *The New York Times* disapproved in an editorial, saying that the US "deserved better than a gratuitous revision of the record by the President".[^85] Felt and Miller said they would seek repayment of their legal fees from the government.

The prosecutor at the trial, [John W. Nields Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Nields_Jr. "John W. Nields Jr."), said, "I would warrant that whoever is responsible for the pardons did not read the record of the trial and did not know the facts of the case." Nields also complained that the White House did not consult with the prosecutors in the case, which was contrary to the usual practice when a pardon was under consideration.[^79]

Felt said,

> I feel very excited and just so pleased that I can hardly contain myself. I am most grateful to the President. I don't know how I'm ever going to be able to thank him. It's just like having a heavy burden lifted off your back. This case has been dragging on for five years.

At a press conference the day of the announcement, Miller said, "I certainly owe the Gipper one." [^81] [^86] Carter Attorney General [Griffin Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffin_Bell "Griffin Bell") said he did not object to the pardons, as the convictions had upheld constitutional principles.

Despite their pardons, Felt and Miller won permission from the [United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_District_of_Columbia_Circuit "United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit") to appeal their convictions so as to remove it from their record and to prevent it from being used in civil suits by victims of the break-ins they had ordered.[^87] Ultimately, the court restored Felt's law license in 1982, based on Reagan's pardon. In June 1982, Felt and Miller testified before the [Senate Judiciary Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Committee_on_the_Judiciary "United States Senate Committee on the Judiciary") 's security and terrorism subcommittee. They said that the restrictions placed on the FBI by Attorney General [Edward H. Levi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_H._Levi "Edward H. Levi") were threatening the country's safety.[^88]

## Family

Felt and Audrey had two children: a daughter, Joan, and a son, Mark Jr. Joan graduated from high school in 1961 in Kansas City during his assignment there, then attended the [University of Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kansas "University of Kansas") for two years, before transferring to [Stanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") in California to study drama.[^89] Felt settled in [Alexandria, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria,_Virginia "Alexandria, Virginia"), while she was an undergraduate, for his post at the FBI Academy.[^90] Prior to the Watergate scandal, Felt had become estranged from Joan. They had been close during her childhood, but after she graduated from Stanford, she had gone to Chile under a [Fulbright scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulbright_Program "Fulbright Program") to continue her studies. While there, she became friends with [Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism") revolutionary [Andrés Pascal Allende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9s_Pascal_Allende "Andrés Pascal Allende"), nephew of future president [Salvador Allende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Allende "Salvador Allende"). When she returned home, her political views had shifted to the extreme left, putting her in conflict with her conservative father.[^89]

Joan earned her [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree "Master's degree") in [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language "Spanish language") at Stanford,[^90] and then joined a [hippie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippie "Hippie") community in the [Santa Cruz Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Cruz_Mountains "Santa Cruz Mountains"). Felt and his wife went to visit her once and were appalled at her [counterculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture_of_the_1960s "Counterculture of the 1960s") lifestyle and use of drugs; it reminded him of members of the militant [Weather Underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_Underground "Weather Underground") that the FBI had been prosecuting. Joan's friends were equally shocked that her father was an FBI agent. Following their visit, Joan terminated most contact with her parents. As a result, and combined with the fact that she did not follow the news, she was unaware of her father's legal problems arising from the Watergate scandal.[^89]

The stress of following her husband's career, which kept him away from home much of the time, resulted in Audrey suffering a [nervous breakdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_breakdown "Nervous breakdown") in 1954 while the family was living in Seattle. When Felt was put on trial in 1980, she attended the first day, but did not return, as she was unable to bear it. On July 20, 1984, she committed suicide using Felt's [revolver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolver "Revolver"). Felt and Mark Jr., an officer in the [US Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Air_Force "US Air Force") who flew [Air Force Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Force_Two "Air Force Two") for then-vice president [George H. W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._W._Bush "George H. W. Bush"), decided to keep this a secret and told Joan that her mother had died of a heart attack.[^89] [^91] Joan did not learn the truth about her mother's suicide until 2001.[^89]

Meanwhile, Joan had become an adherent of [Adi Da](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Da "Adi Da"), who had founded a [new religious movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement "New religious movement") in San Francisco called Adidam. At the time, she lived in [Santa Rosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Rosa,_California "Santa Rosa, California"). Joan had borne three sons – Ludi (later Will), Rob, and Nick, the latter two from another Adidam devotee whom she never married. Her parents met Ludi during their visit in 1974. After Audrey's death, Felt began making yearly visits to see Joan and his grandsons, and they would visit him and his new girlfriend, who lived in the same apartment complex.[^89] In 1990 Felt permanently moved to Santa Rosa, leaving Alexandria. He bought a house where he lived with Joan, and took care of the boys while she worked, teaching at [Sonoma State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonoma_State_University "Sonoma State University") and [Santa Rosa Junior College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Rosa_Junior_College "Santa Rosa Junior College").[^89] He suffered a stroke before 1999, as reported by Kessler in his book, *The Bureau.* According to Kessler's book, in mid-1999, Woodward showed up unexpectedly at the Santa Rosa home and took Felt to lunch.[^92]

Joan, who was caring for her father, told Kessler that her father had greeted Woodward like an old friend. Their meeting appeared to be more of a celebration than an interview. "Woodward just showed up at the door and said he was in the area," Joan Felt was quoted as saying in Kessler's 2002 book: "He came in a white limousine, which parked at a schoolyard about ten blocks away. He walked to the house. He asked if it was okay to have a martini with my father at lunch, and I said it would be fine." [^92]

## Memoir

Felt published his memoir *[The FBI Pyramid: From the Inside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_FBI_Pyramid "The FBI Pyramid")* in 1979. It was co-written with Hoover biographer [Ralph de Toledano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_de_Toledano "Ralph de Toledano"), though the latter's name appears only in the copyright notice. Toledano in 2005 wrote that the volume was "largely written by me since his original manuscript read like *[The Autocrat of the Breakfast-Table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Autocrat_of_the_Breakfast-Table "The Autocrat of the Breakfast-Table")* ". Toledano said: "Felt swore to me that he was not Deep Throat, and that he had never leaked information to the Woodward-Bernstein team or anyone else. The book was published and bombed." [^93]

In his memoir, Felt strongly defended Hoover and his tenure as director; he condemned the criticisms of the bureau made in the 1970s by the [Church Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Committee "Church Committee") and civil libertarians. He also denounced the treatment of Bureau agents as criminals and said the [Freedom of Information Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_Information_Act_\(United_States\) "Freedom of Information Act (United States)") and [Privacy Act of 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy_Act_of_1974 "Privacy Act of 1974") served only to interfere with government work and helped criminals. (He opens the book with the sentence, " [The Bill of Rights is not a suicide pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bill_of_Rights_is_not_a_suicide_pact "The Bill of Rights is not a suicide pact") ", Justice [Robert H. Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Jackson "Robert H. Jackson") 's comment in his dissent to *[Terminiello v. City of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminiello_v._City_of_Chicago "Terminiello v. City of Chicago")*, 337 U.S. 1 (1949)).[^7]

*[Library Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_Journal "Library Journal")* wrote in its 1980 review that "at one time Felt was assumed to be Watergate's 'Deep Throat'; in this interesting but hardly sensational memoir, he makes it clear that that honor, if honor it be, lies elsewhere." [^94] *[The New York Times Book Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Book_Review "The New York Times Book Review")* was highly critical of the book in 1980, saying Felt "seeks to perpetuate a view of Hoover and the FBI that is no longer seriously peddled even on the backs of cereal boxes". It said the book contained "a disturbing number of factual errors".[^95] [Curt Gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curt_Gentry "Curt Gentry") said in 1991 that Felt was "the keeper of the Hoover flame".[^96]

[Ronald Kessler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Kessler "Ronald Kessler") (a journalist who had worked at *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* alongside Woodward and Bernstein) had thought of Felt as Deep Throat when conducting interviews and research for his 2002 book *The Bureau: The Secret History of the FBI*, namely due to the measures Woodward took to conceal a meeting with Felt in 1999.[^97]

## Deep Throat speculation

The identity of Deep Throat was debated for more than three decades, and though Felt was not prominently mentioned as Watergate unfolded, his name was subsequently mentioned often as a possibility. An October 1990 *[Washingtonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washingtonian_\(magazine\) "Washingtonian (magazine)")* magazine article about "Washington secrets" listed the 15 most prominent Deep Throat candidates, including Felt.

[Jack Limpert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Limpert "Jack Limpert") published evidence as early as 1974 that Felt was the informant.[^98] On June 25 of that year, a few weeks after *[All the President's Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_the_President's_Men "All the President's Men")* was published, *[The Wall Street Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal "The Wall Street Journal")* ran an editorial, "If You Drink Scotch, Smoke, Read, Maybe You're Deep Throat". It began "W. Mark Felt says he isn't now, nor has he ever been Deep Throat." The *Journal* quoted Felt saying the character was a "composite" and "I'm just not that kind of person." [^99] In 1975 [George V. Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_V._Higgins "George V. Higgins") wrote: "Mark Felt knows more reporters than most reporters do, and there are some who think he had a *Washington Post* alias borrowed from a dirty movie." [^100] During a [grand jury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_jury "Grand jury") investigation in 1976, Felt was called to testify. The prosecutor, [J. Stanley Pottinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stanley_Pottinger "John Stanley Pottinger"), [Assistant Attorney General for Civil Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice_Civil_Rights_Division "United States Department of Justice Civil Rights Division"), discovered that Felt was "Deep Throat", but the secrecy of the proceedings protected the information from being public.[^101]

In 1992 [James Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mann_\(writer\) "James Mann (writer)"), who had been a reporter at *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* in 1972 and worked with Woodward, wrote a piece for *[The Atlantic Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic_Monthly "The Atlantic Monthly")*, saying the source had to have been within the FBI. He noted Felt as a possibility, but said he could not confirm this.[^102]

[Alexander P. Butterfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_P._Butterfield "Alexander P. Butterfield"), the White House aide best known for revealing Nixon's taping system, told the *[Hartford Courant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartford_Courant "Hartford Courant")* in 1995, "I think it was a guy named Mark Felt." [^103]

In July 1999, Felt was identified as Deep Throat by the *Hartford Courant*, citing Chase Culeman-Beckman, a nineteen-year-old from [Port Chester, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Chester,_New_York "Port Chester, New York"). Culeman-Beckman said Jacob Bernstein, the son of [Carl Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Bernstein "Carl Bernstein") and [Nora Ephron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nora_Ephron "Nora Ephron"), had told him the name at summer camp in 1988, and that Jacob claimed he had been told by his father. Felt said to the *Courant*, "No, it's not me. I would have done better. I would have been more effective. Deep Throat didn't exactly bring the White House crashing down, did he?" Bernstein said his son didn't know. "Bob and I have been wise enough never to tell our wives, and we've certainly never told our children." [^104]

However, Ephron did figure out the secret on her own, and she was the one who told their children:[^105] Ephron read in Woodward and Bernstein's book *All the President's Men* that in Bernstein's notes, he referred to Deep Throat as "MF";[^105] Bernstein said it stood for "My Friend", but Ephron correctly guessed it stood for Mark Felt, the former associate director of the FBI.[^105] After Ephron's marriage with Bernstein ended, Ephron revealed Deep Throat's identity to her son Jacob and anyone else who asked. She once said, "I would give speeches to 500 people and someone would say, 'Do you know who Deep Throat is?' And I would say, 'It's Mark Felt.'" [^106] Classmates of Jacob at the [Dalton School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalton_School "Dalton School") and [Vassar College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vassar_College "Vassar College") recall him revealing to numerous people that Felt was Deep Throat. This revelation attracted little media attention despite Deep Throat's identity being publicly unknown. Ephron said, "No one, apart from my sons, believed me." [^107] Ephron was invited by [Arianna Huffington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianna_Huffington "Arianna Huffington") to write about the experience in *The Huffington Post*, for which Ephron was a regular blogger and part-time editor.[^105] (Bernstein reiterated on June 2, 2005, on the *Today Show* that he had never told Ephron.)

[Leonard Garment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Garment "Leonard Garment"), President Nixon's former law partner who became White House counsel after John W. Dean's resignation, ruled Felt out as Deep Throat in his 2000 book *In Search of Deep Throat* (though he did also mention Jacob Bernstein telling other summer campers it was Mark Felt). Garment wrote:

> The Felt theory was a strong one... Felt had a personal motive for acting. After the death of J. Edgar Hoover... Felt thought he was a leading candidate to succeed Hoover... The characteristics were a good fit. The trouble with Felt's candidacy was that Deep Throat in *All the President's Men* simply did not sound to me like a career FBI man.[^108]

Garment said the information leaked to Woodward was inside White House information to which Felt would not have had access. "Felt did not fit." [^109]

In 2002 the *[San Francisco Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Chronicle "San Francisco Chronicle")* profiled Felt. Noting his denial in *The FBI Pyramid*, the paper wrote:

> Curiously, his son—American Airlines pilot Mark Felt—now says that shouldn't be read as a definitive denial, and that he plans to answer the question once-and-for-all in a second memoir. The excerpt of the working draft obtained by the *Chronicle* has Felt still denying he's Throat but providing a rationale for why Throat did the right thing.[^110]

In February 2005 reports surfaced that Woodward had prepared Deep Throat's [obituary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obituary "Obituary") because he was near death. Chief Justice [William Rehnquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rehnquist "William Rehnquist") was battling cancer at the time (he would die in September 2005), and there was speculation that Rehnquist might have been Deep Throat. Rehnquist was [Assistant Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Assistant_Attorney_General "United States Assistant Attorney General") of the [Office of Legal Counsel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Legal_Counsel "Office of Legal Counsel"), from 1969 to 1971,[^111] and then served on the Supreme Court until his death in 2005.

## Deep Throat revealed

*[Vanity Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_Fair_\(magazine\) "Vanity Fair (magazine)")* magazine revealed that Felt was Deep Throat on May 31, 2005, when it published an article (eventually appearing in the July issue of the magazine) on its website by John D. O'Connor, an attorney acting in Felt's behalf. Felt said, "I'm the guy they used to call Deep Throat." After the *Vanity Fair* story broke, [Benjamin C. Bradlee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_C._Bradlee "Benjamin C. Bradlee") on June 1, 2005, the editor of the *Washington Post* during Watergate, confirmed that Felt was Deep Throat.[^112] According to the *Vanity Fair* article, Felt was persuaded to come out by his family. They hoped to capitalize on the book deals and other lucrative opportunities which Felt would be offered in order to help pay for his grandchildren's education.[^2] His family was unaware that he was Deep Throat for many years. Although Felt was suffering from dementia and had previously denied he was Deep Throat, both Woodward and Bernstein confirmed the attorney's claim. Felt's family realized the truth after his retirement, when they became aware of his close friendship with Bob Woodward.

Nixon's Chief Counsel [Charles Colson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Colson "Charles Colson"), who served [prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prison "Prison") time for his actions in the Nixon White House, said Felt had violated "his oath to keep this nation's secrets".[^113] A *Los Angeles Times* editorial argued that this argument was specious, "as if there's no difference between nuclear strategy and rounding up [hush money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hush_money "Hush money") to silence your hired burglars".[^114]

[Ralph de Toledano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_de_Toledano "Ralph de Toledano"), who co-wrote Felt's 1979 memoir, said Mark Felt Jr. had approached him in 2004 to buy Toledano's half of the copyright. Toledano agreed to sell but was never paid. He attempted to rescind the deal, threatening legal action. A few days before the *Vanity Fair* article was released, Toledano finally received a check. He later said: "I had been gloriously and illegally deceived, and Deep Throat was, in characteristic style, back in business—which given his history of betrayal, was par for the course." [^93]

After the revelation, publishers were interested in signing Felt to a book deal. Weeks later, PublicAffairs Books announced that it signed a deal with Felt. Its [CEO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CEO "CEO") was a *Washington Post* reporter and editor during the Watergate era. The new book was to include material from Felt's 1979 [memoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoir "Memoir"), plus an update. The new volume was scheduled for publication in early 2006. Felt sold the movie rights to his story to [Universal Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Pictures "Universal Pictures") for development by [Tom Hanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Hanks "Tom Hanks") 's production company, [Playtone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playtone "Playtone"). The book and movie deals were valued at US$1 million.[^115] A film based on those rights, *[Mark Felt: The Man Who Brought Down the White House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Felt:_The_Man_Who_Brought_Down_the_White_House "Mark Felt: The Man Who Brought Down the White House")*, in which Felt is portrayed by [Liam Neeson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liam_Neeson "Liam Neeson"), was released in 2017.[^116]

In mid-2005 Woodward published an account of his contacts with Felt, *The Secret Man: The Story of Watergate's Deep Throat* ([ISBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISBN_\(identifier\) "ISBN (identifier)") [0-7432-8715-0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:BookSources/0-7432-8715-0 "Special:BookSources/0-7432-8715-0")).

## Death

Felt died at home, in his sleep, in Santa Rosa, California,[^117] [^118] on December 18, 2008.[^119] He was 95 years old and his death was attributed to [heart failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_failure "Heart failure").[^120]

In the 2023 TV mini-series *[White House Plumbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House_Plumbers_\(miniseries\) "White House Plumbers (miniseries)")*, he was played by [Gary Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Cole "Gary Cole").[^121]
