Shoshone County Courthouse, Wallace, Idaho–early undated photo

Shoshone County’s county seat moved from Murray (where it had been since July, 1885) to Wallace in November, 1898.

As county seat, Wallace needed a proper courthouse.

Six years would pass before county commissioners Israel B. Cowan and Ewell McIntosh announced a special meeting scheduled for March 8, 1905. Its topic, to discuss a bond measure that would provide funds for the purchase of land and the construction costs of a suitable new courthouse structure. Their measure, which would raise a fund of $75,000, soon passed handsomely in the County, with 1,440 yes votes and 347 no. On April 14, 1905 all three commissioners–Commissioner Fremont S. Rowe now present as well–voted unanimously to proceed with the courthouse project.

But an obstacle almost immediately arose. A suit challenged the process by which the commissioners had made the decision to issue bonds and launch the new construction project. It would go all the way to Idaho’s supreme court. It ruled, on August 10,1905, that the commissioners had acted properly and that the project could move forward.

![](https://ronroizen.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/courthouse-post-1910-fire.jpg?w=2048)

Courthouse survives the Big Burn, August, 1910

According to one source, the courthouse’s site was purchased from Henry E. Howes, who’d previously run either a sawmill or a grocery store there.  
  
The new structure was designed by two noted Spokane architects, Lewis R. Stritesky and John B. Sweatt, although one source (*Building Idaho: An Architectural History*, by Jennifer Eastman Atteberry, 1991, p. 91) also granted Francis P. Rooney a role in the design.  
  
Construction began on September 4, 1905. Initial plans suggested a completion date within a year’s time. But labor shortages and “union troubles” soon slowed progress. According to a 1906 article in *The Idaho Press*, 42,000 concrete blocks measuring 12 inches by 24 inches, many tons of cement, and “three \[railroad\] cars of steel” would be required for the project.

Historical architect, Fred Walters, some years ago noted that the building employed an advanced type of concrete block for its day, called “two-piece blocks.” Their use, said he, testified to “…the progressive design and refinement of the building’s construction.”  
  
The courthouse was “constructed with locally-produced concrete blocks containing mine tailings. The blocks are naturally soft, so over the years the courthouse outer walls have been covered with a stucco material.”

Said another source about the building’s aesthetics:

> *This structure is a good example of neoclassical revival style….it stands three stories on a raised foundation. It is distinguished by the two-story arched windows about the central entry with its balustraded balcony supported by medallions. Other features include the non-derivative fluted pilasters, denticulated cornice and high parapet.*

According to its original plan, the courthouse basement would supply space for the county jail, the sheriff’s office, and the boiler room. A small chamber adjoining the jail’s kitchen would be used for the detention of women prisoners. The main, second, and third floors would be used largely as they are today.

\* \* \* \* \*

Trouble visited the project as it neared completion. In November, 1906, the plasterers working on the interior left their task uncompleted and departed for a more attractive job in Davenport, Washington. Their union, however, ordered them back to Wallace to complete the job “under pain of being fined $100.”

As the end of April, 1907 approached, more trouble brewed. News emerged that General Contractor August Ilse stood to lose more than $30,000 on the project. The high price of labor and unanticipated extra expenses had increased construction costs from the originally contracted amount of $68,495 to “not less than $100,000.” By August 24 <sup>th</sup>, Ilse was ready to hand over the keys to the new structure to the commissioners, but he wanted more money. The commissioners balked.

On April 27 <sup>th</sup>, the *Spokesman-Review* published a detailed account of the project’s current financial situation. The county’s building fund for the project totaled $74,206.92. Expenditures so far were: $61,600 (of $68,495) to Ilse; $2,397.23 to Stritesky & Sweatt, architects; and a total of $4,645 to three subcontractors. This left a balance of $6,164.59 in the county’s fund.

Still outstanding construction-related obligations, however, summed to $10,983.55. Ilse had every right of course to receive the $7,495.00 he still hadn’t been paid to cover his $68,495 contract amount. But paying off Ilse would still leave $3,488.55 in unpaid bills. The commissioners were willing to cover only $784.40 of this amount, which left $2,704.15 in unpaid bills Ilse was responsible for.

![](https://ronroizen.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/courthouse-1940s.jpg)

Courthouse, in the 1940s (SOURCE: Barnard-Stockbridge Collection, University of Idaho Library)

In May came the additional news that Architect L.R. Stritesky had “filed a certificate with the commissioners to the effect that certain work on the building had not been completed by the contractor according to his contract.” The commissioners responded that they would not accept the building from Ilse unless and until the project was fully completed.

By the end of June, with the dispute between Ilse and the commissioners still hanging fire, county offices and personnel were making the move to the new building. County officials, said a June 27 <sup>th</sup> report, “…all have their coats off and are making creditable attempts at amateur furniture moving.” The county recorder’s office would by the last to make the transition.

In July, 1907, three subcontractors filed suit requesting liens on the new courthouse in amounts they were still owed by Ilse. Days afterward, Alfred Doull, Ilse’s superintendent of construction work, filed suit against both Shoshone County and Ilse for unpaid back wages.

Legal wrangling spawned more legal wrangling. In April, 1908, Shoshone County sued August Ilse amounts it had been forced to pay subcontractors. In 1910, Ilse sued the Aetna Indemnity Company for $15,000, which had bonded the courthouse project. As late as December, 1912, Shoshone County was renewing its suit against Ilse, asking $16,000 for the cost overruns in had been obliged to cover to subcontractors. In short, five years after the building was completed and occupied, determined attorneys were still seeking compensation for one or another party to the courthouse project.

\* \* \* \* \*

Despite all, the completed structure was an object of pride and satisfaction for both the Shoshone County community and the citizens of Wallace. Beyond its stately exterior appearance and handsome interior spaces, the building was furnished with the most modern steel furniture of the time, at no small expense.  
  
It was a time of prosperity and change in the county. The state of Idaho, admitted to the union on July 3, 1890, was still young and full of promise. Shoshone County was one of Idaho’s leading sources of prosperity. On September 1, 1907 came news that the county’s total assessed valuation had risen to $10,240,000—now second only to Ada County in Idaho. Wallace was flourishing as well. “The town had built up and changed wonderfully,” wrote detective Charles Siringo of his return there in 1906. The first automobile appeared on the streets of Wallace in 1907, purchased by Dr. McGee. A 1906 newspaper article promised the courthouse would be one of the most imposing and beautiful in this section of the state.  
  
It remains so today.
