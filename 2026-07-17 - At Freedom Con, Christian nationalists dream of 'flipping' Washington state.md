**Guest Analysis by Joshua C. Tom and Jason Wollschleger**

*The views expressed in this opinion column are those of the author. They do not necessarily reflect the* [*views*](https://favs.news/about-favs-news/) *of* [*FāVS News.*](https://favs.news/)

The full force of contemporary Christian nationalist rhetoric echoed in an unusual space this past Father’s Day weekend. While the Gorge Amphitheatre typically hosts blockbuster music acts, June 19 and 20 saw crowds of conservative Christian men gather for Freedom Con 2026.

Advertised as “An American Congress of Christian Men,” Freedom Con promised two days of religious revival, masculine empowerment and political mobilization. That was enough to draw several thousand attendees, and it was just as alluring for us, two sociologists of religion working in Washington State: Joshua Tom, an associate professor of sociology and director of the University Scholars Honors Program at Seattle Pacific University, and Jason Wollschleger, professor of sociology and the Master of Social Work program director at Whitworth University.

Drawn by the opportunity to observe the collision of religion, gender and politics in a spectacular venue, we paid our attendance fees and witnessed what happens when Christian nationalism goes smaller and bigger.

## Christian nationalistic ambitions of all sizes

“Smaller” is a relative term, of course. Discourse and research on Christian nationalism often highlight its macro-level ambitions, such as the takeover of federal government by conservative Christians and the imposition of conservative Christian values from the top down.

But nation-size objectives also require action at smaller levels of politics, like school-board elections and book bans at libraries.

Between the local and the national lies the state, and while Freedom Con brought in national-level speakers, the real meat of the weekend lay in the plans that organizers had for Washington State: nothing less than “flipping” the state legislature through the mobilization of Christians via churches.

That’s the “smaller.” The “bigger” not only exceeds the national scale, it teleports to a level where nations are barely a memory. Because the other layer of this conflict is cosmic, where fundamental forces of good and evil engage in battles with eternal consequences.

Freedom Con’s speakers continuously reminded the attendees that culture wars are battlefields in a much bigger war, where Satan and his demons work to destroy humanity through progressive politics.

## The ‘one, true conflict’

This weekend was more than political mobilization for the harried conservative Christians of Washington state; it was enlisting men into the one, true conflict that defines reality.

When the stakes are cosmic and civilizational, it may seem strange to center the Pacific Northwest as a location of import, but through nearly 12 hours of speakers we watched as Freedom Con attendees were implored to see the vital, strategic role of their home state.

On night one, Mark Driscoll, who built the Mars Hill Church empire in Seattle and fled its ruins for the gentler deserts of Arizona, had harsh words for the city he formerly called home:

“Seattle is one of the two injection points for the atheistic communistic poison that is seeking to destroy the United States of America, along with New York City, and the goal is that the government would own everything and control everyone … from a spiritual warfare strategy, if you were to invade our country, Seattle and New York would be good places to set up strongholds, and then to invade. What we are seeing right now is absolutely spiritual, and the clown show on the other side of this mountain is nothing more than old demons up to their old tricks in new days.”

The next day Josh McPherson, pastor of Grace City Church in Wenatchee and the host of the weekend, picked up Driscoll’s theme:

“I believe that Washington state is a covert black op state for every demonic progressive idea that the demonic powers are trying to run on our nation … What \[Mayor Zohran\] Mamdani is trying to do in New York and establishing it as a demonic stronghold, we’ve been doing institutionally as a state for 41 years.”

## Waging war against ‘supernatural evil’

This multi-layered view of this reality is crucial to understanding the men of Freedom Con, and Christian nationalism writ large. While they share the same mundane concerns and fears that every American engaged in politics feels, they live with the added burden of knowing that they struggle against powerful, personal supernatural evils.

This is highly motivating as a rallying cry — every political effort has eternal meaning — and as the context in which America’s saviors are called to step up. For who could possibly contend with Satan and his demonic armies?

According to McPherson, God has chosen the Christian men of Washington to lead the charge.

“We are stepping into the call of God as happy warriors, running downhill with the momentum of the Spirit of God to crash the gates of hell, and I believe God’s going to give us victory.”

With that, he introduced Adam James as an exemplar of this call. James, a pastor at McPherson’s church, is running as a Republican for the State House to represent the 12th Legislative District, covering Chelan county and parts of King and Snohomish counties.

James wasted no time in drawing the stakes.

“Pastor Mark was right, there’s injection points in this nation … make no mistake, friends, Seattle sends its demons to Olympia.”

## A call to political ‘infliltration’

This was the ultimate goal of the weekend: a call to action for the men of Freedom Con to show up in the political sphere, whether that means running for office themselves or simply getting more involved in local and state politics.

Guest speaker Nate Schatzline, a pastor and state representative from Texas, was there touting his organization “For Liberty & Justice,” which specializes in mobilizing churches for conservative politics.

“We will be in 28 states minimum by the end of the year … the reality is that we have a specific blueprint that we go in and begin to infiltrate to put Bible-believing Christians into local office … I believe as Washington goes, so goes the West Coast. As goes the West Coast, so goes an awakening and revival that can wake up America,” Schatzline said.

The goal of this infiltration is the legislative flipping of Washington from Democratic to Republican control.

“We believe God has given us a divine strategy,” declared James.

Citing numbers from the Family Policy Institute of Washington and the Pew Research Center, he shared that “1.2 million self-identified Christians in Washington State” did not vote in the last election, and that at least 10 districts could have been swung if 41,000 votes had been cast by those Christians.

## Checking the numbers

These numbers were repeated by speakers for the rest of the weekend, and as sociologists we were naturally interested in verifying them. First, we question the assumption that 1.2 million non-voting Christians mean what they imply; “Christian” is a category of diverse political, cultural and even theological commitments, and is not synonymous with “Republican.”

But even apart from that, the statistics are dubious. Replicating those numbers required us to use Pew data that 51% of Washingtonians are Christian and apply it to voting data from the last election — required us to do poor social science data analysis, in other words. God’s divine strategy accommodates some shaky electoral math.

We have little faith in the numbers presented that weekend, but then we were far from the target audience. Schatzline directly urged Freedom Con’s attendees to remain in Washington for this moment: “You must commit to stay and fight alongside the remnant. … There are people that were planning to move to my state in Texas in this crap. I am calling you to stay. I am calling you to stay, Washington is too important to lose more ground.”

The weekend’s oratorical force was effective: Washington’s Christian men had not been showing up to a winnable fight. But with the right combination of efforts, Washington could be retaken, and then the West Coast, and then the country, with Satan, his demons and political progressives the big losers.

Our immersion in the words and company of Washington’s Christian nationalists revealed the depths of their mission, and the worldview that defines it. It also gave us a peek into the organized people and groups seeking to capitalize on the resentments bubbling at the intersection of religion and politics.

The rest of the summer at the Gorge belongs to artists and fans, but for that one weekend it belonged to strategists engaged in a political, cultural and spiritual war.

*Joshua Tom has a PhD in Sociology from Baylor University. He is an associate professor of sociology and director of the University Scholars Honors Program at Seattle Pacific University.*
