**Forrest Morton Bird** (June 9, 1921 – August 2, 2015) was an American [aviator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_pilot "Aircraft pilot"), [inventor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inventor "Inventor"), and [biomedical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_engineering "Biomedical engineering"). He is best known for having created some of the first reliable [mass-produced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_production "Mass production") [mechanical ventilators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_ventilator "Mechanical ventilator") for acute and chronic [cardiopulmonary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiopulmonary "Cardiopulmonary") care.[^1]

## Biography

Bird was born in [Stoughton, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoughton,_Massachusetts "Stoughton, Massachusetts"). Bird became a [pilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviator "Aviator") at an early age with his father's encouragement. By age 14, he flew his first solo fight.

By age 16 he was working to obtain multiple major [pilot certifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_licensing_and_certification "Pilot licensing and certification").[^2] Bird enlisted with the [United States Army Air Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Air_Corps "United States Army Air Corps"), and entered active duty in 1941 as a technical air training officer due to his advanced qualifications. This rank, combined with the onset of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), gave him the opportunity to pilot almost every [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft "Aircraft") in service, including early [jet aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_aircraft "Jet aircraft") and [helicopters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicopter "Helicopter").

The newest models of aircraft were capable of exceeding [altitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude "Altitude") at which humans can breathe, even with 100% oxygen supplementation, introducing the risk of [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_\(medical\) "Hypoxia (medical)"). Bird discovered an oxygen regulator in a crashed German bomber he was ferrying to the U.S. for study seemed to contain a [pressure breathing circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_breathing_circuit?action=edit&redlink=1 "Pressure breathing circuit (page does not exist)"). He took the oxygen regulator home, studied it, and made it more functional. It became the standard design for high-altitude oxygen regulators for most military aircraft until recent time. Bird studied medicine "... to understand the human body and its stress in flight". This led to him developing efficient respirators and ventilators.[^3] In 1957 he released the Bird Mark 7 ventilator, the first mass produced ventilator.

In 1967, Bird developed the [Bird Innovator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_Innovator "Bird Innovator"), a conversion of the Consolidated [PBY Catalina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBY_Catalina "PBY Catalina") amphibian aircraft, the aircraft being based at [Palm Springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_Springs "Palm Springs") until 1976.[^4] His company was Bird Oxygen Breathing Equipment Inc, later renamed Bird Corporation.

Bird resided in [Sagle, Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagle,_Idaho "Sagle, Idaho"), close to the [Canada–US border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada%E2%80%93United_States_border "Canada–United States border") which is where his home, production facilities, museum and ranch were located. Bird collected and restored old planes, old cars, and motorcycles.[^5]

Forrest and Pamela Bird opened the Bird Aviation Museum and Invention Center in July 2007, with aviator [Patty Wagstaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patty_Wagstaff "Patty Wagstaff") cutting a ceremonial ribbon at the end of the runway *while* flying. The Bird's are the founders and owners of the museum, which showcases Bird's various aircraft and inventions.[^6] On December 10, 2008, Bird received the [Presidential Citizens Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Citizens_Medal "Presidential Citizens Medal") from President George Bush. The United States honored him for his groundbreaking contributions and for his work to keep America at the forefront of discovery.[^7] On October 7, 2009, President Barack Obama awarded Bird the [National Medal of Technology and Innovation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Technology_and_Innovation "National Medal of Technology and Innovation"), a recognition of his "outstanding contributions to the promotion of technology for the improvement of the economic, environmental or social well-being of the United States." [^8]

Bird died at the age of 94 of natural causes at his Sagle, Idaho home on August 2, 2015.[^9] [^10]

## Mechanical ventilators

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d7/Bird_Mark_8_Medical_respirator1.JPG/250px-Bird_Mark_8_Medical_respirator1.JPG)

Bird Mark 8 ventilator

### The first "Bird" units

All of Bird's ventilators were contained in transparent plastic cases. Bird's rationale for see-through encasements for his machines was a stroke of genius: he thought if people could see the inner workings of mechanical devices, they would understand them better, and be able to use, fix, and apply them better in the real world. Bird created a car unit which was tested on seriously ill patients with limited success. His first prototype consisted of strawberry shortcake tins and a doorknob. Most of these first units were sold to the Army, in the original format of tins and the doorknob. Further revision resulted in the 1955 release of the "Bird Universal Medical Respirator" (sold as the Bird Mark 7 Respirator and informally called the "Bird"), a small green box that became familiar to hospital patients soon after its introduction. The Bird Mark 8 added the capabilities of NEEP (Negative End Expiratory Pressure). This was frequently used to power a set of fluidic servos (sort of relays.) He subsequently made a ventilator for infants, nicknamed the "Babybird". This device was one of several devices that appeared on the market designed to effectively ventilate small children and infants. These devices played a significant role in reducing the rate of breathing-related infant mortality from 70% to 10%.[^11] [^12] The Bird Mark 7 Respirator is still in use around the world. In addition, he produced the Fluid Control Device.

### Accolades

Bird was awarded the Lifetime Scientific Achievement Award in 1985 by the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science "American Association for the Advancement of Science") (AAAS), receiving a second award in September 2005. He continued to contribute to the field of [pulmonary science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung "Lung") by participating in the development of the VDR, a ventilator that permits management of the most challenging patients including ARDS, trauma and inhalation injury. In 1995, Bird was inducted into the [National Inventors Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Inventors_Hall_of_Fame "National Inventors Hall of Fame").[^13] He was named "Inventor of the Week" by [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology") in February 2001. The [American Respiratory Care Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Respiratory_Care_Foundation "American Respiratory Care Foundation") names one of its annual awards after Bird.[^14]

### History of Bird Corp.

- 1965: First factory assembly line rolls out a medical respirator for home health, the Mark III.
- 1971: Bird introduces first infant ventilator.
- 1978: Bird sells his namesake company to [3M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3M "3M"), which took it public.
- 1984: 3M sells Bird Products to the management group of a competitor, Bird Medical Technologies Inc.
- 1987: The Bird 6400ST is released, the first new-generation ventilator.
- 1990: Bird Medical Technologies goes public, and is traded on [NASDAQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASDAQ "NASDAQ") under the ticker, BMTI.
- 1992: Bird Medical, reporting $36.5 million in sales in 1991, lays off 21 of 211 workers, citing poor economic conditions and falling sales. Despite the downturn, construction of its new, 120,000-square-foot (11,000 m <sup>2</sup>) building at 1100 Bird Center Drive, its present site, continues.
- 1995: [Thermo Electron Corp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermo_Electron_Corp. "Thermo Electron Corp."), which acquired Bird in a $67 million buyout of Bird stock, moves a [Riverside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside,_California "Riverside, California") -based subsidiary into the Palm Springs location. That year, Bird is inducted into the [National Inventors Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Inventors_Hall_of_Fame "National Inventors Hall of Fame").
- 2002: Through acquisition and consolidation, the venture becomes a part of VIASYS Respiratory Care.
- 2009: [Cardinal Health Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Health_Inc. "Cardinal Health Inc."), a *[Fortune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_\(magazine\) "Fortune (magazine)")* 20 company based in [Dublin, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin,_Ohio "Dublin, Ohio"), that was spun off from its parent company to the wholly owned subsidiary, [CareFusion Corp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CareFusion "CareFusion") The year before, Cardinal Health relocated three sister companies to the Palm Springs operation: Bear Medical of Riverside, SensorMedics Corp. of [Yorba Linda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorba_Linda "Yorba Linda") and EME Medical of [Brighton, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton,_England "Brighton, England").
