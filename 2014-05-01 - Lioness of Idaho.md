The Life of Louise Shadduck

*Photos Courtesy of Mike Bullard*

*After I retired as a minister, I decided to write about my friend Louise Shadduck, whom I knew was a great role model for young women, although at that time I had no idea of just how colorful and influential she had really been. Digging through boxes of her papers in the basement of the library at the University of [[Idaho]], I saw photos of her with U.S. presidents and world leaders, and newspaper articles about how she changed the whole state. My book, Lioness of Idaho: Louise Shadduck and the Power of Polite (2013) documents how, as Secretary of Commerce and Development, she gained national attention for leading Idaho into its ten best economic years of the twentieth century. It tells how this high school graduate became an administrator for two governors and for a U.S. senator and U.S. congressman, a recognized international leader among women journalists, author of five books, and was once one of the most celebrated citizens in Idaho.*

The following story excerpted from my book shows a different side of her. In private life she was a fast-driving, horseback-riding sister of six brothers from a pre-Depression Idaho farm. When she saw a small plane crash in the distance on an Idaho mountain, she thought nothing of plowing through knee-deep snow in her sneakers to help a stranger in trouble. Yet she never told most of her friends and family. Starting only with an unusual clipping in the Idaho Statesman, I searched for a year to put together the accounts and find someone who was there.

A life and death ordeal on a snowy mountainside was the last thing on the minds of Louise or her staffers Jan and Kathleen as they set out driving from Boise on an eighty-degree June day in 1967.

Louise had hired Janice Moulton to help with much of the department’s copious writing work, and Jan became, in effect, the department’s publicity officer. Eighteen-year-old Kathleen Barrett had been taken on just a year before as a part-time intern.

One of Louise’s favorite pastimes was introducing young adults to celebrities who might inspire them and help their careers. The three were headed for Sun Valley to attend the annual convention of the powerful National Federation of Press Women where Louise could introduce Jan and Kathleen to some of the greatest female role models in the country. \[private\]

As Louise drove past Mountain Home and turned north at Twin Falls, unknown to her, two young parents in faraway San Gabriel, California readied their private plane to take their baby on a camping trip in Idaho’s Sawtooth Mountains. No one could have foreseen how these separate lives would soon intersect.

Louise had several significant roles at the convention. She had never lost that “printer’s ink” she once said ran in her blood. In the midst of her other work, Louise had continued to write and to participate in journalistic endeavors, including the statewide organization known as Idaho Press Women \[now Media Network Idaho\]. That group had elected her president the year before. Democratic Senator Frank Church reached across party lines to send Louise a letter congratulating her on that election. His letter marveled that she managed to get so much done. One of the first of her accomplishments as president of the state organization was to get them to invite the National Federation of Press Women to Idaho. Louise was tremendously excited about that venerable organization coming to Idaho for its first-ever convention in a western state.

Louise worked with Sun Valley’s Dorice Taylor and others to develop an occasion that would make every participant want to return to Idaho. Dorice was one-time publicity director for Union Pacific Railroad, which first owned the Sun Valley Resort. She did for Sun Valley what Louise did for the entire state—marketing, inviting and accommodating famous people. Dorice had worked hard behind the scenes long before even being given a title, much less due credit. Many in Sun Valley now believe she was the driving force that really made Sun Valley, in her time, the most famous winter destination resort in the world.

Now, as president of the host organization, as a member of the accommodations planning committee, and as an official of the host state’s government, Louise wore many hats at the gathering. A couple of years later, one of Louise’s friends would write from Ephrata, Washington, that the convention in Sun Valley was the best she had ever attended, and that Louise was the event’s guiding light. The keynote speaker for the convention was to be Maggie Bellows, urban affairs correspondent for United Press International. Bellows was to speak on problems and opportunities in cities all over the U.S.

Kathleen and Jan checked into a room together, but Louise roomed with some of the event’s other leaders in the historic Sun Valley Lodge where Ernest Hemingway is said to have stayed in room 206 while writing *For Whom the Bell Tolls*.

The next day, Louise was slated to present a framed painting to eighty-eight-year-old Dona Coulter Carnes for fifty years of writing for the *Daily Eagle* in Bryan, Texas. Louise herself would be receiving an award in the category of “Promotion and Publicity” for an article she had written about Redfish Lake and the Sawtooth Mountains. That would all be later in the evening, but after breakfast and morning activities a long lunch break allowed time for fresh air, and a chance to show off Idaho. Louise had planned numerous sightseeing activities for the conventioneers, but the weather was not cooperating and some events were canceled. Still Louise invited Kathleen, Jan, and Dorthee Polson, food editor of a Phoenix newspaper, to drive up to see this Redfish Lake she had described in her article.

Meanwhile at nearby Hailey airport, Arthur O’Rourke looked up at the broken clouds and thought about the intermittent rain showers. He and his young wife Helen had left San Gabriel the night before with their baby and their camping gear hoping to meet friends at a landing strip in Stanley Basin. Stopping to look at maps in Hailey, Arthur told rangers the weather was too unstable for them and they were going to check into a hotel in Hailey. Unfortunately, at some point they changed their minds.

Driving north out of Ketchum, Louise and friends passed the old North Fork Restaurant where Marilyn Monroe’s *Bus Stop* was filmed in 1956. It was Dorice Taylor who had suggested that spot for the movie’s winter scenes at a place the movie called “Gracie’s Diner.”

June in Sun Valley usually meant shirtsleeve or perhaps sweater weather, but as the women continued north on Route 75 toward the 8,500-foot pass at Galena Summit, they started seeing hard-cased piles of snow still left among the trees from the previous winter. The sky was grey with cool mist hanging over the white mounds. The rocky crags of the Sawtooth range were making their own weather over Smiley Creek Airport.

When the women got to Redfish Lake, nestled at 6,500 feet among the mountains, Kathy, Jan and Dorthee probably breathed a sigh of relief. Louise had driven since she was old enough to reach the pedals of the old farm truck. Louise told friends that God watched over her as she drove, but others said Louise’s fast driving just made people pray more.

Louise told the women how the glacier-carved lake got its name from the sockeye salmon that once made the river glow red. The hardy fish had climbed upstream through nine hundred miles of rivers to the place where they were hatched. The Sunbeam Dam ended that in 1910 but, with its destruction twenty-five years later, hope returned that salmon would someday run again. Even on a rainy day with no salmon the lake had its own beautiful mystique.

Heading back down the road and over Galena Pass, Janice and Kathleen were busily talking in the backseat when Louise blurted out, “Oh gosh! They’re going down!” Coming out under the clouds, a small plane was too low and flying at the wrong angle.

Louise’s car lurched to a stop and before the others fully comprehended what had happened, Louise dove into the rocky, snow-packed forest. Jan stayed in the car, but Kathleen and Dorthee ran after Louise. Louise was fifty-one, but all eighteen-year-old Kathleen could do was follow her trail as the sometimes waist-deep snow engulfed the women’s sneakers.

Louise knew exactly which way to lead them through the woods. When Kathleen finally got there she was almost upon the crash site before she realized it. She heard a voice she had not heard before. It was not the booming voice that echoed between the offices of the Capitol. It was not the strong voice holding forth on a podium. This was Louise’s soft and comforting voice.

“You’ll be okay. Hang on. You just be strong and keep yourself alive. We’ll take care of your baby.” Helen O’Rourke was pinned in the plane and could see that her husband was dead. Louise knew she was not in a condition to take the rest of the bad news.

Wrapping the silent baby in a colorful blanket from the plane, Louise handed her to Kathleen. Louise looked up at the trees sheared off and realized the angle at which the plane had come down. An experienced passenger who had flown over the mountains many times, Louise commented to others who had reached the scene about how cruel the air currents and drafts could be in the mountains. Louise told Kathleen to take the baby and head for the road fast. She didn’t want the trapped mother to know – at least not until medical help got there.

Louise stayed behind a few more minutes as others arrived to care for Helen. Louise easily caught up to Kathleen and Dorthee, as they slowly picked their way through the rocks and snow. Sirens began to echo among the mountains. It was not until she handed the baby to Louise that Kathleen realized what Louise had known all along. The little girl and her father had both perished.

The next day, Thursday, June 22, a Boise newspaper showed two very different pictures of Louise, both taken on the same day just a few hours apart. Preserved in her files without the full header, one of these clippings shows that Louise was at the NFPW convention, nicely dressed and beaming as she presented Dona’s award. The other one showed Louise carrying a tiny body out of the woods.

In public Louise was the consummate, poised professional, with a quick wit and an encyclopedic knowledge of people, names and facts, ready to speak to heads of state at a moment’s notice.

Outdoors she was a strong, adaptive and rugged pioneer spirit. One of her friends said she was reluctant to wear short sleeves that would show just how muscular her arms were. For Louise, the two different identities were just different sides of the same one confident person with an unusual balance between realism and idealism.

After the convention, Louise had yet another task. It would be hard to do, but she was not someone to leave such things undone. When Kathleen left the convention for Boise, Louise had her take the baby’s blanket to get it cleaned. Louise might not have been a biological mother, but her instincts told her that in times of deep grief something familiar can help a little. Louise got the blanket and told Kathleen where she was going. Putting the blanket in the car she drove toward the hospital in Boise where Helen O’Rourke was being treated. \[/private\]
