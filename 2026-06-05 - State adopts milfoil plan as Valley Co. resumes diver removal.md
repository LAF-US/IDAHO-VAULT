Herbicides will be used in Payette Lake for the first time this summer to kill Eurasian watermilfoil.

The Idaho State Department of Agriculture recently approved [a five-year management plan](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/564b8c9ae4b0459b2b8187a3/t/69fe6add93c3a862c540fdf3/1778281181908/Payette+Lake+Reciprocal+Plan_PrintVersion.pdf) that calls for the use of herbicides as well as mechanical removal by divers to reduce the invasive plant infestation to “manageable levels.”

The plan was approved by ISDA Director Chanel Tewalt following a review by city, county, and state agencies that helped draft the plan. The group included the City of McCall, Valley County, Ponderosa State Park, the Big Payette Lake Water Quality Council, and the Idaho Department of Environmental Quality, among other agencies.

“Once the advisory group came to a consensus on the plan, Director Tewalt gave her final signature,” said Sydney Kennedy, an ISDA spokesperson.

For this summer, the plan calls for applying herbicide to 25 to 50 acres of Payette Lake at a cost of about $123,000 to $237,000. The exact locations of the treatments will be determined by updated surveys of the infestation, starting with Pilgrim Cove, Wagon Wheel Bay, and the western shoreline of Ponderosa State Park – all areas shown by previous surveys to have dense infestations.

“In the treatment plan, we have surveying scheduled for July, though we are hoping to get out there as early as this month,” Kennedy said.

Milfoil grows in shallow waters around the lake’s shoreline that receives sunlight, or areas up to about 30 feet deep. Each time the plant is broken — by a swimmer, boater, or even wave action — the fragment drifts off and settles elsewhere on the lakebed to sprout a new plant.

The plant can grow three to five feet in length each summer. If left unchecked, it could degrade water quality while also choking out boat propellers, fish habitat, and native plants.

## Herbicide of choice

The state’s plan identifies [ProcellaCOR](https://sepro.com/aquatics/procellacor-product) as the “herbicide of choice,” in part because it “minimizes impacts to native plants, fish and wildlife.”

The herbicide would also not require any restrictions related to recreation, fishing, or drinking water. The City of McCall’s water system is fed by water from Payette Lake.

Irrigation restrictions for broadleaf plants and crops will apply for water drawn from within a quarter mile of the treatment zone. Those restrictions will be publicly noticed two weeks ahead of scheduled treatments and would remain in place until post-treatment testing can no longer detect traces of ProcellaCOR. Public notices would also be posted on nearby docks 24 hours before treatments.

Post-treatment testing would include lakebed sediment samples collected 30 days, 90 days, and one year later to inform future herbicide treatments. Pre-treatment samples would also be collected.

## Mechanical removal by divers also planned

The state also plans to deploy diver teams with mechanical dredges that remove milfoil from the lakebed without allowing any plant fragments to escape. Those efforts are likely to be targeted around public boat ramps, but Kennedy said the exact locations and extent of mechanical removal will also be determined by the surveys.

“This is consistent with any of our treatment plans,” she said. “Survey first, then determine the approach.”

Meanwhile, Valley County just approved spending $35,000 to hire a team of divers to perform mechanical removal this summer. Weed Department Supervisor Steve Anderson said the county’s efforts will be coordinated with ISDA.

“We’ll concentrate more on the boat ramps and the docks and that kind of area, and the main marina area where most of the traffic is,” Anderson said. “I want people to see us out there working.”

Anderson said he is optimistic that the county could go back to keeping the milfoil infestation in check using only mechanical removal by divers after a few years of herbicide treatments.

He estimates it would cost about $100,000 to have one crew of divers work for three months removing milfoil from the lake. Anderson suggested other organizations and even homeowners around the lake would likely be willing to contribute funding to the project if it means fewer herbicide treatments.

“We just need to shake the tree and see what falls out of it,” he said.

Last summer, ISDA planned to test the use of herbicides on milfoil in Payette Lake, but [called it off amid public opposition](https://valleylookout.com/2025/07/24/chemicals-or-bust-state-punts-on-payette-lake-milfoil-amid-public-opposition-to-herbicide/). The five-year management plan approved by the state was then drafted alongside local agencies over the winter.

Beyond this summer, annual treatment plans would be based on new surveys completed each July, when new milfoil growth is typically about eight to 12 inches. The “integrated” approach prescribed by the draft plan calls for a blend of herbicide treatments and mechanical removal.

The plan’s success will be gauged based on total infested acres in the lake, milfoil density in infested areas, and transitioning from large-scale treatments to localized maintenance.

In 2024, ISDA surveys showed the infestation covered about 183 acres of Payette Lake, compared to 98 acres in 2023 and 77 acres in 2022.

Milfoil was first found in Payette Lake in the late 1990s. An annual removal program led by Valley County began in 2001 and continued through 2022, at which point ISDA assumed control of the program at the county’s request.

No mechanical removal has been completed since 2023 after ISDA determined it was no longer a cost-effective approach to managing milfoil given the amount observed in Payette Lake.
