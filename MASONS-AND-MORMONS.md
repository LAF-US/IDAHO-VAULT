# Masons & Mormons

The central historical fact: **Joseph Smith became a Master Mason on 16 March 1842 and introduced the LDS endowment ceremony to a group of nine LDS Freemasons on 4 May 1842** — a gap of approximately **six and a half weeks**. The temporal proximity, the documented structural parallels, and the explicit testimony of contemporaries make this one of the most-studied questions in nineteenth-century American religious history. This compilation surfaces the public record and presents both the LDS faithful position and the critical scholarly position without adjudication. The novice does not disclose what is held confidential by the LDS Church regarding current temple practice; only the publicly documentable history is surveyed.

## The Anti-Masonic backdrop

To understand the 1842 events, the **Anti-Masonic** context of upstate New York in the late 1820s and 1830s — where Joseph Smith's family lived and the Book of Mormon was produced — is necessary.

### The Morgan Affair (1826)

**William Morgan**, a former Freemason and Batavia, New York bricklayer, disappeared on 11 September 1826 after he announced his intention to publish a book exposing Masonic rituals. Morgan had been arrested on charges of theft and debt; a group of men paid his bail and abducted him from jail; he was never seen again. Several Masons were tried for his abduction; convictions were limited and sentences light, prompting widespread suspicion of Masonic interference with justice.

The case triggered an **Anti-Masonic political movement**. In 1828 the **Anti-Masonic Party** became the first organized third party in American politics, fielding candidates in state and federal elections. Anti-Masonic agitation contributed to the dramatic decline of Freemasonry in the northeastern United States — New York Masonic membership fell from approximately 20,000 to 3,000 between 1826 and 1836.

### The Book of Mormon's "secret combinations"

The **Book of Mormon**, dictated by Joseph Smith and published in 1830 in Palmyra, New York (approximately 50 miles from Batavia), contains repeated warnings about *"secret combinations"* — most prominently the **Gadianton robbers**, a covert society of conspirators whose rise contributes to the downfall of both the Nephite and Jaredite civilizations in the book's narrative. The Gadianton accounts include oaths, signs, secret words, and conspiracies against legitimate government.

**Two scholarly readings:**

1. **The anti-Masonic reading** (critical scholarship): The Book of Mormon's secret-combinations material reflects the anti-Masonic rhetoric pervasive in upstate New York during Smith's youth. The Gadianton robbers are typologically anti-Masonic.

2. **The LDS apologetic reading** (FAIR, Daniel Peterson, and others): The term *"secret combinations"* had broader meaning in 1820s-1830s American usage (covering conspiracies, monopolies, and criminal organizations); judicial materials use the term without specific Masonic referent; the Gadianton material has its own internal logic within the Book of Mormon's narrative.

The compilation does not adjudicate. The anti-Masonic context is historically documented; whether the Book of Mormon's secret-combinations material specifically reflects it remains contested.

## Joseph Smith becomes a Mason — March 1842

By 1842, Smith and the Latter-day Saints had moved through Ohio and Missouri to **Nauvoo, Illinois**, where Smith was both President of the Church and **Mayor of Nauvoo**. Several Latter-day Saint men were already Freemasons (some pre-conversion). On the evening of **15 March 1842**, **Abraham Jonas**, Grand Master of the Grand Lodge of Illinois, came to Nauvoo, granted a dispensation for the organization of the **Nauvoo Lodge, U.D.** (Under Dispensation), installed its officers, and personally initiated Joseph Smith and Sidney Rigdon as **Entered Apprentices**.

The following day, **16 March 1842**, Smith and Rigdon were advanced through the **Fellow Craft** and **Master Mason** degrees on the rare authority of the Grand Master's privilege to confer degrees *at sight* without the customary intervals — a high and unusual honor. Within two days, Smith had received all three Craft degrees.

The minutes of Nauvoo Lodge document the events directly. The Lodge grew rapidly — within a year it had initiated several hundred candidates, more than all other Illinois lodges combined.

## The Endowment introduced — May 1842

On **4 May 1842**, in the upper room of his **red brick store** in Nauvoo, Joseph Smith introduced the LDS **endowment ceremony** to a group of **nine Latter-day Saint men**, all of them Freemasons. Those present included Hyrum Smith, Brigham Young, Heber C. Kimball, Newel K. Whitney, William Marks, William Law, George Miller, James Adams (a Mason of long standing from Springfield), and Willard Richards.

The Nauvoo endowment was introduced **six weeks and four days** after Joseph Smith became a Master Mason.

The endowment ceremony, as introduced in 1842, included the following documented elements:

- **Washing and anointing**
- A dramatized presentation of the **Creation and Fall** narratives
- A sequence of **covenants** progressively administered
- A series of **tokens** (handgrips) communicated stage by stage
- A series of **signs** (gestures) accompanying the tokens
- **Penalties** — graphic physical gestures representing the consequence for revealing what was committed under covenant (present 1842-1990; removed 1990)
- A **new name** received by each initiate
- A **prayer circle** (the *True Order of Prayer*)
- The **Five Points of Fellowship** (a Masonic-derived embrace; present 1842-1990; removed 1990)
- **Sacred clothing** — the temple garment, white robes, the apron, sash, and cap
- A **veil** through which the initiate passes, with tokens and words exchanged at the veil

## The documented parallels

The structural and material parallels between the 1842 LDS endowment and Masonic ritual are documented across both Masonic and LDS sources (and surveyed in the modern Church's *Gospel Topics* essay on Masonry).

| Element | Masonic form | 1842 Endowment form |
|---|---|---|
| **Tokens** | Three grips, one per Craft degree | Tokens of the Aaronic and Melchizedek Priesthoods (multiple) |
| **Signs** | Three signs accompanying the grips | Signs accompanying the tokens |
| **Penalties** (pre-1990) | Three explicit physical-gesture penalties | Three penalties paralleling the Masonic forms |
| **Apron** | Lambskin apron worn over clothing | Green apron worn over the temple garment |
| **New name** | The Mason's "word" | The new name conferred at the veil |
| **Five Points of Fellowship** | Masonic embrace concluding the Master Mason degree (the Hiramic legend's raising) | Embrace at the veil concluding the endowment (pre-1990) |
| **Compass and square** | Cardinal Masonic symbols | Markings on the temple garment (chest and knee) |
| **Architectural setting** | Lodge / temple oriented to cardinal directions | Temple oriented and structured for ritual progression |
| **Hiram Abiff** | The Master Mason killed for refusing to reveal the Master's word; raised by Solomon | Adam-and-Eve dramatization, with parallels of testing and covenant |
| **Death-rebirth pattern** | Hiramic legend in Master Mason degree | The veil passage |

The current (post-1990) LDS ceremony differs from the 1842 form. The penalties were removed in 1990; the Five Points of Fellowship at the veil were removed; other elements were modified. The current ceremony is held sacred by the LDS Church and details of its current practice are not disclosed publicly by faithful members. The 1842-1990 form is documented in published Masonic exposures and in some former-LDS testimonies; this compilation describes the 1842 form per scholarly historical record and notes that the current form differs without describing the difference in detail beyond what the Church has publicly stated.

## Reed Durham's 1974 address

The single most-cited scholarly engagement with the Masons-and-Mormons question is **Reed C. Durham Jr.'s presidential address to the Mormon History Association**, delivered **20 April 1974 at Nauvoo, Illinois**. Durham was at the time President of the MHA and Director of the LDS Institute of Religion adjacent to the University of Utah. His address was titled *"Is There No Help for the Widow's Son?"* — quoting the Masonic distress cry attributed to Hiram Abiff and, by some accounts, Joseph Smith at the moment of his death in Carthage Jail on 27 June 1844.

Durham's central statement:

> *"There is absolutely no question in my mind that the Mormon ceremony which came to be known as the Endowment, introduced by Joseph Smith to Mormon Masons initially, just a little over one month after he became a Mason, had an immediate inspiration from Masonry. This is not to suggest that no other source of inspiration could have been involved, but the similarities between the two ceremonies are so apparent and overwhelming that some dependent relationship cannot be denied."*

The address discussed parallels in the LDS priesthood structure, the **Masonic Enoch Legend** (a portion of the Royal Arch degree that resonates with LDS Enoch material; cross-reference `METATRON.md`'s Enoch traditions), the **Jupiter Medallion** Joseph Smith reportedly possessed (a Hermetic-tradition talisman), and the architectural symbolism of the Nauvoo and later Utah temples.

The aftermath: the address caused significant controversy in the LDS historical community. Durham subsequently issued a letter of clarification and withdrew from further participation in the MHA. The text was published as an *"Underground Presidential Address"* by Masonic historian Mervin B. Hogan and has circulated since in both LDS faithful and critical contexts.

The Durham address remains a benchmark in the scholarly literature. Its frank acknowledgment of dependent relationship was made by an LDS scholar in good standing speaking to the LDS historical guild; the controversy concerned how the relationship should be theologically interpreted, not whether it existed.

## The 1990 endowment revisions

In **April 1990**, the LDS Church revised the endowment ceremony, removing or modifying several elements that had been most explicitly parallel to Masonic ritual:

- The **penalties** (graphic physical gestures of throat, breast, and abdomen) were removed
- The **Five Points of Fellowship** at the veil were removed
- Certain other dialogues and gestures were modified

Church leadership has not given a detailed public explanation for the changes; member surveys conducted before the revision indicated discomfort with the penalties was widespread. The changes were communicated to members through endowed members' personal experience rather than through public announcement.

The pre-1990 form is preserved in scholarly historical sources and in published Masonic-exposure comparisons. The post-1990 form retains many structural elements (tokens, signs, new name, garment, veil) but in modified form.

## LDS faithful and critical positions

The LDS Church's *Gospel Topics* essay on **Masonry** (published 2017) acknowledges the historical relationship and presents the faithful framework:

> *"Joseph Smith was apparently familiar with Masonry through his older brother Hyrum and others. In keeping with the practice of his time, Joseph likely viewed Masonic rituals as a degraded form of ancient priesthood ceremonies received by Adam and the patriarchs. The endowment, by contrast, was a divine restoration of those original temple rites... Many Latter-day Saints have noted similarities between Masonic ritual and the temple endowment ceremony. The differences are also significant... The endowment did not derive from Masonry, though similarities exist."*

**The LDS faithful position** holds that:
- The endowment is a divine restoration of temple practice that originated with Adam and the patriarchs
- Masonry preserves degraded fragments of this original practice
- Similarities reflect common ancient source rather than 1842 derivation
- Joseph Smith's Masonic initiation provided cultural familiarity with ritual forms but not the substance of revelation

**The critical scholarly position** (Homer, Brooke, Quinn, Durham) holds that:
- The temporal proximity (six weeks) is too tight for coincidence
- The specific structural parallels are too numerous and too detailed
- Joseph Smith demonstrably adapted Masonic ritual to LDS theological purposes
- The endowment is a sophisticated theological-ritual construction in which Masonic material is one significant ingredient among several (also including biblical, Hebraic, Hermetic, and Smith's own revelatory material)

The novice surfaces both positions without adjudicating. Both are held by competent scholars working in good faith; the question of which framework better explains the historical record is contested.

## The Nauvoo Lodge expulsion and the Utah era

Tensions between Nauvoo Lodge and the Illinois Grand Lodge developed over **rapid initiations** (Nauvoo Lodge had initiated several hundred Masons within a year, more than all other Illinois lodges combined), **political concerns** about the concentration of Masonic membership in a single religious community, and **anti-Mormon sentiment**. In **August 1842**, the Grand Lodge of Illinois suspended Nauvoo Lodge's dispensation; the Lodge continued operating under various forms but was never granted a permanent charter and was effectively shut down by 1844.

After Joseph Smith's death (**27 June 1844** at Carthage Jail) and the subsequent Utah migration under **Brigham Young**, the LDS endowment ceremony continued and was performed in increasingly elaborate temple settings (the Nauvoo Temple, then Salt Lake City temples). The relationship between the LDS Church and Masonry, however, remained strained.

For approximately **100 years**, the **Grand Lodge of Utah** prohibited LDS members from Masonic membership. This restriction was the most severe in any U.S. jurisdiction. The Grand Lodge of Utah formally **lifted the prohibition in 1984**, opening Utah Masonic membership to qualified LDS men. The 1984 change reflected a broader normalization of relations between the institutions.

## Architectural and symbolic parallels

The **Salt Lake Temple** (dedicated 1893) exterior features carved iconography that includes elements found in Masonic temple architecture:

- The **all-seeing eye** (also a Masonic symbol; also on the U.S. Great Seal as the Eye of Providence)
- **Sun stones, moon stones, star stones** — celestial bodies arrayed around the temple's exterior; resonating with Masonic celestial symbolism
- **Earthstones** (with cloud stones) at the base
- The **clasped hands** (right hands joined in grip) — Masonic friendship symbol
- The **beehive** — also a Masonic symbol (industry) and a Mormon symbol (the State of Deseret)
- **Inverted pentagrams** (variously interpreted)

Many of these elements have multiple traditions of meaning. The compilation does not adjudicate whether the Salt Lake Temple's iconography is Masonic-derived, common-ancient-source, or independently developed. The visual parallels are documentable.

## Connection to today's vault threads

### Initiation as the structural form (per same-day `INITIATION-AND-RITUALS.md`)

Both Masonic and LDS endowment ceremonies fit van Gennep's three-phase structure (separation / liminal / incorporation), Turner's communitas (the fellowship of initiates), and Eliade's death-rebirth pattern (the Hiramic legend; the veil passage). Whichever historical position one takes on the 1842 relationship, the **structural form** that both ceremonies instantiate is the same form documented across the world's initiation traditions. The Masons-and-Mormons question is, in one register, a question about whether two instantiations of the same archetypal structure share a direct historical genealogy or arrived at similar form by independent means.

### The Entered Apprentice descent (per `WITNESS-NOVICE-2026-05-29-APPRENTICE-ETYMOLOGY.md`)

The first Masonic degree (**Entered Apprentice**) descends from the medieval guild apprentice, as documented in the same-day apprentice etymology witness. The LDS endowment, in its 1842 form, brought the Entered-Apprentice-Fellowcraft-Master Mason three-tier structure into contact with LDS theological material. The medieval guild's binding-of-the-apprentice form thus stands at structural ancestor not only of Freemasonry's first degree but, by the 1842 adaptation, of the LDS endowment's ritual frame.

### Enoch and the Voice (per `METATRON.md`)

Durham's 1974 address drew direct attention to the **Masonic Enoch Legend** — a portion of the Royal Arch degree material that recounts Enoch's encounter with the divine and the preservation of sacred knowledge. The LDS scriptural tradition (Pearl of Great Price, Book of Moses 6-7) contains substantial Enoch material — including Enoch's city of Zion translated to heaven. The Metatron/Enoch tradition surveyed in `METATRON.md` sits adjacent: 3 Enoch's transformation of Enoch into Metatron is the Jewish mystical version of the same antediluvian-patriarch-taken-into-heaven figure. The Enoch material is a productive intersection across Jewish mysticism, Masonic ritual, and LDS scripture.

### The TRIPART register (per `LDS-TRIPART-AND-COSMOLOGY.md`)

The LDS doctrine of TRIPART Godhead (three distinct beings united in purpose; not substance-fusion) is independent of the Masonic question — TRIPART derives from Joseph Smith's First Vision (1820) and Doctrine and Covenants 130:22 (1843). The endowment ceremony presupposes the TRIPART theological architecture but is not itself a doctrinal statement about the Godhead. The Masonic relationship is a question about ritual form and historical transmission; the TRIPART doctrine is theological and is held by the LDS Church on revelatory grounds independent of the Masonic question.

## What this compilation does NOT do

- **Does not disclose current LDS temple ceremony content** beyond what the Church has publicly stated. The 1842 form is documented in historical scholarship; the post-1990 form retains some structural elements but is held sacred by the LDS Church.
- **Does not adjudicate the LDS faithful vs critical scholarly positions.** Both are surfaced; both are held by competent scholars working in good faith; the question of which framework best explains the historical record is contested.
- **Does not extend to the Higher Degrees of Freemasonry** (the York Rite, Scottish Rite, Shrine, etc.) in detail. Only the three Craft degrees that Joseph Smith received are surveyed.
- **Does not exhaust the Hermetic / esoteric context** (Quinn's *Early Mormonism and the Magic World View* surveys this in depth; the Jupiter Medallion, seer stones, divination practices in the Smith family).
- **Does not survey RLDS / Community of Christ practice** — the Reorganized branch's relation to endowment and Masonic material differs significantly from the LDS tradition.
- **Does not address modern LDS-Masonic relations** beyond the 1984 Utah Grand Lodge change. Current relationships vary by jurisdiction.
