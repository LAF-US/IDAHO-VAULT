**Nez Perce Flat Twined Bag**  
Plateau region, n.d., acquired 1938.  
L. 13 ¾ in, W. 12 in. Materials unlisted.  
Catalogue number 1938.767.  
Provenance: Denver Art Museum (by purchase), 1938; Grace Nicholson, Pasadena, CA.

#### Despite all the changes in the Plateau in the last two centuries, it is a true testament to the importance of the art form and the sustenance provided by the indigenous weavers who never abandoned their flat twined bag weaving.

Flat twined bags, frequently called cornhusk bags, are an art unique to indigenous groups of the Plateau region of North America. The three bags in this exhibition, all from the Denver Art Museum, are attributed to the Nez Perce peoples (who call themselves Nimi’ipuu), and were each acquired in different decades of the twentieth century. Together they form a narrative of innovation in materials, shape, design, and use, and change in the roles of weavers, but they also offer a story of strong continuance of tradition and cultural practices,.

> ###### “Cornhusk weaving was extremely important to the Nez Perce culture … Women would make baskets, nets and rope among other items that directly impacted the livelihood of the tribe”
> 
> ###### —Nez Perce weaver Jenny Williams (“Weaving the Past to Create a Future.” Char-Koosta News Moscow, Idaho. September 27, 2007.)

Through practicing the art of cornhusk weaving, weavers of the past and present and the bags they create sustain their community by providing nourishment and strength, though how exactly they do that has changed over the past two centuries. This has shifted from access to food in the early nineteenth century, to economic support in the later nineteenth century, to assertion of cultural identity in the twentieth century. The twined bags in this exhibition, from the Denver Art Museum collections, are symbols of continuity in indigenous Plateau culture through centuries of rapid change, and of the important contributions made by women artists.

Bags in the early nineteenth century were used primarily for storage of root, berries, dogbane, grasses, and other subsistence goods. They were valuable for their flexibility and how much they expanded, but also how little space they took up when empty. Bags as storehouses of food goods could literally have made the difference between survival and starvation. These bags were also then adapted as saddle bags for transporting goods seasonally when horses became part of Plateau culture in the early eighteenth century. They were portable storage as well as mobile art. And finally, as the handbag style bag emerged in the later nineteenth century, the bags became a symbol of Plateau identity.

Weavers use the false embroidery twining technique to create patterns and motifs. This is done during the actual weaving, by wrapping a strand of decorative material (cornhusk, yarn) around the outside weft as it crosses over the warp. The later bags of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries are almost always completely covered in false embroidery because they were used more as handbags and the same decorative concerns around practicality for subsistence use were no longer as prevalent. One of the most characteristic features of these bags is the distinction of two different geometric designs on each face of the bag. The weavers were thus creating two different designs at once, and because of this no two bags look alike.

The most common material for the older bags was *Apocynum cannabinum* (also called dogbane). This fiber was used because it was incredibly strong and flexible and the bags took considerable wear and tear in their original use, the gathering of food. With the introduction of corn in the region, cornhusks (*Zea mays*) were used in the false embroidery technique, to work designs into the weaving, and twine was became part of the artform as well, to cut down on preparation time. After the 1850s, aniline dyes were introduced by incoming white settlers in the Plateau and other techniques were adapted for color, such as the use of yarn, wool, and strips of cotton. It is important to note that while elements such as materials and dyes changed during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the techniques have remained the same. Bags are still woven without the use of a loom or any other tools and the typical bag is still seamless.

The first bag (#1938.767) is an example of an earlier, nineteenth -century style, though the date of construction is unknown. It was acquired in 1938 from basketry arts dealer Grace Nicholson in Pasadena, CA. The materials for this bag are unlisted, but on close inspection we can identify the cornhusk of the warp and weft and wool false embroidery that creates the design. This dates it to after 1820s when corn husks began to be used in weaving.

**Nez Perce Flat Twined “Wallet”**  
Plateau region, n.d., acquired 1953.  
L. 14 ¾ in, L. (with handle) 19 ½ in, W. 10 ¾ in. Cotton string base, dyed corn husk false embroidery, wool yarn false embroidery, an animal skin handle.  
Catalogue number 1953.829.  
Provenance: Denver Art Museum (by purchase), 1953; Indian Arts and Crafts Board, Washington, D.C.

The second bag (#1953.829) represents a transitional style, when compared to the early nineteenth century bags. It is elaborated with additional colors and and does not have the drawstring handle of the earlier style, which would have indicated its use for food storage. It was acquired in 1953 from the Indian Arts and Crafts Board in Washington D.C. and is made of a cotton string base, dyed corn husk false embroidery, wool yarn false embroidery, with an animal skin handle.

**Nez Perce Flat Twined Cornhusk Bag**  
Plateau region, 1965, acquired 1965.  
Delia Davis, weaver; Idah \[sic\] Blackeagle, lined the bag.  
L. 11 ¼ in, L. (with handle) 16 ½ in, W. 10 in. Corn husk false embroidery background, yarn false embroidery decoration, tanned and smoked hide handle, print cloth lining.  
Catalogue number 1965.473.  
Provenance: Denver Art Museum (by purchase through Norman Feder), 1965; Idah \[sic\] Blackeagle, Spalding, Idaho.

#### The Plateau has for thousands of years been a nexus of commerce and contact. Due to this constant influx of trade, we see an incredible amount of innovation in Plateau artwork that continues to this day.

The last bag (#1965.473) represents the later twentieth -century type of twined cornhusk handbag, in a very contemporary style. This bag was made and acquired in 1965. It was woven by Delia Davis and lined by Ida Blackeagle, both Nez Perce women who are considered to be twentieth- century revivers of the art form. Bought directly from Ida Blackeagle by the Denver Art Museum, the cornhusk false embroidery background is worked with yarn false embroidery designs. It has a tanned and smoked hide handle and a print cloth lining.

The indigenous peoples of the Plateau recognize the cornhusk bags as an important artwork in their communities, but also consider them more than simply works of art as the bags have little meaning without their contents and their connection to Plateau identity.
