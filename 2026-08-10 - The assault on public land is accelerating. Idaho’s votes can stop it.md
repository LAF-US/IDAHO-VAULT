All elections are important, but this election may be the most important for the future of public lands in Idaho.

And across the U.S., Idaho has one of the highest total areas of public land in the country, at 32.8 million acres, or 70.4% of the state. I am proud to live here and enjoy these lands, but I worry that my two-year-old granddaughter may not have the same opportunities to enjoy it as I do, because of the Trump administration’s consistent threats to public land.

On July 14, President Donald Trump signed two proclamations. One would shrink Bears Ears National Monument by 91%; the other would shrink Grand Staircase–Escalante National Monument by about 90%. These cuts are even more drastic than the reductions made in Trump’s first term. Together, they remove nearly 3 million acres — an area larger than Yellowstone National Park — from federal protection.

What do these proclamations have to do with Idaho? Any challenge to the Antiquities Act of 1906 — an act signed by President Theodore Roosevelt, allowing a president to declare public lands, waters, and cultural sites as national monuments in order to protect objects of historic and scientific interest — should be a concern for all our country’s national monuments.

Trump’s actions are evidence of a more broad-reaching strategy for shrinking national monuments. This policy could threaten Idaho’s Craters of the Moon and Hagerman Fossil Beds.

The strategy of attacking our public land can be traced to Steve Pearce, director of the U.S. Bureau of Land Management (BLM). In 2016 Pearce sponsored a bill to sell off BLM land to private interests, which thankfully failed. Pearce is presently working on an effort to subvert the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969, a law requiring federal agencies to assess environmental effects, consider alternatives, and involve the public before taking actions. Pearce wants to weaken that law to expedite oil and gas sales on public land.

There is also a proposal to open public lands in the Greater Chaco Region in New Mexico to oil and gas extraction. Archaeologists estimate that Chaco contains 12,000 cultural sites, which would be exposed to harmful drilling. The decision would completely disregard the wills of 20 sovereign Pueblo nations, comprising 19 communities that participate in the All Pueblo Council of Governors, the National Congress of American Indians, the Mescalero Apache Tribe and other communities that want to protect Chaco Canyon.

Pearce’s goals also align with those of Secretary of the Interior Doug Burgum, who is targeting national monuments to unleash fossil fuel and mineral production and to limit environmental reviews for public land energy projects. The Trump administration’s Wilderness Stewardship Review targets core backcountry safeguards in America’s most iconic wildernesses within Yellowstone, Glacier and Yosemite national parks. This could potentially open up backcountry havens to industrial exploitation, development and unregulated motorized vehicle use.

Roosevelt was our premier conservationist president, protecting 230 million acres of public land. He created 150 national forests, 51 wildlife refuges, 5 National parks, and 18 national monuments. Sen. Frank Church of Idaho supported the Wilderness Act of 1964 and the Central Idaho Wilderness Act of 1980 which protected 2.3 million acres of backcountry and 125 miles of the Salmon River. It’s time for our current representatives to create similar legacies. It’s not enough for Idaho Gov. Brad Little, U.S. Sen. Jim Risch, U.S. Rep. Russ Fulcher, and U.S. Rep. Mike Simpson to say that they support public lands, while remaining silent on the new threats against them.

Pulitzer Prize-winning writer Wallace Stegner famously called national parks “the best idea we ever had. Absolutely American, absolutely democratic, they reflect us at our best rather than our worst.” Having lived and worked in Yellowstone National Park, I agree with Stegner, and I am very concerned that we have an administration that is threatening that “best idea.”

If we want to pass on the beauty and legacy of public land to our children, grandchildren and future generations, we need to vote for representatives who will act to protect our public lands — not representatives who endorse irreversible, reckless cut, or who choose strategic silence in order to protect their personal political power.

Once public land is privatized or exploited, that public access, wildlife habitat, clean water, and history of place will be gone forever.

Stegner said, “We simply need that wild country available to us,even if we never do more than drive to its edge and look in.”

So many legislators and stewards before us knew the value of creating public land and protecting it. We need to vote for representatives who will keep that commitment, to protect our public lands so we may enjoy them and can pass on that gift to future generations.

*David Gallipoli is a photographer, writer and activist.*
