Patrick Haight Correspondent

He was a high-speed itinerant mural painter who left evidence of his passage on nearly every vertical surface that would stand still long enough for a brush stroke.

It once seemed as though every tavern and restaurant in our area had a Breckenridge wall.

Joseph R. Breckenridge was born in 1894, just as barbed wire and abundant railheads were bringing the cowboy era to a close. He served with the Marine Corps in World War I, and traveled throughout North America as a rodeo performer.

At the age of 34, he settled in Kootenai County to spend his remaining years painting western landscapes.

For a period during the late 1960s, Joe was the artist in residence at Wild Bill’s Frontier Saloon on the old highway between State Line and Post Falls.

Wild Bill’s was a fire marshal’s nightmare of sawdust floors and barn-wood walls covered with cowboy tools and old shootin’ irons. It thrived in the years when Washington bars still closed at midnight on Saturdays.

In a far back corner, surrounded by rectangular pine boards primed for painting, sat a skinny old cowpoke with paint brush in hand.

His was not the face of an effete salon painter, but a mug perfectly comfortable in tough hangouts. It took more than one set of knuckles to send that nose zigzagging chinward.

It’s unclear how he came to painting in saloons, or how his landscapes compared with others, but what “Cowboy Joe” lacked in style, he made up for in speed.

Using four-inch-wide brushes and paint from Sears Roebuck, he could finish a pastoral landscape in the time it took to down a schooner of beer. If you liked, he would use the same tools to duplicate the scene on a matchbook cover.

He once boasted of setting a record on the old television program “You Asked For It,” by finishing a painting in 42 seconds.

His muted landscapes sold for $1 - or $2 if framed in scorched wood - and they displayed a predictable format. A patron might choose between a placid lake reflecting distant mountains, or a rickety bridge spanning a quiet stream. A broken wagon wheel or dilapidated fence rendered an Old West feel to the scene. For animation, he often added a horse or an elk. His horses resembled hammer-headed mules, and his elk were mules with antlers.

A Breckenridge sky was greenish blue; lakes were bluish green. His trees were slender evergreens done in two brush strokes; one gray, one green, and clumps of buckbrush were toned whatever green currently was on sale.

Distraction seemed the chief value of his murals. Whether sawing through a gristly chicken fried steak or wincing down a Lucky Lager, the sight of a Breckenridge on the wall comforted like Grandma’s lullaby.

His paintings never would hang in art galleries, but some people proudly claimed to have five or six Breckenridges hanging on their walls.

With such a prolific output, why is it so difficult to find a surviving Breckenridge?

The one occupying a wall of the Bronco tavern in Dishman was painted over long ago, and the one at Falk’s Lodge at Mount Spokane turned out to be another artist altogether.

An old newspaper photo of a Greenacres Irrigation District meeting in Phil’s Cafe showed a mural in the background that could have been a Breckenridge. But a fire destroyed Phil’s in the late 1970s.

Likewise, the dingy old Breckenridges that once darkened the walls of Don’s Pool Hall in Post Falls were destroyed when the joint was demolished in a fit of civic beautification.

While Breckenridges might have become scarce in Post Falls, stories of his legendary tippling were not. Lifelong Post Falls resident Geraldine Engan, a retired waitress who once purchased a Breckenridge for her son Darrell, recalls occasionally finding the old fellow asleep on the family’s front lawn. Joe was known to paint while holding a brush in one hand and a beer in the other.

But a Breckenridge of an elk-mule beside a rickety bridge has survived the decades at a vacant Spokane Valley restaurant that once was Joe’s Pizza and most recently was known as the Lame Duck.

Joe Rizzuto, the retired former owner, explained that Breckenridge spent a day in 1958 painting the scene for $200 and a case of beer. (Rizzuto’s sister Mary Hill remembers it as more like two hours and $50.)

Breckenridge was “A real character,” Rizzuto said with a chuckle. On that, his sister agreed.

There’s also a surviving Breckenridge mural at the Wagon Wheel tavern in Medical Lake. On the wall beside the big screen TV, hammer-headed horses and broken wagon wheels roamed the scenery.

And at the Saddle Inn at Four Lakes, the Breckenridge winter scene stretches from the back wall all the way to the jukebox. Who would have guessed Joe would travel this far for a beer?

In Cheney, there are three Breckenridges near the front window in Bill’s Tavern.

“I used to know old Joe,” offered Walt Merwin, who was at the bar awaiting a chicken dinner. He began talking of a time when Breckenridge was working on a threshing gang in Rudyard, Mont.

“It was back when Joe had a lot of waterfalls in his paintings, and this rich gal came up in the bar and offered him a sum to paint a wall in her parlor. But she didn’t want him puttin’ any waterfalls in it.

“Well, Joe worked all day on the job, and when he went to get paid, she said she didn’t care for the way he did the painting and offered him half wages, take it or leave it. Everybody thought Joe was crazy for givin’ in, but he just shrugged it off.

“That night when the old gal was gone, Joe sneaked in a window and put waterfalls all over that painting! Got a big kick out of it.”

With few family members remaining, there is little to define Breckenridge’s life from 1928 to 1960, aside from the occasional old news article (he was a shameless self-promoter who treated the truth as a malleable virtue), or the random anecdote.

By his own account, he was born in Miles City, Mont., or Ione, Mich. He was either “a natural artist with nary a lesson in brush stroking,” or a graduate of the Art Institute of Chicago. He claimed to have appeared twice on “You Asked For It,” and to have been featured in a Life magazine article.

He claimed he once sold paintings to Jack Dempsey and to Franklin D. Roosevelt, and loved telling the story of how he awoke one morning, broke and thirsty, to complete a painting in 40 seconds using his brother’s sock for a paint brush.

Joe Breckenridge died in Coeur d’Alene in 1972.
