First, we need to break down the phrase. When someone says, “Always chimp,” what do they mean? First you have “Always.” Whatever this is, you’re supposed to be doing it all the time, or at least a lot. Then you have “chimp,” a reference to the expression “chimp out.” You’re supposed to have an outsized, even seemingly irrational, response to perceived setbacks or obstacles, basically an adult tantrum.

I’d describe “Always chimp” as a political strategy of hyperbole that goes beyond the normal exaggerations you’d expect in politics. Whenever you perceive that something you don’t like has or even might happen, you have a big public blowup in the hope that someone with control over the situation will try to avoid potentially triggering this outsized response in the future. This strategy is more effective if the reaction is seen as disproportionate and unreasonable: Targets don’t know what’s going to set you off so they avoid anything that might potentially do so, including actions that could otherwise seem reasonable or at least undeserving of such a response.

There’s precedent for this kind of approach. Take the “madman theory.” Wikipedia explains:

> The madman theory is a political theory commonly associated with the foreign policy of U.S. president Richard Nixon and his administration, who tried to make the leaders of hostile communist bloc countries think Nixon was irrational and volatile so that they would avoid provoking the U.S. in fear of an unpredictable response.
> 
> The premise of madman theory is that the appearance of irrationality makes otherwise non-credible threats seem credible. For instance, in an era of mutually assured destruction, threats by a rational leader to escalate a dispute may seem suicidal and thus easily dismissible by adversaries. However, suicidal threats may seem credible if the leader is believed to be irrational.

This all makes sense, at least in the way Nixon was applying it.

For starters, Nixon was not actually a madman and was pursuing a deliberate strategy to force negotiations in Vietnam. He was a smart guy with goals that were more or less clear and realistic. Although Nixon created the appearance that he was willing to trigger nuclear war over seemingly minor slights, this was a false appearance. He wasn’t threatening the apocalypse out of theoretical principle, and it wouldn’t have been an acceptable outcome to him under virtually any circumstances.

Nixon was also applying this strategy to international relations, where he was dealing with external parties. If Nixon had created the appearance that he was irrational in internal or domestic politics, the effect would be very different. His colleagues and employees wouldn’t be able to trust him and wouldn’t want to work for him. He wouldn’t be seen as a reliable partner or good actor by allies and potential allies. Voters probably wouldn’t even vote for someone who was always behaving in crazy ways.

In contrast, other countries *had* to deal with Nixon’s seemingly irrational behavior: Nixon was President of the United States, the most powerful country in the world, with the biggest economy and military. Countries can’t just leave the world stage and usually couldn’t afford to just ignore America. This was even more true back then it is today.

As President, Nixon was also the chief executive of the United States. He had the clear and unambiguous authority to speak on behalf of the entire American government. He could control how rational or irrational US foreign policy would be and had final authority on most matters. There was only one Nixon, and when the madman theory was being applied Nixon could dictate exactly how that strategy would look in practice. It wasn’t like every nuclear missile silo launch technician was encouraged to behave like a madman, forcing everyone to constantly ask “Will he or won’t he press the button?”

Basically, Nixon’s strategy seems all well and good, but it starts to break down quickly when you apply it to other contexts. If someone tried to apply the madman theory to their personal relations, for instance, you probably would just decide to not interact with them. If someone applied the madman theory at their job, they’d probably be fired. It’s pretty easy to understand how this behavior could potentially be very destructive. If people don’t have to deal with you, or reach the conclusion that they can’t deal with you, then the approach becomes a lot less effective.

When applied to mass politics, the flaws of encouraging people to always act in irrational and volatile ways should be obvious. I think for the average person, “Always chimp” becomes a call to catastrophize.

Psychology Today [describes](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/catastrophizing?msockid=347d5272528d6f653adc41c853146e5a) catastrophizing or catastrophic thinking as “\[A\] cognitive distortion that prompts people to jump to the worst possible conclusion, usually with very limited information or objective reason to despair. When a situation is upsetting, but not necessarily catastrophic, they still feel like they are in the midst of a crisis.”

I think the connection between “Always chimp” and catastrophizing is pretty clear: “Chimping out” translates to having an outsized and hyperbolic reaction to something and, in order to justify that reaction to yourself and/or others, you’re going to need to make whatever problem you’re reacting to seem more severe than it actually is. Given the self-reinforcing nature of algorithmic recommendations (if you engage with certain content you will begin to see more of it) on social media, catastrophizing is a phenomenon that someone online today might encounter or engage in thousands of times per week.

In my [most recent article](https://www.theconundrumcluster.com/p/fight-back-why-republicans-must-defeat), I outlined a pretty typical exchange on Rightwing twitter: Department of Homeland Security Secretary Mullin gave a standard answer to a question about Temporary Protected Status (TPS), one of many “temporary” programs that had been turned into backdoor permanent status for illegal immigrants, that the Trump administration had just fought and won a lengthy legal battle to end.

Rightwing influencers immediately set about twisting Mullin’s words and misrepresenting the Trump administration’s actual policies. They claimed that the Trump administration hadn’t bothered to end TPS at all (it [ended this week](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/federal-judge-acknowledges-supreme-court-ruling-clearing-path-trump-end-haiti-tps?msockid=347d5272528d6f653adc41c853146e5a)) and pointedly questioned why anyone should vote for Republicans in the upcoming elections given that they had abandoned key promises about mass deportations. Soon the timeline was filled with attacks on Mullin and DHS. This was the response to a clear successthat the Trump administration had spent more than a year fighting for.

Mullin issued a statement clarifying what the government’s policy had always been, that temporary status was only temporary, and the same influencers who created this moment of hysteria with their lies celebrated: “Always chimp.”

I think the political strategy of these influencers is to catastrophize and encourage others to catastrophize constantly as a way to protect and expand their political power.

There is little incentive to tell the truth or accurately represent our situation under this arrangement. In fact, the incentives are to lie and keep lying: If you stop chimping, the logic goes, then you stop having power.

The crisis can never end. In the same way that a mother with Munchausen syndrome by proxy knows that if her child is no longer sick, then she will no longer receive sympathy and attention for adopting a caregiver role, influencers know that if they do not regularly send their audiences into hysteria or despair by framing new developments in exaggerated negative ways, then their influence will decline.

Beyond the real-world electoral implications of acting in this way, which I’ll expand on later, catastrophizing has powerful and well-established psychological impacts on the people who engage in it.

I’m not a doctor, I’m sure I’m going to be a bit clumsy with some of these terms, but I think what I’m describing should be intuitive to anyone who has observed these behaviors in real life.

Catastrophizing is [strongly tied](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10608-016-9763-3) to a variety of serious psychiatric problems: Generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder. Catastrophic thinking is [linked to](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/catastrophizing?msockid=347d5272528d6f653adc41c853146e5a) clinical depression as well. One article on the problem [stated](https://positivepsychology.com/catastrophizing/): “research suggests that catastrophic thinking enhances depressive and anxious symptomatology among adults, teens, and children.”

In other words, catastrophic thinking is tied to mental illness (either causing it or being caused by it, it’s difficult to disentangle these kinds of relationships) and makes the symptoms of mental illness more intense.

Related to catastrophic thinking is repetitive negative thinking, a term for excessive fixation on future worries, past regrets, or unanswerable questions, in other words, the individual thoughts that someone who catastrophizes might indulge.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ii2u!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F018179dc-6a56-4694-b167-6efd56e2ee97_960x720.jpeg)

Repetitive negative thinking is strongly tied to mood and anxiety disorders and also makes it more difficult to deal with them. [A recent study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022395621000789) suggested that those who engage in repetitive negative thinking are seriously impacted by it: Their brains are less able to cope with stress, they feel pain more deeply, they are emotionally dysregulated, they show less mental acuity and adaptability. Repetitive negative thinking has also been shown to be [strongly associated](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12131619/) with cognitive impairment in older adults.

I think the “always chimp” political strategy encourages participants to act as though they have mental problems, which seems to me to be generating actual mental problems in these people, or at least making any existing problems that they have worse. I certainly have encountered many people in rightwing politics who, over the last few years, began to engage in these destructive thought processes and then became unable to stop. Usually, their personality begins to disintegrate afterwards. I cannot tell you how often I’ve seen something strange from a longtime associate and thought “Wow, I guess this guy is just going to be a mentally ill compulsive liar forever now,” and then been proven right.

Beyond the risk of making them stupid and crazy, encouraging someone to interpret new developments in the worst light possible is not a good way to motivate them to become more active in order to solve their problems.

There is a famous [experiment](https://www.simplypsychology.org/learned-helplessness.html) that illustrated the psychological concept of “learned helplessness”: Dogs were forced to stand on an electric shock pad. Some of them could escape the shock by moving to a different section of the pad. Others were shocked no matter where they stood.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!AfuX!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F682b94e8-db29-4377-a68d-4ef2b42f8d28_374x281.gif)

We need to kill whoever come up with this thing

The dogs who were able to escape constantly moved around the pad in the hope of finding an area where they would not be shocked. The dogs who were shocked constantly began to lie down and accept the shock, even after parts of the pad were de-electrified and the dogs could have easily escaped if they had moved a few inches. In essence, the dogs began to over-generalize their lack of control in one situation to all situations.

It’s kind of an intuitive concept; I don’t know why they needed to electrocute those dogs: When people don’t think they can affect the outcome of a situation, they are less inclined to even make an attempt to do so, even if they do actually have the power to affect the outcome.

Learned helplessness is [associated with depression](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4920136/): In several tests on humans that were similar to the dog-shock experiment, being exposed to unsolvable problems caused symptoms of depression in subjects. Already depressed subjects also showed passivity and were more likely to give up when they encountered solvable problems.

There was an [interesting revision](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4920136/) to the “learned helplessness” concept that occurred in 2016. Neuroscientists concluded that passivity was actually the default state in our brains. Human beings naturally learn over time that they have control over their environment: If they do something, they will get some kind of reaction. The brain sends signals that you can control the outcome of an event, which suppress the default passive response. When exposed to repeated uncontrollable events, like the shocks that never stop no matter where you stand, the brain begins to block the control signals, producing the default fear and passivity. What the “learned helplessness” experiments were actually revealing was “unlearned control.” Control, not helplessness, was the main operating mechanism.

I think the main problem of the “always chimp” phenomenon is that by constantly embellishing or inventing problems, you make these problems appear far more difficult to solve than they actually are. You are teaching people that they do not have control of their situation, and this makes them depressed and passive. Often influencers will outright tell their audiences that they have already lost and that nothing they do will alter their predicament.

Worse, they might actually try to achieve it somehow and, in their inevitable failure, damage our position. There are lots of ideas floating around once-fringe rightwing circles that are not particularly realistic if you think about them at all. “Just launch a military coup.” “Unilaterally overturn our legal system when you have narrow legislative and electoral majorities and don’t even have unanimous support from your own side.” People advance these positions without much if any thought as to how these things would look in practice and what the response from the public would be.

Influencers start and end with the theoretical case for these things: If they can make the theoretical argument for it, then it is good, then you should demand it, and if someone doesn’t deliver it then they are bad and must be removed.

Republicans face many challenges in the upcoming elections. Here’s a selection from Shelby Foote’s *The Civil War: A Narrative Vol. III* that I [posted 5 months ago](https://www.theconundrumcluster.com/p/selection-from-shelby-footes-the) that I thought was really relevant to our situation:

> Intent as \[Confederate President Jefferson Davis\] was on gathering and bracing his scattered and diminished armies for the shock of an eastern Armageddon, Davis had the still harder concomitant task of preparing the nation at large for survival after the defeat made probable by the odds. He too was a military realist, in his way, and as such **he knew that, far more important than the loss of any battle—even one on such a promised cataclysmic scale as this—was the possible loss of the will to fight by those behind the lines. There was where wars were ultimately won or lost, and already there were signs that this will, though yet unbroken, was about to crumble. “It is not unwillingness to oppose the enemy,” Governor Magrath informed him from threatened South Carolina, “but a chilling apprehension of the futility of doing so which affects the people.”** Just so: and Davis took as his chief responsibility, as the people’s leader, the task of replacing this chill with the warmth of resolution. Whatever the odds, whatever the losses, he believed that so long as they had that, to anything like the degree that he possessed it, their desperate bid for membership in the family of nations could never be annulled.
> 
> His need to rally the public behind him had never been more acute, but neither had it ever been more stringently opposed by his political adversaries, who saw in the current dilemma a fulfillment of all the woes they had predicted from the outset if Congress continued to let him have his way on such issues as conscription and the periodic suspension of the writ of habeas corpus, in violation of the rights not only of the states but also of individuals. Under the press of circumstance, Davis by now had gone beyond such preconceptions. **“If the Confederacy falls,” he told one congressman in a fruitless effort to bring him over, “there should be written on its tombstone, Died of a Theory.”**
> 
> That might be; still, the hard-line States Righters could not see it. Desist from such wicked practices, they were saying, and volunteers would flock again to the colors in numbers sufficient to fling the invader back across the Mason-Dixon line.

As President Davis was attempting to organize the Confederacy to resist an impending Union offensive in the real world, he faced endless bad faith criticism and obstruction from those supposedly most committed to the idea of secession. The only way to stop the theoretical criticism, they insisted, was to take steps that would guarantee real-world defeat.

These people claimed to be acting on principle, in reality, they were acting to sabotage the war effort. Many of those who worked diligently to close off victory for the South were among the first to try to make a separate peace with their supposed bitter enemies.

In the Russian Civil War, during a critical moment of weakness for the anti-communist Whites, when the entire war effort threatened to topple over and the Bolsheviks were advancing rapidly, a group of Kuban Cossack politicians decided to begin viciously attacking their own side in an attempt to extract concessions over questions of autonomy that would (and did) become meaningless if the Whites lost.

General Wrangel was forced to travel behind the lines to put down the mutiny, which he did easily by hanging the ringleader and restructuring the Kuban government to prevent future outbursts. Despite the quick resolution, the damage this brief internal conflict caused to the morale of Kuban soldiers in the field was devastating and made what was already a bad situation far worse. No one benefitted. Peter Kenez writes in his book *Red Attack, White Defeat: Civil War in South Russia 1919–1920* that:

> The Kuban federalists showed themselves once more for what they were: Provincial men who played in politics, who had no sense of responsibility, who could not evaluate their forces, who could not distinguish the important from the trivial.

We are still dealing with people who play in politics, who have no sense of responsibility, who cannot evaluate their forces, and have made not distinguishing the important from the trivial a political strategy. These are eternal types and eternal behaviors in difficult situations.
