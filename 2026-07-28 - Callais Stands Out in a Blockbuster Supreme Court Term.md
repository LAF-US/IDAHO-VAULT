The Supreme Court tackled its share of election and redistricting cases in its recent term. But, basically, the category came down to one of one.

In the landmark case Louisiana v. Callais, the justices ruled that Louisiana’s 2024 congressional map, which created a second majority-Black district to remedy racial vote dilution, was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander.

During a session at NCSL’s Legislative Summit, Jason Torchinsky, a partner at Holtzman Vogel law firm, and Sam Hirsch, a partner at Jenner & Block, made concise arguments not only about Callais but also about the intricate nature of race-based redistricting.

“Here’s a case that I think is an unmitigated disaster,” Hirsch says. “I think it’s been somewhat misunderstood for a couple reasons. The main holding is about the Voting Rights Act. It’s about a statute. And the majority (of the court) says, ‘We are just reinterpreting it. We are updating our interpretation of specifically Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act as it applies to what are called minority vote dilution claims.’

“Those are the kind of claims that deal with redistricting,” he says. “The dissent says this is effectively the demise, the end, of the Voting Rights Act. I think a lot of the press coverage came a little bit down the middle, understandably, because it’s hard to referee that. But I will make the claim that if anything, even the dissent understated the significance of what this does to the (Voting Rights Act).”

Torchinsky, who represented Louisiana in another case, offered a perspective on the Voting Rights Act over the last 40 years and how it has played out politically.

“In the ’80s and ’90s, there was what we would think of today as a very strange alliance between minority advocates and Republicans who were trying to create more majority-minority districts,” he says. “Because what happened when you created a majority-minority district? Every district around it became more Republican.”

Over time, he says, particularly in the Southeast, African Americans have continued to identify with the Democratic Party at a very high rate, and white voters have continued to move away from the Democratic Party. “In Louisiana, what you have now is essentially a situation where there are very, very few white Democrats except in some of the urban centers like New Orleans and Baton Rouge. Outside of that, there are very few white Democratic voters left in Louisiana.”

So, he says, when advocates talk of wanting another minority-opportunity district in Louisiana, in Georgia, in Alabama, what Republican political strategists say is, “OK, what they really mean is another Democrat district.”

As for state lawmakers, he says, “The Supreme Court basically said, ‘Look, the use of race in government decision-making is extremely limited.’ And if you’ve got these other factors that you can point to that are not essentially intentional discrimination, you can defend almost any map now post-Callais.”
