# Nearby history: Bovill history preserved by dedicated volunteers and hard-earned grants

Joann Jones

Becky and Dallace Kellom sat on the sidelines during Bovill Days last weekend and observed.

"As always, I felt proud of the friends and families participating," said Becky, who during previous Bovill Days helped organize the parade and oversaw community center activities. But this former Bovill mayor from 1992 to 1999, is now lovingly shouldering a new priority - the increasing health care needs of her husband of 59 years.

As Bovill's mayor, she added many volunteer hours to her administrative tasks.

Her entire family, including in-laws, demonstrates the same volunteer spirit.

"Volunteers keep small towns and celebrations alive," Becky said during a recent chat with me over a pizza lunch in Bovill's Elk Saloon. Since 1978, the Bovill Improvement Group, or "BIG," initiated by then Bovill Presbyterian minister Steve Hammond and his wife Nancy, has been a key force for civic revitalization.

BIG's current focus is restoring the Bovill opera house, a popular venue for all kinds of entertainment (except opera) for decades. An anonymous donor's $25,000 gift enabled the replacement of the roof, the Idaho Heritage Trust Foundation provided $8,000 as a matching grant, and fundraising is underway for restoring the exterior, starting with the weathered and rotting clapboard front. Raffles and sales (including huckleberry ice cream, old postcards and newspapers, and birdhouses made from old shingles salvaged from the opera house roof) raised over $600 during Bovill Days. BIG has applied for more funds from the IHTF. Individuals wishing to help out may give tax-deductible gifts, via check, to the Bovill Improvement Group, P.O. Box 324, Bovill, ID 83806.

An Arkansas native, Becky grew into her current role as one of Bovill's historians - along with Karen Eggers and others - in part by reading the late John B. Miller's eloquent history, "The Trees Grew Tall."

"Very local history is not emphasized enough in our schools," Becky said. "Children and adults, both, benefit by knowing stories behind the places they experience every day as residents."

While mayor, Becky used Miller's narratives and other guides like Lillian Ottness's "A Great Good Country" to educate students around town about the lives of settlers and other immigrants to Bovill, including Hugh and Charlotte Bovill who established the first Bovill hotel.

During walk-about field trips with students, Becky also did lively show-and-tell sessions about the opera house, the first two hospitals, the bank building (now the saloon), the two-room jail now relocated in Caroline Park, and former logging camp bunkhouses relocated and repurposed here and there throughout the town.

On these strolls, myriad logging trucks would rumble past, interrupting her history lessons and impressing on her the need for better walkways for the safety of all pedestrians. In the last decade a state grant provided badly needed sidewalks in Bovill and other small towns. As a wheelchair pedestrian myself, I truly appreciated these sidewalks in the private history tour Becky gave me following our lunch.

The town is fortunate to have had some key grants that complement the ongoing efforts of dedicated volunteers. For example, a Horizons grant from the Northwest Area Foundation provided playground equipment in Caroline Park. As mayor, Becky secured a grant for restrooms in East City Park. She teamed with other town leaders to get a U.S. Forest Service grant for funds to build and maintain the Bovill Community Center. The center not only provides a convenient meeting place for diverse groups and events but also offers a shady shelter for people out and about on hot days.

Becky came to Bovill as an 11-year-old in 1947 when her dad, Alfred Brady, left his job in Grand Coulee to take on the dangerous employment of choker setter in the north Idaho woods.

Brady was killed on the job a year later, leaving his wife, the late Susan Smith Brady, to raise Becky and her three siblings Joe, Jack and Mary Jane. Alfred is buried in the Moscow Cemetery.

**Joann C. Jones** is curator emeritus of the Latah County Historical Society.
