The Moyle Loyals had a very bad, no good, awful day.

And they earned every minute of it.

For two years, Lauren Walker, Brandy Paradee, Brian Almon, Brian Lenney, and Tammy Nichols have worked overtime to convince Idaho’s conservative electorate that Speaker Mike Moyle and Senate Pro Tem Kelly Anthon are *based* conservatives.

That narrative unraveled Monday morning.

Last week, the House of Representatives passed two bills aimed at combating illegal immigration. As part of the normal legislative process, the House sent those bills to the Senate for committee hearings.

That’s when everything changed.

When the bills reached the Senate floor for assignment, Senate President Scott Bedke directed them to Sen. Jim Guthrie’s Senate State Affairs Committee.

Guthrie has publicly supported policies such as extending welfare benefits and driver’s licenses to illegal immigrants. Few observers expect those bills to advance out of his committee. More likely, they’ll never see the light of day this session.

Initially, Almon — who has functioned as the chief defender of the Moyle-Anthon camp — blamed Bedke for the committee assignment. Bedke remains an easy target among grassroots conservatives, making him a convenient villain in the story.

But Idaho Public Television’s Logan Finney quickly dismantled that claim, reminding viewers that under Senate rules, committee assignments ultimately fall to the Pro Tem.

That would be Kelly Anthon.

The same Kelly Anthon who has repeatedly been described as “based” by his most vocal online supporters.

Timing makes the episode even more striking.

Just 48 hours earlier, Lauren had shared glowing comments about dining with Anthon at the Kootenai County Republican Central Committee’s annual dinner. “Raddest table in the building,” she told her largely out-of-state audience on X.

How “rad” does that table look now?

It’s also worth noting that candidate filing had just closed. With no primary challenger, Anthon had little political incentive to placate grassroots conservatives who might otherwise have sought to replace him.

None of this should have been surprising. Many observers saw it coming from a distance.

This wasn’t a surprise to those of us who chose not to eat the donuts. Anthon has been a moderate his whole career, and one session didn’t change that.

Beyond that, one need only look at Anthon’s campaign finance records to see the truth. His PAC took $2,500 from a pro-amnesty dairy PAC, and his campaign has taken at least four different donations from the same PAC.

Yet, both Anthon and Moyle gave Lauren, Brandy, Spoon, Almon, and others limited access and token wins (see: Human Rights Commission) to buy support among these so-called influencers.

And the Moyle Loyals bought it. All of it, despite numerous warnings from more seasoned political operators.

Moyle and Anthon are not “based” conservatives. They never have been. A few flattering tweets don’t change that reality. Both men have consistently aligned with institutional interests while positioning themselves for higher office.

Consider the record.

Idaho remains a ruby-red state that still provides government-funded healthcare to single, able-bodied adults who choose not to work full time.

The state continues to fund a free college program championed by IACI and major corporate interests.

Spending remains historically high after years of rapid budget growth. Minor cuts this year do not reverse that trajectory.

The three-legged tax structure remains intact, with working families bearing the consequences.

And the grocery tax persists.

As the leaders of their respective chambers, Moyle and Anthon own those outcomes.

If you believed the branding — if you accepted the narrative that these men were the standard-bearers of grassroots conservatism — you’re not alone. But this episode should serve as a wake-up call.

Political branding is not the same as political performance.

The conservative movement cannot afford to confuse online rhetoric with legislative results. Nor can it afford to reward influencers who amplify division while delivering little in the way of substantive change.

It’s time to refocus on outcomes, not optics — and to hold leaders accountable not for how they are marketed, but for how they govern.
