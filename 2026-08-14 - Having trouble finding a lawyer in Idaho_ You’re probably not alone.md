Does the world need more lawyers?

Not many would make that argument. But does Idaho need more lawyers?

The answer would appear to be “yes.”

A 2024 survey by the American Bar Association found that Idaho had the [second-lowest number of lawyers per capita](https://www.americanbar.org/news/profile-legal-profession/demographics/), at around two per 1,000 residents, ahead of only South Carolina.

“Attorneys sometimes are a little bit tougher to sell on: ‘Oh, we need more attorneys,’ versus like a doctor,” Brian Kane, new dean of the University of Idaho College of Law, said in a phone interview. “But when you think about economic development and how we make our communities vibrant, we need doctors *and* lawyers. Professions become part of the equation for how we develop our communities and make them more robust.”

Making matters worse is that Idaho’s population isn’t spread out very evenly, Kane said.

“Geographically, we are a very large state, but population-wise, we tend to have population centers,” Kane said. “We’re organized into 44 counties, which then make up our prosecutorial units as well as public defense units, and then the judicial districts, and so one of the question marks is, ‘How do we make sure that we’ve got attorneys in all of those spots where they’re absolutely necessary?’”

Some rural areas don’t have a lawyer for miles around. So it’s easier to find a lawyer in Boise than it is in Buhl, easier in Meridian than Melba.

Maureen Braley, executive director of the Idaho State Bar, said Idaho doesn’t necessarily have a lawyer shortage problem as much as it has a lawyer distribution problem.

Idaho has about 7,200 licensed attorneys, but roughly 2,000 live out of state, and some rural counties have no resident lawyers at all, creating acute shortages such as family law and landlord-tenant matters, she said.

“There are counties in Idaho that don’t have a single licensed lawyer living in them, and that’s an issue,” Braley told me in a phone interview.

## Number of lawyers by Idaho judicial district varies

Braley sent me the State Bar’s membership numbers, broken down by judicial district for the past 11 years.

I plugged those numbers into an AI research tool that helped me crunch the numbers.

Just as Kane and Braley speculated, Idaho has a disproportionate number of attorneys in District 4, which represents Ada, Boise, Elmore and Valley counties.

District 4 has 3.95 attorneys per 1,000 residents.

That would put District 4 in 12th place in the country, ahead of Pennsylvania and just behind Colorado.

From there, it’s a steep dropoff:

- **District 2** (Clearwater, Idaho, Latah, Lewis and Nez Perce counties): 1.82 lawyers per 1,000 residents
- **District 1** (Benewah, Bonner, Boundary, Kootenai and Shoshone): 1.58
- **District 6** (Bannock, Bear Lake, Caribou, Franklin, Oneida and Power): 1.31
- **District 7** (Bingham, Bonneville, Butte, Clark, Custer, Fremont, Jefferson, Lemhi, Madison and Teton): 1.23
- **District 5** (Blaine, Camas, Cassia, Gooding, Jerome, Lincoln, Minidoka and Twin Falls): 1.19

By far, the district with the fewest attorneys per capita is District 3, which includes Canyon, Adams, Gem, Owyhee, Payette and Washington counties. That district has only 0.81 attorneys per 1,000 residents.

One of those attorneys is John Shirts, who is also a Republican state legislator from Weiser.

Although he doesn’t have a private practice — he’s a U.S. Air Force reserve lawyer — he knows firsthand the importance of having a local lawyer.

“I know when we were growing up on the farm, our family’s attorney was just such a great resource for so many different things,” Shirts told me in a phone interview. “I think they can have a really outsized impact on their community.”

## The need for qualified lawyers

Most people need a lawyer at some point in their lives, whether it’s a civil or criminal matter, having your will drawn up or having a lawyer look at a business contract.

And taxpayers also have a vested interest in having qualified lawyers: city attorneys, prosecuting attorneys, public defense lawyers and judges.

Kane said he saw the effects of a lawyer shortage firsthand when he was with the Idaho Attorney General’s Office, where he was chief deputy.

“One of the things that we saw was we had counties where it was sometimes hard to find a prosecuting attorney, or if they did find a prosecutor, they would get a part-time prosecutor,” he said. “And then also on the judicial side, there are certain judicial districts where it can sometimes be a little tough to recruit judges, and so it all works together again within the system to make sure it’s fully functioning.”

## Some judicial districts are shrinking

As an illustration of the problem, the Idaho Judicial Council this month extended the deadline for applicants for a district judge opening because [only two candidates applied](https://judicialcouncil.idaho.gov/vacant.htm). State code requires three or four names to be submitted to the governor for consideration.

Pay, for sure, has something to do with it, as I wrote last year about [the need to increase judicial salaries](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/politics-government/state-politics/article297888888.html). But scarcity also plays a part.

Bar membership has grown about 3% per year over the last three years. Statewide practicing growth expanded by 16% from 2015 to 2026, rising from 4,902 active attorneys in 2015 to 5,701 in 2026. That may seem like a lot, but it didn’t keep pace with Idaho’s growth rate of 23% over those same years. And the increase in bar membership has not been spread evenly throughout the state.

While four of Idaho’s seven judicial districts grew, anywhere from 7% to 29%, three judicial districts actually have fewer attorneys today than they had in 2015:

- **District 2** decreased by 2 active attorneys (falling from 212 to 210), representing a 0.94% decline.
- **District 5** decreased by 23 active attorneys (falling from 288 to 265), representing a 7.99% decline.
- **District 6** decreased by 16 active attorneys (falling from 192 to 176), representing an 8.33% decline.

Half of Idaho’s 44 counties have [10 or fewer lawyers](https://www.uidaho.edu/newsroom/heritage-project-rural-lawyers), including government attorneys who do not practice privately, according to a recent article by the University of Idaho.

## So what does that mean going forward?

“To me one of the question marks is how do we encourage folks that there’s work to be had in those communities, and for judicial access, we need folks in those communities,” said Kane, who spoke to me on his third day on the job as the College of Law dean.

One of the tools at Idaho Law is the Idaho Heritage Project Rural Scholarship Fund, which provides financial support for students to obtain summer internships in rural communities throughout Idaho.

And the good news is that the University of Idaho law school has seen an uptick in enrollment, including students who are coming from an Idaho college or university.

“The most likely folks to go out to a rural or suburban community is most likely going to be someone that’s from there,” Kane said. “They grew up there, they’ve got family there, they’ve got roots there, they want to go back and be part of that community, and so to me, the more that we can encourage folks from Idaho to apply and come to Idaho law, the better it is for our whole system.”

I never thought I’d write a column arguing in favor of having more lawyers in the world, but Idaho — particularly Idaho’s rural communities — could use a few more good attorneys.
