Twin Falls is a churchgoing town, and for that, Sunday mornings tend to be a still and quiet time.

But on Aug. 2, a day after a mass shooting at a local restaurant left [three victims dead and seven others wounded,](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/local/crime/article316756042.html) the pews and coffee shops and big-box stores along Blue Lakes Boulevard hummed with the same question, asked in two parts: “Why?” and “Why here?”

The city, around 50,000 people at the heart of a metropolitan area twice that size, continues to wrestle with those questions days later, knowing that answers may not come for many more, if they come at all. The shooter died of a self-inflicted gunshot wound, police said.

The Saturday shooting at In-N-Out Burger marked what several longtime locals speaking to the Idaho Statesman called the most “shocking,” “saddening” moment in Twin Falls’ modern history.

“There are so many questions, and we just don’t know,” said Brent Reinke, a Twin Falls County commissioner and lifelong resident of the Magic Valley. “That’s what makes this so difficult.”

“I’m heartsick,” he added. “You’ve got so many impacted families, for something so completely senseless. It’s like a pebble in a pond — it just ripples and ripples.”

Pastor Chuck Swoboda saw many of his parishioners from the [Twin Falls Reformed Church](https://www.tfrc.org/) twice on Sunday, at a morning service and then later, at a special evening prayer.

“We hear about mass shootings too often, and we don’t think it will ever happen here,” [he said from the pulpit Sunday night.](https://www.facebook.com/tfrc.org/) “And it did, and it feels surreal, and we are shaken.”

Swoboda moved to Twin Falls in 2003. Alongside the COVID-19 pandemic, he said he “can’t think of another event” that hit the community harder. Swoboda gestured to a table in the center of the church. On it, he’d arrayed pens and blank cards. He asked people to write notes of thanks to first responders, “a simple, tangible way” to encourage those who confronted the shooter and cared for the wounded. Later, he’d put the cards in the church’s dedicated first responder room, a quiet place set aside for front-line workers to “decompress” after trauma.

“It did get used yesterday,” Swoboda said.

Cards in hand, the gathered broke in to groups of eight or 10 and prayed.

“Our hope is that in our grief and in our shock, we will be able to move forward,” Swoboda said, “even if we have no idea how.”

Mark Tonge hoped that process could take a small step Tuesday night. Tonge’s sister and brother-in-law were driving their SUV down Blue Lakes when a shot ripped through both windows in the back seat of their car. One window shattered on impact; in the other, the bullet carved a fist-sized hole directly behind the passenger headrest, six inches from where Tonge’s sister sat.

Tonge spent Sunday afternoon with his sister, he told the Statesman. Then, he turned his attention to his city. Tonge worked with volunteers to organize a candlelight vigil on Tuesday night at [Twin Falls City Park](https://www.tfid.org/facilities/facility/details/City-Park-10).

“To every volunteer, donor, business, church and community member who reached out — thank you,” Tonge said in a text message. “Your kindness and willingness to serve have been overwhelming and are a powerful reminder of the compassion that exists throughout the Magic Valley.”

Authorities on Tuesday afternoon [released the names](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/local/crime/article316764750.html) of the three victims who died. The investigation, spearheaded by local police and the FBI, remains ongoing.

Tonge plans to coordinate a larger event and fundraiser — for both the victims and a permanent memorial of some kind — once the investigation concludes.

“We will wait until law enforcement and the FBI have completed their investigation and the families have had the time and privacy they deserve before moving forward,” Tonge wrote.

Pastor David Graham planned to attend the vigil. Three families who attend his [Kingdom Church](https://www.kingdomchurch.life/) near downtown were caught in Saturday’s shooting. Two members were shot, he said.

“Twin Falls is not so big yet that something at this level doesn’t impact every one of us,” Graham said.

Graham has seen trauma before. He worked 15 years as a firefighter, he said. As a pastor, he was part of a team sent into Columbine High School in Colorado to counsel survivors in the wake of a massacre that killed 12 students and a teacher.

Every tragedy is different, he said. So is every person’s pain. Someday, Twin Falls may find its “whys.” But David Graham won’t be the one to offer them.

“Even if answers are given, here’s what it doesn’t do,” he said. “It doesn’t bring closure to families who lost loved ones. And it doesn’t make this any easier.”

On Sunday morning, “people were afraid,” he said. “People were concerned. Some people were angry, and rightfully so.

“But the truth is, we live in faith, not fear.”

In his sermon, Graham spoke directly to the shooting that took place. He opened to Psalm 23: “Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil.” The Bible is full of sayings like that. Scripture, he says, tells readers 365 times not to live in fear.

“There’s one for every day,” he said. That was his message, and that’s what he has seen since.

“The attitude of this community so far — what I’ve heard, what I’ve seen, what I’ve witnessed, that we’re not going to be afraid,” he said.

On Monday, two of the fast-food restaurants in the In-N-Out plaza reopened. Graham and some of this congregation brought ice cream to their staffs — teenagers mostly, some of whom likely worked the weekend shift and [heard the shots and screams next door.](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/local/crime/article316743551.html) He found the places full. People were talking. Some were laughing, others crying. They were living, showing up for one another. When In-N-Out reopens, he already has heard of many who plan to do the same. The thought of that gave him hope.

“We’re not going to live in fear,” Graham said. “We’re going to rally around this. We’re going to get out from under this. And we’re not going to let this change the way we do life.”
