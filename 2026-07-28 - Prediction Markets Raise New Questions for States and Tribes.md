Sports betting forced states to rethink gambling laws over the last decade. Now, prediction markets are testing the boundaries of state authority, tribal sovereignty and federal oversight.

At the center of the debate: When does a financial contract become a bet?

That question was at the core of the session “Behind the Bet: Inside the Prediction Markets” during NCSL’s 2026 Legislative Summit, as panelists urged lawmakers to prepare for a wave of sports‑related contracts that may not fit neatly into existing state or tribal regulatory structures.

## ‘We Are Not the House’

Sara Slane, secretary and head of corporate development at the regulated exchange and prediction market Kalshi, says prediction markets are fundamentally different from sports betting because exchanges match buyers and sellers rather than taking the other side of a wager.

“A swap is a contract where you can buy a yes/no outcome on what you believe will happen in a future event,” she says. “The big distinction on sports betting versus sports contracts is we are not the house.”

Kalshi, she adds, is regulated by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission and charges a transaction fee rather than profiting when users lose.

“All of our trades are transparent, unlike a sportsbook,” she says.

## Tribal Sovereignty at Stake

Michael Hoenig, vice president and associate general counsel for gaming for the Yuhaaviatam of San Manuel Nation in California, pushed back on the idea that the absence of a traditional gambling establishment, or house, means the activity is not gambling. Many forms of gambling, he says, do not require a house at all, such as pari-mutuel horse racing and bingo, where players compete against one another while an operator takes a fee.

For tribes, he adds, the issue is not opposition to prediction markets writ large, as many tribes understand the value of financial contracts used to hedge agricultural or commodity risks. The concern, he says, is sports outcomes—and, potentially, casino-style games—being offered outside the regulatory frameworks states and tribes have negotiated over decades.

“Tribes see this as an existential threat to not just the industry and the revenue that they’re building, but as an existential threat to their ability to function as governments,” he says.

Gaming revenue, Hoenig adds, supports tribal government services, including education, healthcare, housing, clean water and infrastructure. He also says prediction markets could undermine tribal-state compacts, particularly where states have granted tribes exclusivity in exchange for revenue sharing.

Mick Mulvaney, executive director of the Gambling Is Not Investing Coalition, a former South Carolina legislator and member of Congress, says his objection is not to gambling itself but to federal preemption of state choices. If two people bet on a baseball game, he says, most people would call it gambling.

“Changing how you get paid doesn’t change the nature of the underlying transaction,” Mulvaney says. “The underlying transaction is betting.”

He says states have long made their own decisions about gambling, including whether to allow sports betting, how to tax it and what consumer protections to require.

## Consumer Protections and Court Fights

Kalshi’s federal framework, Slane says, offers a uniform set of rules that state sports-betting systems can’t match. She cites anti-money-laundering requirements, deposit limits, timeouts, self-exclusion tools and mental health resources. She also says the exchange bans athletes, referees and others with inside information from trading sports contracts.

“We have one framework, one regulator, that makes it much easier and much more manageable to actually implement those protections for consumers,” she says.

Mulvaney disagrees that federal oversight is enough. The CFTC, he says, is designed to protect markets, not gambling consumers. State sports betting systems typically include age restrictions, helplines, responsible gambling requirements and enforcement mechanisms.

“Every single state that has approved sports betting has set up a way to protect consumers in a way the CFTC simply cannot do,” he says.

The panelists agreed on at least one point: The issue is headed for more litigation, and likely the U.S. Supreme Court. In the meantime, they urged lawmakers to pay close attention to how they respond to the issue.

“My guess is that discussions are happening in your state legislature at some level right now,” Mulvaney says, “even if you don’t know about it.”
