CANYON COUNTY, Idaho — For Morgan Boswell, the pain of losing her mother is a weight she hopes no other family ever has to carry.

"Losing a loved one, like, I wouldn't wish that on anybody," Morgan said. "It literally took away one of the most important people to me. I just don't want it to happen to anybody else."

Her mother, 50-year-old Kristy Boswell, died in February.

Following a months-long investigation, the Canyon County Coroner linked her death to kratom or kratom-like substances. Now, Morgan and her sister, Lauren Lopez, are sharing their mother's story to warn others about a highly potent, unregulated drug class hiding in plain sight.

### Remembering Kristy

To her daughters, Kristy was wonderful, funny, and deeply empathetic.

"She wanted to help everyone all the time," Lauren recalled, flipping through old photos. "She had that 'life of the party' type of personality."

"When I look at older photos, I wish I knew her then. She seemed so cool," Morgan added.

Without her, they say, their lives are noticeably darker.

"It's weird not having her around, not being able to call her," Lauren said. "I know the day after she passed, I had messaged her, fully expecting that she was going to respond."

Kristy had struggled with drug and alcohol addiction for years. But about a month before she passed away, she moved in with her parents in Nampa to get clean and rebuild her life.

She had been feeling unwell and extremely fatigued for a couple of days before she died, her daughters told 7Investigates.

"I think she had passed in her sleep," Lauren said. "They found her just a little too late."

### What is 7-OH?

According to the autopsy report, Kristy died from a severe alveolar hemorrhage with pulmonary edema, meaning severe bleeding and fluid buildup in her lungs.

While she had prescribed antidepressants and Xanax in her system, the autopsy did not list those as contributing factors to her death. Instead, testing revealed her death was due to her use of kratom or kratom-like substances - specifically 7-hydroxymitragynine, commonly known as 7-OH, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

Botanical kratom is a tree native to Southeast Asia that has been used for centuries. However, 7-OH is an alkaloid found in the plant that manufacturers have synthesized and concentrated into highly potent pills, gummies, and liquid shots.

[According to The Coleman Institute](https://www.thecolemaninstitute.com/tci-blog/pseudoindoxyl-pseudo-withdrawal-outpatient-kratom-detox/), mitragynine pseudoindoxyl - also known as Pseudo or MP - is one of the most powerful compounds linked to kratom and is created when 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) is rearranged into an even more potent form.

Doctors and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) warn that 7-OH is a highly addictive, psychoactive substance that behaves like an opioid, carrying similar risks of shallow breathing, dependency, and severe withdrawal.

Kristy's daughters first noticed her using standard powder kratom around late 2020, but she switched to 7-OH in February 2025.

"She would use the powdered form of kratom if she couldn't afford the 7-OHs, but usually it was just the 7-OHs," Morgan said. "She spent a lot of money on it. Some of them were like $50 for just one pack."

### 'A Shell of a Person'

As the dependency took hold, Kristy’s physical health began to fail.

"She would nod off sometimes... she was a shell of a person," Lauren said. "She always sounded like she was out of breath, like she had been running. In between words, she would take deep, inhaling gasps."

When Kristy tried to quit, her daughters say she became incredibly sick.

"She wasn't the same person at all when she started taking the 7-OHs," Lauren said.

Because of how it is marketed, Kristy believed the drug was entirely safe.

"She thought it was natural and it was safe," Lauren explained. "It's marketed to people as something that you can take to get off of the harder, more dangerous drugs... but really, you're just going to a different addiction that can potentially be just as dangerous."

### A Looming Federal Ban

Because kratom products are unregulated by the FDA, they are sold at gas stations, convenience stores, and smoke shops across Idaho with no statewide legal age limit to buy them.

However, federal action is on the horizon. [The Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) recently filed an intent to temporarily classify synthetic kratom and products with high levels of 7-OH, including mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, as Schedule I controlled substances](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-06/pdf/2026-13580.pdf). That would make them completely illegal to possess or sell.

That federal emergency ban is set to take effect on August 5, 2026 and will last for two to three years while safety studies are conducted, according to the DEA.

Dr. Scott Hadland, Chief of Adolescent and Young Adult Medicine at Mass General for Children and Harvard Medical School, said labs are taking the 7-OH compound from natural leaf kratom and synthesizing it to make it extremely potent.

"I see a lot of 7-OH in my own clinic, a lot of young people who have started to use it, and because 7-OH has these opioid properties, they come in to me and they're actually experiencing, in many cases, symptoms of opioid addiction. This includes things like withdrawal from opioids or strong cravings to use opioids, such that they find themselves using 7-OH throughout the day to get by."

In [its letter of intent](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-06/pdf/2026-13580.pdf) to schedule the 7-OH products, the DEA says evidence shows 7-OH products may also contain other potent opioid alkaloids, like mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

As of July, research shows nine states and Washington D.C. banned all forms of kratom, while four others banned 7-OH specifically. Idaho is not on that list.

Until 7-OH is classified as a Schedule I drug, the highly potent products remain readily available to anyone.

"It's just scary that anyone can access it," Lauren said. "We have kids. It freaks me out for the future."

### Failed Legislative Efforts in Idaho

During Idaho's last legislative session, lawmakers wrestled with how to handle the drug.

Police chiefs and some lawmakers pushed for an outright ban on all kratom products, both natural and synthetic, but that effort failed. Other lawmakers attempted banning highly concentrated, synthetic 7-OH products while regulating and keeping natural leaf kratom legal.

Advocates for the natural kratom industry argued that the natural leaf helps thousands of people manage physical and mental pain.

"We have over a decade of testimonials," Chris Deoudes, owner of Happy Hippo, a large Meridian-based kratom manufacturer, told KTVB during the legislative session in March. "So many people are writing in saying it's changed their lives, their marriage, and that they're not bedridden because of natural leaf kratom."

Ultimately, the debates stalled, and no regulations or bans were passed in Idaho.

To Kristy’s daughters, however, the distinction between natural leaf and concentrated extracts is a dangerous loophole.

"It is like gas station heroin," Lauren said. "As long as kratom is around, they're going to be able to make worse stuff with it. I think it just all needs to be gone."

### The Rising Death Toll

According to the Idaho Department of Health and Welfare (IDHW), 96 people have died from kratom-related overdoses since 2021.

While the vast majority of those cases involved other highly lethal substances like fentanyl, methamphetamine, or alcohol, nine overdose deaths during that period listed kratom only.

So far this year, four deaths in Idaho have officially involved kratom alkaloids or 7-OH, according to IDHW.

The Canyon County Coroner’s Office told 7Investigates that deaths involving kratom represent a relatively new and challenging area of investigation, and their toxicology partners are still actively refining their testing methods to detect these compounds.

As local health agencies and toxicologists work to better understand the drug, Kristy's daughters hope their mother's story acts as a vital warning.

"Because of these pills that she was able to access, our lives are never going to be the same," Morgan said. "We lost someone that we can't get back. I just want more people to pay attention to what they're putting into their bodies, and understand that just because it's said to be natural, doesn't mean it's safe."

If you have teens at home, experts say check for unfamiliar gummies or tablets. And if you suspect an overdose, call 911 and use naloxone or narcan.
