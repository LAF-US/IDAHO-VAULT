LACEY, WA – For the past six years, North Thurston Public Schools’ high schools have gathered to honor and educate about the life of Billy Frank Jr. through a showcase of student work inspired by the late Nisqually fishing rights activist and environmentalist.

The Billy Frank Jr. annual life and legacy celebration began in 2019, and aims to bring the community together through student work created by North Thurston Public School’s K-12 students, according to the director of English language arts, social studies and library programs, Jami Roberts.

“I think it gives students a place to share with the community what they’re learning about, especially our Native neighbors and local tribes,” Roberts said. “To bring it out to the community and share it with others, I think it just really motivates them and makes them really excited.”

The legacy of Billy Frank Jr. is strong throughout Native nations across the Pacific Northwest and Indian country as a whole. An integral part of the historic Boldt case, Frank Jr. is a local hero. And yet, some people in his own community have little knowledge of who he is. This celebration aims to change that.

As part of the [Since Time Immemorial](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/student-success/resources-subject-area/john-mccoy-lulilas-time-immemorial-tribal-sovereignty-washington-state) curriculum in Washington State, students are taught Native studies and history. But in 2017, Willie Frank III, Billy Frank Jr.’s son, wanted to tell Native history through Native lenses and include his own community. This led to the development of a curriculum with the North Thurston Public Schools district and a celebration of his father’s legacy.

“Just to see the growth, the interest and the respect for our people now, it wasn’t there before,” Frank III said. “A lot of people, they kind of just wanted to check the box with the tribes and building relationships with Indian communities.”

Held this year at the River Ridge High School, the smell of fresh frybread hung in the air as families packed into the school cafeteria, bouncing between the aisles of students proudly showcasing their projects.

For weeks, students spent time learning about Billy Frank Jr. and his involvement in the [Fish Wars](https://www.underscore.news/land/the-50th-anniversary-of-the-boldt-decision-is-a-celebration-of-native-leadership/), creating art, poems, displays, games and posters to share with the community on March 4.

**Students at work**

Tenth grader Aspen Bermensolo watched people engaging with her artwork, a painting of a glowing salmon in the hands of a human alongside a poem “Journey of the Salmon.” For Bemensolo, the work is meant to convey a deeply personal, symbiotic relationship between salmon and human beings.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Aspen-Bermensolo-3-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1365&ssl=1)

Aspen Bermensolo, a sophomore at North Thurston High School, stands next to the booth her class made through the North Thurston High School’s English Language Learners (ELL) peer mentor class. The booth was filled with artwork, poems and more all dedicated to showing appreciation for Billy Frank Jr. and his lasting legacy. (Photo by Jarrette Werk, Underscore Native News)

“It’s \[the salmon\] glowing to represent that connection and the power that it holds in the environment and in people’s lives,” Bermensolo said.

Frank III came and spoke to her class, inspiring Bermensolo to make changes in her own community, starting with her poem and painting.

“I think of Billy Frank Jr, and I’m like, ‘oh, that was before I was born,’” Bermensolo said. “But seeing Willie was really cool, because he’s doing all of that stuff today. And I think anyone can do that.”

Xavric Smith, a senior at Envision Career Academy, was inspired by last year’s Billy Frank Jr. annual life and legacy celebration and was eager to get involved this year. He and several fellow students worked on a project about honorable harvesting, a practice that includes asking permission, never taking the first plant or last plant one sees, taking only what is needed, minimizing harm, wasting nothing, sharing and ensuring reciprocity.

Smith, who has Guatemalan ancestry, said he noticed a similarity in his culture and Frank Jr.’s in regards to low waste and environmental respect.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Child-looking-at-fish-eggs-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1365&ssl=1)

A young family with two small children stopped at a booth to look at the various stages of fish development; from eggs, to embryos, to larva. (Photo by Jarrette Werk, Underscore Native News)

“I’m really into my Islander culture and learning about other cultures. I was excited to learn about another culture and how they do things. Especially the ways they protect their environments, how they harvest their resources,” he said.

Visitors at Smith’s booth were invited to make gratitude bundles and play a scent-guessing game with sage, sweetgrass and cedar. Watching people take part in his booth was exciting for Smith.

“Last year I came, I enjoyed it and I found a lot of new information about Billy Frank Jr., who, at the time I had no information on,” Smith said. “To me he \[Billy Frank Jr.\] was inclusive whether you’re tribal or not. All should know about this, \[and\] feel included and be treated equally.”

**Sharing Knowledge**

Each year, the celebration travels among the three high schools in the district, according to Roberts, director of English language arts, social studies and library programs, to ensure that all schools feel the celebration is communal. In her time working on organizing the celebration she said honoring Frank Jr.’s legacy is celebrating a hero from her own community.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Willie-Frank-III-2-scaled.jpg?resize=2000%2C3000&ssl=1)

Willie Frank III, is the son of Billy Frank Jr., a Nisqually fishing rights activist of the Fish Wars and an environmentalist. Nearly a decade ago, Frank III and his cousin wanted to give back to the community and tell true Native history, which ultimately led to the development of curriculum with North Thurston public schools and the celebration of his father’s legacy, which first began in 2017. This year marked the 7th year the event was held in-person. (Photo by Jarrette Werk, Underscore Native News)

“In this day and age, it gives us a hero, a figurehead of what we want for our community,” Roberts said. “I grew up in Lacey, and I didn’t know Billy Frank Jr. I just feel like we need to bring those people that are leaders in our community to the forefront, instead of thinking it’s always somebody outside of our community.”

Reflecting on his own school days, Frank III remembers asking his father why schools weren’t teaching students about the Boldt decision. The infamous court case, settled in 1973 by U.S. District Judge George Boldt, ruled in U.S. v. Washington that treaties guaranteed Native nations in Washington state the right to half the fishing harvest. The ruling also ushered in a new era of co-management between sovereign Native governments, city and state governments.

“He’d always say, ‘son we aren’t ready for it,’” Frank III recalled.

But now, Frank III has helped usher in a partnership that has led to a community celebration that brought him to tears.

“They \[the projects\] were just so impactful and so meaningful,” he said. “These inspired young kids never knew my dad. But they were inspired by him.”

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.underscore.news/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/FEATURE-IMAGE-scaled.jpg?resize=2048%2C1365&ssl=1)

A group of children smile as they color worksheets at a booth during the 7th Annual Billy Frank Jr. Day Celebration on March 4, 2026. (Photo by Jarrette Werk, Underscore Native News)

As the celebration continues to grow, Frank III hopes that people continue to admire the work his father has done and also be open to learning about their history.

“We have rich stories and rich history here,” Frank III said. “And so I’m hoping that, as we move on and in life, that more of our tribes are willing to work with our schools and partner with our schools. We’re not pimping out our history or trying to do anything crazy, but the only way that we are going to be able to find balance in the state is if we are able to respect one another.”
