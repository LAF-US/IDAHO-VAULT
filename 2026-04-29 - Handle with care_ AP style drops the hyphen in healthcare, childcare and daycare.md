The Associated Press Stylebook's fusion of healthcare to one word drew the biggest crowd reaction at the ACES: The Society for Editing conference in Atlanta. (Christine Steele/ACES)

The bombshells still explode. No more hyphen in email (Phoenix, 2011). Over is allowed, as a synonym for “more than” (Las Vegas, 2014). Most hyphens are gone after prefixes (San Diego, 2024). Now, the Associated Press Stylebook has again sparked tremors, shaking copy editors, style geeks and writers everywhere.

Atlanta, 2026: Healthcare, now fused into a single word, along with childcare and daycare.

Discuss.

Stylebook editor Anna Jo Bratton announced this, and several other changes, Friday at the ACES: The Society for Editing’s annual conference. On its social media pages, the AP showed a big, red slide announcing the change. The caption read, “You’ve been asking for this one for years. We heard you.”

The online stylebook’s Ask the Editor Forum shows the “-care” question had lingered. In October 2024 and November 2023, for example, posters from Pawcatuck, Connecticut, and Indianapolis asked why the AP insisted on two words when Merriam-Webster, the official dictionary, had one.

“Safe to say that there are as many who prefer the two-word version as who prefer the one-word version,” the AP told Indy.

“We don’t always agree 100% with our primary dictionary,” the AP told Pawcatuck.

But Bratton said her six-person style committee reviewed public sentiment and changed its mind.

“Our decision to make healthcare one word was a long time coming, and I knew people had strong feelings both ways,” Bratton said in a postconference email. “When I announced it at ACES, the gasps and applause (and maybe some groans) underlined just how much people care! We also got a ton of feedback online, and it was the same — lots of excitement, some horror.

“As editors, we like things to stay the same,” she added. “Consistency is our bread and butter. But language evolves, and in this case, it was time.”

Attendees said the news electrified the packed ballroom.

“There was definitely an ‘Oh my God, finally’ vibe,” Washington Times copy editor Rhiannon Root said.

Leslie Poster, the Washington, D.C.-based National Museum of American History’s editorial services director, said, “I’ve been to several AP style sessions where changes have been announced and it’s been a while since I’ve seen such a celebration.”

The exuberance spilled onto LinkedIn. Gabriel Perna, a New York-based health tech storyteller, writer and communicator, [wrote:](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/gabriel-perna-b41b114_thats-one-small-step-for-common-sense-one-share-7454534013576429569-uHTI/) “That’s one small step for common sense, one giant leap for never having to learn another way to write healthcare. Hallelujah!”

Daniel Heuman, founder of London-based Intelligent Editing Ltd., [wrote,](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7453509740732538880/) “Am I the only one who saw this and excitedly burst into the next room to tell my partner that ‘healthcare’ is finally one word?”

And Dave Nelsen, deputy editorial manager for Tipp City, Ohio-based Dragonfly Editorial, [wrote:](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7453441705699192832/) “In my 30 years following AP style, this is all I’ve ever asked for. Thank you, AP.”

But at least one exasperated cry arose on the platform.

Wendy Opsahl, vice president of marketing for Alexandria, Virginia-based Aptiv Resources, [wrote:](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7453539635491938305/) “To my fellow comms professionals who have spent a career inserting a space, removing a space and then inserting the space again depending on the client, the context and whether Mercury was in retrograde, here we go again!?”

The “healthcare” hubbub overshadowed several other changes, which were immediately included in the stylebook’s online edition and will make the print stylebook, set for May 28 release.

- **Native Americans, Indigenous people/peoples received a new entry, separate from the race-related coverage section.** “Racial issues are vital to consider when writing about these identities,” the AP wrote in its ACES slideshow, explaining the move. “But identity as Native American or Indigenous peoples goes beyond race, reaching into topics of sovereignty and self-determination of tribal nations and citizens. This move reflects that broader classification.”
- **The “accident” entry added context for gun incidents.** The AP now says, “Avoid gun accident or phrasing such as ‘a gun went off’ when describing a shooting, except when attributed or in a developing story with incomplete information.
- **Sports terms, such as ping-pong, got updates.** Per the AP, lowercase ping-pong is acceptable as an alternate name for table tennis. (Uppercase Ping-Pong is a trademark.) By Rod Serling-esque coincidence, this update came just as “Marty Supreme” landed on HBO Max.

Current events inspired other style shifts.

New entries:

- **Iran war** to describe the 8-week-old U.S. conflict that started Feb. 28 with the U.S.-Israeli strikes on Iran and Iran’s retaliation.
- **Invasion** to describe Israel’s military actions in southern Lebanon. “Israel has moved thousands of troops across the border into Lebanon, and Israeli forces and Iran-backed Hezbollah militants have been fighting on the ground for at least three weeks,” the AP’s slide wrote.
- The **Taliban** definition had been, “Extremist Islamic movement in Afghanistan.” The definition is now, “The governing entity in Afghanistan since 2021. If referring to actions undertaken by the government of Afghanistan, it is correct to say, ‘Afghanistan entered the negotiations,’ not ‘the Taliban entered the negotiations.’”

Changes:

- **Ceasefire.** The guidance had been cease-fire (n. and adj.) The verb form remains cease fire.
- **Assassination.** “Killing” replaced “murder” in the description for a politically important or prominent person’s killing by surprise attack. This keeps the AP’s guidance to use “murder” only when someone is convicted of murder.

To close, a moment of oddity.

Ever heard of LYBUNT? No, it’s not a fake sacrifice hit in baseball. It’s evidently fundraiser slang to describe donors who gave “last year but unfortunately not this.”

AP advises against using LYBUNT and its cousin, SYBUNT, short for donors who will give “some year but unfortunately not this.” (This change comes as PolitiFact’s spring fund drive is starting. Rod Serling’s ghost must be here somewhere.)

Says AP: “If a source uses the terms to discuss donations, generally avoid a direct quotation and paraphrase instead. If the direct quotation is essential, explain the term or terms.”

Got that?

***Correction (Sunday, May 3, 9:30 p.m.): Leslie Poster works at the National Museum of American History, not the National Museum of Natural History.***
