# County and the Magister/Minister Polarity

> *"The Vaulted Architect has deigned to divide the landscape into Counties for ease of administration."*

A county is, etymologically, **the territory of a count** — and a count is, etymologically, **a companion of the sovereign**. What looks like a cartographic act (dividing the landscape) is the *record of a delegation* (somebody was sent to govern the patch on the sovereign's behalf). This note traces the word back to its root, then maps the offices that hang off it onto the older Latin grammar of *magister* and *minister* — more and less, master and servant — and shows where common-law administration still sits on that polarity today.

---

## Part I — The word's spine

**county** ← Anglo-French *counté* ← Old French *conté / cunté* ← Late Latin ***comitātus*** ("the office, retinue, or jurisdiction of a *comes*") ← Latin ***comes, comitis*** ("companion, attendant"), from ***com-*** ("with") + the root of ***īre*** ("to go") — literally **"one who goes with."**

So a *county* is, at root, **the going-with-ness of a count** — the territory and office of the king's companion. Every county line is a faded property line of a vanished *comitatus*.

### How "companion" became "official"

In the early Roman Republic *comes* was informal — a friend traveling with a magistrate. Under the late Empire (especially Constantine), it hardened into a formal rank: *comites* of the sacred consistory, *comites rei militaris*, *comes Orientis*, *comes sacrarum largitionum*. Intimacy with the sovereign became the source of administrative authority. Closeness was the credential.

When the Western Empire collapsed, the Franks kept the vocabulary. A Merovingian or Carolingian ***comes*** was the king's man placed over a ***comitatus*** — a district of royal administration, justice, and revenue. By the 9th century, the *comitatus* is already what we would recognize as a county: a bounded administrative unit run by a royal officer who held it as a delegation, not as private soil (in theory).

### The Norman import into England

Pre-Conquest England had a parallel architecture in its own vocabulary:

| Anglo-Saxon | Norman/Latin overlay | What survived |
|---|---|---|
| **scīr** ("shire": office, charge, district) | *comitatus* / *county* | Both. "Shire" persists in place-names (Yorkshire); "county" became the legal/administrative term. |
| **eorl** ("earl": man of high rank) | *comes* / *count* | "Earl" kept as the English noble title — but his wife is a *countess*, because there is no native feminine of *eorl*. The title's gender stitched itself to the Latin root. |
| **scīr-gerēfa** ("shire-reeve") | *vicecomes* | "Sheriff" in English; *vicecomes* in Latin records — the king's executive officer in the county. |

After 1066 the Normans did not redraw the shires; they relabeled the office. The land was already organized; what changed was the language of authority. *Vicecomes* (vice-count, the count's deputy in the field) is the Latin behind both **sheriff** and the noble title **viscount**.

Note on *scīr*: the appealing folk-etymology connecting *scīr* to *scieran* ("to shear, to cut") — making the shire "the cut portion" — is contested. The settled Germanic sense is *office, charge, jurisdiction*. The shire is the *administered portion*, whether or not the metaphor of cutting holds.

## Part II — Magister / Minister and the offices that hang from them

### The polarity itself

Latin built the pair deliberately:

- **magister** ← *magis* ("more") + agent suffix — "the more-one"
- **minister** ← *minus* ("less") + agent suffix — "the less-one"

Same word-shape, opposite sides of a comparison. The pair is the *constitutional grammar* of every office below. It does three things at once:

1. **Hierarchy** — who outranks whom.
2. **Function** — who decides, who executes.
3. **Fiduciary** — the *minister* acts in the *magister's* name and owes account.

These rarely separate cleanly. Most real offices sit somewhere along the polarity; some slide from one end to the other over centuries. But the polarity is the constitutional grammar, and almost every term in the bouquet locates itself on it.

#### Cognates of each side

| magister | minister |
|---|---|
| **magistrate** — the office and its holder | **ministry** — the body of ministers |
| **maior / mayor** — "the greater" | **administer / administration** — *ad-* + *ministrare*, "to serve toward" |
| **master, mister, mistress, maestro** | **administrator** — one who serves toward |
| **magisterial** (authoritative; doctrinal) | **ministerial** (executive; non-discretionary) |

The entire vocabulary of "executive government" descends from the *minister* side. "Administration" is, literally, *service-toward*. Whose service, toward what end — that is the question every regime answers differently.

---

### I. Magisterial offices (original authority)

For each: power held, authority claimed, ends served.

#### Sovereign / Crown
- **Power:** fount of jurisdiction; in classical theory, all other authority is delegation from this point.
- **Authority:** God (coronation theology); the people (modern constitutionalism); both, in uneasy overlap (the U.K. still).
- **Ends:** peace, justice, defense, *salus populi*.

#### Magistrate ← *magistratus*
- **Power:** judgment within a delegated scope. In Rome, the higher magistracies held *imperium* (the power to command, including the power of life and death in the field).
- **Authority:** the people, by election (Rome); the Crown, by commission (medieval/early-modern England); statute (modern).
- **Ends:** *deciding*. The magisterial function is judgment; the ministerial function is execution. Justices of the peace, lay magistrates, U.S. federal [[magistrate judges]] and Idaho's [[magistrate court]] all preserve this.

#### Earl / Count (the title, not the office)
- **Etymology:** *eorl* (Anglo-Saxon, "man of high rank") and *comes* (Latin, "companion") collide after 1066. The Latinate title sticks to the English noun.
- **Power:** by Edward I's reign, essentially honorific; the *real* county work had migrated to the sheriff.
- **Authority:** royal creation (letters patent in England).
- **Ends:** status, precedence, Parliamentary peerage (until largely curtailed 1999), lordship of lands where any remain.
- **Note:** the count was *originally* a minister (the king's companion-officer). The title petrified into a magisterial honor as actual administration drained away. This is the most common slope in the whole vocabulary.

#### Count Palatine / *Pfalzgraf*
- **Etymology:** *comes palatinus* — "count of the palace."
- **Power:** quasi-royal within a *palatinate*. Could hold pleas the king would have heard; in England the great palatinates (Durham, Chester, Lancaster) had their own writs, their own chanceries, sometimes their own coinage.
- **Authority:** original royal grant — but a grant so total it functioned as sovereignty within the palatinate's bounds.
- **Ends:** regional governance where central reach was thin (the Welsh and Scottish marches; the Holy Roman frontier).
- **Note:** the limit case of delegation. A minister with a magister's effective reach. Where the polarity nearly collapses.

#### Lord of the Manor
- **Etymology:** Latin *manēre*, "to dwell" — the lord's *staying-place*.
- **Power:** private magisterial within the manor. Held *court baron* (civil, over free tenants) and *court leet* (criminal/police, exercising franchised royal jurisdiction).
- **Authority:** feudal grant from the Crown; later, simple property right.
- **Ends:** collection of dues, regulation of village life, petty justice.
- **Note:** a *feudal pocket* — private magisterium operating inside public magisterium. Extinct as jurisdiction in modern English law; survives as a curious form of incorporeal property right.

#### Mayor ← *maior*, "the greater"
- **Power:** chief executive of a municipal corporation.
- **Authority:** borough charter historically; local-government statute today; popular election in most modern systems.
- **Ends:** local administration, civic representation, sometimes (in U.S. strong-mayor cities) genuine executive command.
- **Note:** began as a *portreeve* — a minister. Climbed into magisterial status as boroughs accumulated corporate powers. The word itself, *the greater*, records the promotion. One of the few cases where a ministerial office walks all the way up the polarity and keeps its name.

### II. Ministerial offices (delegated executive authority)

#### Reeve ← Old English *gerēfa*
- **Power:** stewardship, accountancy, enforcement.
- **Authority:** the master whose reeve he is (king, shire, hundred, port, manor).
- **Ends:** keeping books, gathering renders, holding minor court, raising the unit's men.
- **Note:** the *root shape* of common-law administration. Every compound — *shire-reeve, hundred-reeve, portreeve, tithingman* — is a reeve qualified by jurisdiction. *Reeve* is the bare ministerial form.

#### Sheriff ← *scīr-gerēfa* / *vicecomes*  ([[County Sheriff]])
- **Power:** ministerial in the strict sense — serves writs, arrests, summons juries, keeps the gaol, raises the *posse comitatus*.
- **Authority:** the Crown (historically); the people, via election (in most U.S. states, including Idaho).
- **Ends:** executing judicial process, peace-keeping at the county scale, custody of the gaol ([[county jails]]).
- **Note:** the *paradigm minister* of common law. The Latin *vicecomes* ("vice-count") pins the dependency on the now-vanished count; the office did the work the title walked away from.

#### Viscount
- **Same root as sheriff** (*vice-comes*) — but ascended into the peerage as the 5th rank.
- **Power:** noble title; no administrative office attached.
- **Authority:** royal creation.
- **Ends:** honor and precedence.
- **Note:** the *twin-stem case*. One word; one branch petrified into magisterial honor (viscount), one stayed working as a minister (sheriff). The polarity bifurcated a single root.

#### Constable ← *comes stabulī*, "count of the stable"
- **Power:** depends on altitude.
  - **Lord High Constable** of England / *connétable de France*: great officer of state, the king's chief military deputy (the French office abolished in 1627 by Louis XIII).
  - **Parish constable** (and modern police constable): peace officer, arrest, service of process.
- **Authority:** royal commission at the top; statute or local appointment at the bottom.
- **Ends:** military command (originally), peace-keeping (eventually).
- **Note:** another sliding word. Magisterial at altitude; ministerial at ground level. The horse-master rises into a great office and then descends, leaving the name on the village peace officer.

#### Marshal ← Old High German *marahscalc*, "horse-servant"
- **Same architecture as constable.** A stable functionary ennobled.
- **Power:** ranges across
  - **Earl Marshal** of England (Duke of Norfolk; ceremonial, College of Arms),
  - **Field Marshal** (top military rank),
  - **U.S. Marshal** (federal-court process server; the federal sheriff).
- **Authority:** Crown, statute, or court.
- **Ends:** ceremonial precedence, military rank, judicial-process service.
- **Note:** the U.S. Marshal preserves the *original* ministerial function. The field marshal preserves the *elevation*. Same root.

#### Coroner ← *coronator*, "keeper of the Crown's pleas"  ([[County Coroner]])
- **Power:** ministerial-judicial. Holds inquests, secures Crown property (originally), determines cause and manner of death (today).
- **Authority:** the Crown — the office's entire identity is in the name; today by statute.
- **Ends:** originally fiscal — securing royal revenue from forfeitures, treasure trove, royal fish (whales and sturgeon were the king's), unexplained deaths whose felons would forfeit chattels to the Crown. Today: determining death.
- **Note:** the purest Crown minister by etymology. The office exists because *the Crown has pleas* and someone must keep them at the county level.

#### Hundred-reeve / Hundredor / Hundred Bailiff
- **Power:** ministerial within the hundred — minor courts, frankpledge view.
- **Authority:** Crown, mediated through the sheriff or a franchise-holder.
- **Ends:** subordinate justice, mustering of the hundred.
- **Note:** essentially extinct in England (the Local Government Act 1894 finished what the 19th century began).

#### Tithingman / Borsholder / Headborough
- **Power:** minimal — head of a frankpledge tithing (group of ten households).
- **Authority:** customary, codified Anglo-Saxon through early-modern.
- **Ends:** mutual surety, presentment of offenses at the hundred court.
- **Note:** the *earliest English policing* — collective rather than professional. Vestige only.

#### Portreeve
- **Power:** ministerial — market regulation, dues, minor justice.
- **Authority:** charter or royal grant.
- **Ends:** trade regulation, revenue collection.
- **Note:** superseded by *mayor* in most chartered boroughs. The portreeve is the chrysalis the mayor emerged from.

### III. The collective bodies (frames, not officers)

These hold no power themselves; they are the *spaces and groupings* within which magisters and ministers act.

#### Comitatus / County
- The *going-with-ness* of the count, treated spatially.
- **Authority of the unit:** royal creation (England); statute (U.S. states).
- **Ends:** the spatial frame in which sheriffs serve, juries sit, taxes assess, schools form.

#### Shire ← *scīr* ("office, district")
- Anglo-Saxon counterpart; the *administered portion*.
- **Note:** *shire* names the office; *county* names whose office it is. Two metaphors for the same thing, welded after 1066.

#### Hundred / Wapentake / Ward
- **Hundred:** 100 households, hides, or fighting men — the original count is uncertain enough to have been disputed since at least Stubbs.
- **Wapentake:** Old Norse *vápnatak*, "weapon-taking" — the assembly where assent was registered by brandishing arms. Danelaw vocabulary.
- **Ward:** northern English equivalent.
- **Ends:** petty justice, frankpledge, local levy.

#### Tithing ← *teóþung*, "a tenth"
- A frankpledge group of ten households.
- **Ends:** mutual responsibility for one another's appearance at court.
- **Note:** *collective ministership* — a unit whose entire reason for being is to stand surety for itself.

#### Parish ← Greek *paroikia*, "dwelling beside [the church]"
- **Dual jurisdiction:**
  - **Ecclesiastical:** parson, churchwardens, vestry — under the bishop.
  - **Civil:** poor relief (Elizabethan), registers, ways and means — accreted by Tudor and Stuart statute.
- **Ends:** pastoral care; civil registration; relief of the poor; highway maintenance until centralization.
- **Note:** in Louisiana, *parish* wholly replaces *county* — a French Catholic survival on American soil that preserves the ecclesiastical word for a wholly secular unit.

#### Borough / City ← *burh*, "fortified place"
- **Power:** chartered self-government with its own court, officers, sometimes Parliamentary seats.
- **Authority:** royal charter, later statute.
- **Ends:** trade regulation, civic governance, local justice.
- **Note:** a *magisterial pocket* within a magisterial frame. The Crown grants the borough corporate magisterium that operates separately from the county sheriff's writ within the chartered matters.

#### *Posse comitatus*
- "Force of the county" — the able-bodied men summonable by the sheriff.
- **Authority:** the sheriff's writ, the Crown's behind it.
- **Ends:** enforcement when ordinary process fails; emergency peace-keeping.
- **Modern:** the U.S. **Posse Comitatus Act** (1878) restricts the federal military from civil law enforcement. The Act *names* the doctrine it preserves: the proper minister of domestic peace is the sheriff (with his county's force), not the army. The polarity defended by statute.

### IV. The slipping line

Three patterns recur across the whole bouquet:

**1. Ministers who drift magisterial.**
*Portreeve → mayor. Comes → count. Comes stabuli → Lord High Constable.* When a minister accumulates wealth, retainers, charter, or hereditary tenure, the office becomes magisterial in fact even where the name preserves the older subordination. Feudalism is largely the story of this drift.

**2. Magisterial offices that ossify into ceremony.**
*Earl Marshal. Lord High Constable. Lord Privy Seal.* Real power leaks to other officers and to bureaucracies; the title persists as protocol. Magisterial in name, null in function.

**3. Modern "Ministers" who are magisters in fact.**
The **Prime Minister**, the **Foreign Minister**, the **Minister of Justice** — the most powerful officers of modern parliamentary states. The word preserves the constitutional fiction that they serve the Crown; the practice is that they wield sovereign authority. The fiction is not empty: it locates *accountability* — to Parliament, to the electorate, eventually to the law — by remembering *whose servants they nominally are*. The polarity preserved as a check.

---

### V. The doctrine that survives in modern administrative law

The polarity is not dead vocabulary. It is alive in the law of remedies.

- A **ministerial act** is one the officer *must* perform — required by law, no discretion. A court may compel performance by writ of **mandamus** (literally, "we command").
- A **discretionary act** is one the officer *must judge* — the law requires that judgment be exercised, but does not dictate its outcome. Mandamus may compel that the officer *decide*, but not *what* he decides.

That is the *magister/minister polarity restated as remedies law*. A court may command the minister to do his duty; it may not command the magister to reach a particular judgment.

The same grammar shows up in:

- **Sovereign immunity** — the magister cannot be sued without consent.
- **Qualified immunity** for executive officers — protects ministers acting in good faith on discretionary matters.
- **Non-delegation doctrine** — the magister may not give away the magisterium without limit; what is given must be capable of *ministerial execution*, not new *magisterial choice*.

## Part III — Where this lands in Idaho

Idaho inherits the architecture twice removed: English shire → American colonial county → territorial and state county. The 44 Idaho counties are run by elected boards of [[County Commissioners]] and elected [[County Sheriff|sheriffs]] — the *vicecomes* in his original job, still riding the county on the Crown's (now the people's) behalf. The frame and the title of the executive officer are both about 1,500 years old. The Vaulted Architect was working from inherited drawings.

Other Idaho county officers preserve other links of the chain:

- **[[County Clerk]]** — *clericus*, the literate one, the record-keeper; the *minister of memory* in any jurisdiction that depends on writing.
- **[[County Assessor]]** — *assessor* in Roman law was a legal adviser who *sat beside* a magistrate (literally *ad-sessor*, "one who sits beside"); the modern assessor sits beside the tax roll instead, but the ministerial logic is the same.
- **[[County Treasurer]]** — *thesaurus* ("treasure-store"); the minister who keeps the chest. Pure ministerial office.
- **[[County Coroner]]** — discussed above; the Crown's keeper of pleas at county scale.
- **[[County Commissioners]]** — *commissio*, "a sending together"; persons *sent with* delegated authority. Etymologically, a commissioner is a *minister with a written warrant*. Idaho's boards are magisterial in operation (they make policy) and ministerial in source (their authority is statutory, not original).
- The county itself frames everything: [[county courthouses]] are where magistrates judge, [[county jails]] are where sheriffs keep custody, [[Ada County]] and the other 43 counties are the *units within which the people's magisterium is ministerially executed* at the scale between township and state.

## Field note

The county is the magister's reach extended through ministers. Its name remembers a master (*count*) who, in England at least, has not actually ruled in his county for the better part of a millennium. The frame stays because *the ministers need a frame*, and the frame is a county because that is the word the ministers inherited from the masters they outlived.

Power lies, finally, with the magister. Authority flows by delegation. The ends are whatever the magister has set — and whatever the ministers, as they execute, can be held to account for. The county is one of the oldest places where that triangle of *power / authority / ends* still draws on the ground.

## Sources and confidence

This note is drafted from settled etymological reference. The chain *comes → comitatus → conté → county* is uncontroversial in OED, Lewis & Short, and Niermeyer's *Mediae Latinitatis Lexicon Minus*. The English administrative architecture (shire, hundred, tithing, parish, manor) is drawn from Maitland & Pollock's *The History of English Law Before the Time of Edward I* and standard constitutional histories (Stubbs, Holdsworth).

Two specific hedges worth flagging for any downstream publication:

1. **Shire / scieran.** The folk-etymology connecting *scīr* to *scieran* ("to cut, shear") is appealing but contested. The settled Germanic sense is *office, charge, jurisdiction*; the cutting metaphor is reconstructed, not attested.
2. **Hundred.** Whether the "hundred" originally counted households, hides, or fighting men has been disputed since Stubbs. This note follows the convention of leaving it open.

Anything in this note that would carry a factual claim into print should be re-verified against OED line citations and the relevant Maitland pages before publication.
