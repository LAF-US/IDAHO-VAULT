Thousands of people evacuated from their homes by the Spokane wildfires could potentially remain shut out for weeks, state and local officials said during a news conference Monday morning, as they urged caution and patience as firefighters continue battling hot spots.

Three fires tore through the northern edges of Spokane and surrounding areas over the weekend, forcing more than 60,000 people to evacuate and destroying an estimated 700 structures. No deaths or injuries had been reported as of Monday morning.

Spokane Mayor Lisa Brown said she didn’t know when residents would be allowed to return, with the answer depending on weather and the risk of flames reigniting.

“It could be a week. It could be weeks, depending on where your home or property is,” Brown said. “Please listen to these professionals and what they are doing to keep us all safe.”

Gov. Bob Ferguson said Washington has requested an emergency declaration and direct federal assistance, including search and rescue, debris removal, medical supplies, temporary shelter and generators. While the request has not yet been formally approved, Ferguson said federal officials were already moving resources toward the state so they could be deployed quickly.

“Governments aren’t waiting around for a decision,” Ferguson said.

The Old Trails, Autumn Lane and Fairview fires, collectively known as the Spokane Complex, ignited Saturday under hot, dry and windy conditions. Flames raced into residential neighborhoods, and two of the fires crossed the Spokane River.

Maj. Gen. Gent Welsh, Washington’s adjutant general of the National Guard, said the scale of the devastation became clear when he toured the burned areas Sunday night.

“I can tell you today that that’s a war zone,” Welsh said. “Some of these folks may not recover. Some of those folks may not rebuild in there, and it’s going to continue to be, I would say, a very significant emotional event for this community long term.”

Public Lands Commissioner Dave Upthegrove said 17 major fires were burning in Washington on Monday, with nearly half a million acres burned across the state already this summer.

“This is shaping up to be a historic fire season in Washington state and the Pacific Northwest,” Upthegrove said.

Washington is in its fourth consecutive year of drought, which left grasses, shrubs and trees primed to burn. Before the Spokane fires erupted, the National Weather Service issued Washington’s first “particularly dangerous situation” fire-weather warning, a designation above a standard red flag warning.

Authorities were also working to verify the safety of 14 people they had not been able to contact after reviewing hundreds of emergency calls. Officials stressed that the 14 were not classified as missing and might have evacuated safely but lost phone service or battery power.

Spokane County officials also warned people not to fly drones near the fires. A drone entering restricted airspace can force helicopters and airplanes dropping water or retardant to land.
