## The Socratic Problem

The defining methodological challenge of any work on Socrates: **he wrote nothing**. Everything we know about him comes second-hand, through four ancient sources, each with its own purposes and limits.

> *"There is, and always will be, a 'Socratic problem'. This is inevitable."* — Guthrie 1969, as quoted in SEP

### The four sources

| Source | Dates | Knew Socrates? | What they wrote | Reliability problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Aristophanes** | ±450 — ±386 BCE | Yes, knew him in midlife | *Clouds* (423 BCE) — caricature; later mentions in *Birds* (414), *Frogs* (405) | Comedy; Kenneth Dover argues *Clouds* amalgamates features of multiple 5th-century intellectuals into the Socrates character for satirical purposes |
| **Xenophon** | ±425 — ±386 BCE | Yes, but lived 15 km from Athens; left in 401 BCE before the trial | *Memorabilia*, *Apology*, *Symposium*, *Oeconomicus* | Pastiches; SEP notes several scenes "could not have occurred as presented"; emphasizes practical/utilitarian Socrates over philosophical |
| **Plato** | 424/3 — 347 BCE | Yes; ~25 when Socrates died; likely knew him most of his life | Roughly 35 dialogues featuring Socrates as principal speaker | Most influential source but also the most literary — *"Plato may have shaped the character Socrates… to serve his own purposes, whether philosophical or literary or both"* (SEP) |
| **Aristotle** | 384 — 322 BCE | No (born 15 years after Socrates's death) | Secondhand reports of Socrates's method and ethical focus, in *Metaphysics* and *Nicomachean Ethics* | Reports doctrines without conversational context |

### Scholarly responses

- **Vlastos's analytic-developmental framework** (mid-20th century, dominant for decades): Plato held philosophical doctrines that developed over time; chronology of dialogues reveals which positions are "Socratic" (historical) vs "Platonic" (later additions). Ten theses attributable to Socrates are "incompatible with the corresponding ten theses held by Plato." The "mouthpiece theory": *"Plato puts into the mouth of Socrates only what Plato himself believes at the time he writes each dialogue."*

- **Current consensus (post-mid-1990s, after Cooper)**: less rigid. John Cooper argued *"it is better to relegate thoughts about chronology to the secondary position they deserve and to concentrate on the literary and philosophical content of the works, taken on their own and in relation to the others."* Modern scholarship operates without strict adherence to either the developmentalist or unitarian camp.

- **The deeper problem**: even if we restrict attention to Plato alone, *"one would soon discover Socrates himself defending one position in one Platonic dialogue, its contrary in another"* (SEP).

## The Socratic method

The IEP distinguishes three related approaches:

### Elenchus (ἔλεγχος) — refutation

The cross-examination by which Socrates tested interlocutors' definitions. Typically he asked **"What is *X*?"** ("What is justice?", "What is courage?", "What is piety?"), pressed the answer, and exposed inconsistencies in the interlocutor's commitments. Whether elenchus is purely negative (refutation) or also positive (establishing truths) is contested in scholarship.

### Maieutic (μαιευτική) — midwifery

The metaphor of intellectual midwifery: Socrates helps others give birth to ideas while remaining "barren" himself, just as his mother Phaenarete was a midwife. Source: Plato's *Theaetetus* (150b-151d).

### Dialectic (διαλεκτική) — conversation toward understanding

Short questions and answers, progressing toward understanding through hypothesis and examination. The genre of the Socratic dialogue itself.

### The Delphic Oracle / Chaerephon episode

Per Plato's *Apology*: Socrates's friend Chaerephon went to the Oracle at Delphi and asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The Pythia answered no. Socrates, treating this as a puzzle, went around interviewing those reputed to be wise — politicians, poets, craftsmen — and found each held confident beliefs they could not defend under questioning. He concluded the Oracle meant he was wiser only in that **he alone knew that he did not know**. SEP characterizes Socrates's own claim as *"a preposterous claim in the eyes of his fellow citizens."*

## The Trial of 399 BCE

### Charges (in translation)

> *"Socrates does criminal wrong by not recognizing the gods that the city recognizes, and furthermore by introducing new divinities; and he also does criminal wrong by corrupting the youth."* — IEP, quoting the official indictment preserved by Diogenes Laertius (2.40)

The principal charge was *asebeia* (ἀσέβεια) — impiety / irreverence toward the city's gods — with two specifications:

1. Not believing in the gods the city recognizes
2. Introducing new divinities (specifically, his *daimonion*)

A second charge: corrupting the youth (διαφθείρειν τοὺς νέους).

### Prosecutors

| Prosecutor | Role |
|---|---|
| **Meletus** | Primary accuser; young poet |
| **Anytus** | Democratic leader, politically hostile to Socrates |
| **Lycon** | Orator |

**Political backdrop (IEP):** Direct political charges against Socrates were precluded by the amnesty following the Thirty Tyrants' rule (which Socrates's associate Critias had led). The prosecutors framed in religious terms what was at root a political grievance.

### Procedure

- **Preliminary hearing**: Spring 399 BCE before the king-archon (basileus)
- **Trial date**: Month of Thargelion (late May / early June 399 BCE)
- **Format**: Athenian dikasteric jury (likely 501 jurors)

### Verdict

The jury found Socrates guilty. SEP does not specify the exact margin; traditional reports (from Plato and Diogenes Laertius) give 280 to 220 or similar, but exact figures vary across ancient sources.

### Penalty phase — the counter-proposal sequence

Athenian capital trials had a two-stage structure: after conviction, the prosecution proposed a penalty and the defendant proposed a counter-penalty. The jury then chose between them.

The prosecution proposed **death**.

Socrates's counter-proposals, in order:

1. **The Prytaneum** — Socrates first proposed that he be honored with free meals at the Prytaneum for the rest of his life, as Olympic victors and public benefactors were. He framed this as what he *deserved*, not as a serious counter-penalty. *(Plato, Apology 36d-37a)*
2. **One mina** — A serious but token monetary fine.
3. **Thirty minae** — Socrates raised the fine to thirty minae when his friends (Plato, Crito, Critobulus, Apollodorus) offered to guarantee the larger sum. Per Xenophon (*Oeconomicus* 2.3.4-5), this amounted to "a fine of six times his net worth."

The jury rejected the counter-proposal and imposed death.

### Procedural choices Socrates declined

Per SEP, Socrates declined three citizenship rights that were available to him:

1. To challenge the admissibility of the case
2. To forgo the hearing entirely
3. To accept voluntary exile

He also later declined Crito's offer to bribe the guards and arrange his escape (Plato, *Crito*).

### Execution

- **Delayed** ~30 days after sentence because of the sacred voyage of the Athenian ship to Delos (the city did not perform executions while the ship was away)
- **Date**: June–July 399 BCE
- **Method**: Hemlock (Κώνειον, *Conium maculatum*)
- **Account**: Plato, *Phaedo* — Socrates's final hours, with friends present; he discusses the immortality of the soul; drinks the hemlock without prompting; dies as the paralysis ascends from his feet to his heart
- **Phaedo's witness** (*Phaedo* 58e): Socrates "appeared happy both in manner and words as he died nobly and without fear"; the dialogue's final line calls him *"the best, … the wisest and the most upright"*

## The Daimonion (τὸ δαιμόνιον)

A "rather strange personal phenomenon" Socrates spoke of: an inner voice that warned him against certain actions. Notably:

- **Always negative**: it warned against actions but never positively directed him
- **Came from childhood**: per IEP, *"a sign or voice from his days as a child that accompanied him and forbid him to pursue certain courses of action"*
- **Private**: accessible only to him, not to the city's priests or oracles
- **Religious-political problem**: the *daimonion* was the basis of the indictment's second charge — "introducing new divinities." A private channel to the divine, outside civic religious authority, was the unlicensed innovation Athens could not tolerate.

Primary references: Plato, *Apology* 31c-d, 40a-c; *Phaedrus* 242b-c; Xenophon, *Memorabilia* 1.1.2-5, 4.8.1.

## Key philosophical positions (per IEP)

- **Socratic ignorance** — the only thing Socrates knows is that "whatever it is that he does know is worthless." He distinguishes *simple ignorance* (awareness of one's lack of knowledge) from *double ignorance* (thinking oneself wise while being ignorant); the latter is the more dangerous condition.
- **Care of the soul** — *"the most important good for a human being was the health of the soul"* (cf. *Apology* 29d-30b). Wealth and reputation matter less than spiritual condition.
- **Virtue is knowledge / unity of virtues** — all the virtues (justice, courage, piety, wisdom) are ultimately one; virtue is a form of knowledge. Wrongdoing therefore stems from ignorance: no one knowingly chooses evil.
- **It is better to suffer injustice than to commit it** — committing injustice corrupts one's soul irreparably (cf. *Gorgias* 469bff, 474b). This is the worst possible evil.
- **Ruling as expertise** — like medicine, governance requires knowledge directed toward citizens' benefit, not the ruler's profit.

## Primary sources

**Plato's Socratic dialogues** (presenting Socrates's life, trial, and death):
- ***Apology*** — Socrates's defense speech at the trial
- ***Crito*** — Socrates in prison; Crito's offer of escape; the personified Laws of Athens
- ***Phaedo*** — Socrates's final hours, the immortality of the soul, the death scene
- ***Euthyphro*** — set just before the trial; on the nature of piety
- Plus dozens of other dialogues with Socrates as principal speaker

**Xenophon**:
- ***Memorabilia*** — recollections of Socrates's conversations
- ***Apology*** — Xenophon's account of the trial (different in tone from Plato's)
- ***Symposium*** — Socrates at a drinking party
- ***Oeconomicus*** — Socrates on estate management

**Aristophanes**:
- ***Clouds*** (423 BCE) — the comic caricature; the earliest surviving source on Socrates

**Aristotle** (secondhand):
- ***Metaphysics*** — Aristotle's reports on Socratic doctrine
- ***Nicomachean Ethics*** — references to Socrates on virtue and knowledge

**Late antique biographical compilation**:
- **Diogenes Laertius**, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* (3rd century CE), Book II, ch. 5 — preserves the wording of the indictment and other late-tradition material
