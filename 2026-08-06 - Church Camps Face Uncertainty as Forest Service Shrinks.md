## As Forest Service research stations close, Northwest church camps lose a wildfire safety net

### From Oregon to Washington, faith-run camps that rely on the U.S. Forest Service science, land access and fire response say staff and research cuts could leave their forests — and communities — more vulnerable.

In 2022, Westminster Woods, a church camp east of Pendleton, Oregon, on Interstate-84, consulted [research](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/63618) published by the U.S. Forest Service (USFS) Research Stations and thinned the overstocked forest on its 231-acre property to prepare for the next wildfire. Recreational camps, many of them church camps, are just one of the entities that face uncertainties about what will happen to that kind of research when proposed streamlining takes effect.

In late March, the Forest Service [announced](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/organizational-realignment-factsheet.pdf) that it will close 57 of its 77 stations and streamline the work into one main research center in Fort Collins, Colorado, in a [reorganization](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/press-releases/2026/03/31/usda-prioritizing-common-sense-forest-management-moves-forest-service-headquarters-salt-lake-city). In the Northwest, the locations in Portland, Seattle and Wenatchee would close, leaving La Grande, Corvallis and Olympia research stations in place. Olympia would also remain as a state office along with Salem and Boise, which do not have research stations.

Some [news reports](https://stateline.org/2026/04/17/forest-service-plan-to-close-research-stations-stokes-fear-as-wildfire-season-approaches/) highlight concerns especially during wildfire season, but Dave Powell, a retired silviculturalist from the U.S. Forest Service (USFS) and church member who consults at Westminster Woods, has a more long-term perspective. He can’t fathom the potential loss.

“It’s mind boggling to me when I think about these many decades, if not close to a century, of datasets \[USFS\] has … How is that going to be carried on through?” he said. “The older it gets the more valuable it gets. You can’t manufacture a 75-year dataset out of thin air. That takes real commitment and real allocation of resources.”

Powell turns frequently to the scientific work produced by the research stations for his current role helping to manage the privately-owned forestland at Westminster Woods.

First Presbyterian Church of Pendleton, where Powell has attended since he took a role at Umatilla National Forest in 1990, owns and operates Westminster Woods. In 1940, a church member donated the first plot of land, and the church camp expanded over the years to its current footprint. The property is surrounded on three sides by Emigrant Springs State Park and only about two miles directly away from national forest land.

Powell put the timber sale together for the tree stock to the west of the camp, knowing that extremely thick growth represented wildfire risk. The 2016 Weigh Station Fire — for which users at the camp evacuated — “was a real wake-up call for the church,” said Powell.

By cutting some of the tree stock, Westminster Woods should withstand the next wildfire better than before. “If a fire occurred — not if but when — it would be able to be managed in such a way that it could be maintained as a surface fire instead of an active running crown fire,” said Powell.

After the sale, the church camp used some of the proceeds to plant 4,000 Western Larch, 1,000 Ponderosa Pine and 1,000 Western White Pine seedlings, which are considered better fire-resistant species than the Grand Fir and Lodgepole Pine that was growing. “The church felt better from a stewardship standpoint,” said Powell.

## Camps that lease, not own

Westminster Woods and many recreational camps own wooded properties throughout the Pacific Northwest, which benefit from USFS [research](https://research.fs.usda.gov/fire/fuelsmanagement) that advises on prescribed burns and thinning that help keep the ecosystem healthy and protect camp facilities from wildfires. The USFS produces [resources](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/63618) using its research arm to advise small landowners on improving forest health.

Many camps, however, do not own their own land and instead operate with special permits on forest service land, often owning the dorms, chapels, cafeterias and other buildings.

In Washington, Oregon and Idaho, 70 recreational youth or family camps and retreat centers lease USFS land, according to current records released to FāVS by the agency. More than half are operated by church groups, with others including youth organizations like the Boy Scouts or 4H. Other nonprofits act as brokers for reserving space for weddings, family reunions, outdoor schools or church groups that may not have their own facilities.

Camp Ghormley, named for a former pastor of Grace of Christ Church in Yakima, Washington, leases 42 acres in the Okanagan-Wenatchee National Forest. The USFS provides guidance to manage woodland on site and recently authorized the camp staff to cut several hazardous trees that were either dead or diseased. Beyond the property, USFS does fuels management and controlled burns in the area, said Ghormley Executive Director Joel Whitehead.

In July 2024, the forest service used Camp Ghormley facilities to lead response for the Retreat/Rimrock Fire, which started five miles to the east. The fire sparked late afternoon, giving camp staff three hours to evacuate 140 people.

“We came back a few weeks later. They had sprinklers on every building and hoses. They did fuels management around the camp — brush around every building and just to the east of here … They also bulldozed a fire line and cut down forest along Lost Lake Road — that was their last line of defense, and the fire didn’t jump,” said Whitehead.

The fire grew to 45,000 acres and knocked out power infrastructure leaving Camp Ghormley without electricity for six weeks, while the buildings were all protected by the USFS response. Whitehead is very grateful for the USFS resources and expertise in the situation.

“I do appreciate that they have resources and expertise that we can leverage,” said Whitehead. “The people who work for (USFS) have not only local knowledge of the forest and the terrain and environment that we’re in, but they can also provide us with educational programs, resources and local history about things we are studying.”

Besides USFS expertise in such matters, Whitehead is concerned about whether dwindling staff availability will impact any permitting processes for building projects. For the last three years, Whitehead has pursued USFS and local governing authorities for permission to demolish cabins and a bathhouse that are over 60 years old to build something that will meet today’s needs.

Because of the history of the buildings and the USFS requirements for building in the forest, it’s been difficult to navigate how to get a project done that fits everyone’s priorities.

“Our relationship with the forest service is complicated because we’re at their mercy for our entire lease. We were assigned a new 20-year lease last year. It means nothing if they decide to make it go away,” said Whitehead, though with an 87-year history, he doubts that will happen.

Todd Bartlett, the executive director of OR-ID United Methodist Camp and Retreat Ministries, also speculated that requests for any site changes at its properties — Suttle Lake in Deschutes National Forest and Camp Latagawa in Rogue River-Siskiyou National Forest — could take longer to be approved if staff levels dwindle.

Last year, the Forest Service lost 6,000 staff members or roughly 16% of its national workforce, according to an end-of-year 2025 [report](https://www.oversight.gov/reports/other/us-department-agriculture-staffing-levels) — some of which opted for the buyout resignation program offered by the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE). This year, the reorganization will [move 5,000](https://stateline.org/2026/04/17/forest-service-plan-to-close-research-stations-stokes-fear-as-wildfire-season-approaches/) staff members to new locations around the country. The [USFS has denied](https://www.fs.usda.gov/about-agency/reorganization) that the moves are intended to force people to quit.

Powell, who retired in 2015, returned to Umatilla as a mentor to earlier career silviculturists through 2024, when the program lost its funding. Of the seven professionals he mentored, only four remain with the USFS while another discusses an exit in the near future. “It’s a shame because there is so much expertise and … to watch the brain drain that seems to be occurring is very disheartening,” he said.

While Sawtooth Camp in Idaho is near but not on federal land, the property, which is also a part of the OR-ID Methodist Camp and Retreat Ministries, contracts with the USFS for annual fire fighter training, which was cancelled when President Donald Trump put a hold on all government contracts early last year.

This year it’s back, but the loss of income negatively affected camp operations, said Bartlett. Like Camp Ghormley, Sawtooth Camp facilities have also been used as a base of operations during active fires.

## Roads, trails and a mudslide

Additionally, many recreational groups — Camp Ghormley included — access camp facilities via USFS roads, which have historically taken time to receive upgrades notwithstanding any staff reductions. Recently, the forest service finally resurfaced a road with gravel going into camp, said Whitehead, but it took some locals with political clout to receive attention. The atrophy of the agency also takes its toll on trails.

Holden Village, a Lutheran retreat center near Chelan, Washington, lost access to its facilities over the winter when a [mudslide washed out a USFS road](https://favs.news/holden-village-closure-landslides-chelan-county/) to their location. The camp is [currently closed](https://www.holdenvillage.org/road-to-recovery/) and may not open until 2027.

In the Okanagan-Wenatchee National Forest in Washington state, “Many of the local trails have begun to fall into neglect because of low staffing,” said James Aalgaard, a pastor at Grace Lutheran Church in Wenatchee. “Garbage and toilet services, as well as trail maintenance have been greatly reduced.

“The Forest Service and related fields are a significant employment base for our greater community,” said Aalgaard. With Wenatchee’s research station closing, Aalgard added, “I do not know how many residents are employed, but I feel the presence of the Forest Service in this locale is important for land management and wilderness conservation.”

Aalgaard’s concerns are also pastoral. He said he’s noticed how government shutdowns have left people who work in environment and forest sciences with tight budgets. As a result of DOGE actions last year, there were layoffs and resignations. For those who are left, “This means fewer people to accomplish the same goals and tasks, so that adds stress,” he said.

Overall, Powell is concerned about the short-sightedness of these decisions, and he described how his faith informs his future-oriented perspective. On the one hand, First Presbyterian Church was established in Pendleton in 1885 and holds its services in a building built in 1935. Westminster Woods preserves Oregon Trail wagon ruts and grave sites.

“It’s not just biblically-based but from a religious standpoint, we tend to look at time a little bit differently. There’s more patience involved. The Lord works within his own timeframe. We need to be patient, wait, listen,” he said. “I always thought that was a good fit for how we looked at things with the Forest Service.”

Forestry by its very nature is a long-term endeavor, he said. Conifer forests live for several centuries. When humans steward something that lasts far longer than their lifetimes, Powell added, “we can’t help but end up with a very long-term perspective. From a faith standpoint, that’s well aligned with the approach we should be taking up there.”
