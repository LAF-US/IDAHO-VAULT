The Idaho State Historic Preservation Office has a lot of job openings right now.

Out of 11 staff positions, [seven are vacant](https://history.idaho.gov/contact/#administration), and six people have quit over the past few months, ever since the Idaho Legislature moved the office out from under the Idaho State Historical Society and over to the newly formed [Office of Species, Minerals and Energy Coordination](https://oemr.idaho.gov/).

I first wrote about [concerns over the move](https://www.idahostatesman.com/opinion/from-the-opinion-editor/article315159641.html) back in March, when lawmakers were debating the issue.

At the time, employees and historians voiced concerns that OSMEC’s mission to advance minerals and energy interests conflicts with the preservation office’s regulatory role of protecting historic and archaeological resources.

It looks like those concerns have led to a wave of departures.

Over two weeks in June alone, the State Historic Preservation Office bid farewell to the outreach historian; the compliance manager, who was also serving as the compliance architectural historian; a compliance architect; and the federal tax incentive coordinator.

Cally Younger, director of the Office of Species, Minerals and Energy Coordination, acknowledges the exodus has to do with the transfer.

“I think so,” she told me in a phone interview this week. “My assumption is that once there started to be some uncertainty, they probably started looking (for other jobs) and then those jobs fell into place when they did.”

She said the departing employees have expressed their concerns about the move.

“I would say some of them did,” she said. “I think a lot of it was just, ‘I don’t know what’s going to happen, so I want to see what’s out there,’ and it was more about the uncertainty of the transition.”

But, Younger said, despite fears to the contrary, she plans to fill every position and continue operating SHPO the way it always has been run.

“Something I’ve told the staff over and over again is that the SHPO office has a very specific role under the National Historic Preservation Act, and the SHPO staff is predominantly funded by a grant,” she said. “There are obligations that we have to meet there, and so it’s my commitment — it’s always been my commitment — to prioritize that and fulfill the obligations of the NHPA.”

## Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act

One of the main functions of the State Historic Preservation Office is to oversee what’s called Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, which requires federal agencies to evaluate how new construction projects would affect historic places.

A lot of the work that went on at SHPO had to do with Section 106 administration. Younger said there are 12 Section 106 agreements active, with several more in the works.

“We get new projects on requests for review every single day,” she said. “It’s pretty significant.”

I asked her about the fear that SHPO staffers would be pressured to green-light projects, historic significance be damned.

“There are very specific processes under Section 106 that have to be met for a federal agency to make a decision, and so it’s always been my commitment that those steps are followed,” she said, noting that she’s a lawyer and worked for eight years at the Department of the Interior. “The importance is to have durable decisions that withstand legal scrutiny, and so to me, green-lighting a decision does not make them durable and does not make them withstand legal scrutiny.”

She also sees the National Historic Preservation Act as a way to protect state rights from the federal government.

“The NHPA provides a real, concrete opportunity for the state to hold the federal government accountable, and really what this office is doing is ensuring that they comply with their own laws and that the state is engaged in those decisions in a meaningful way,” she said.

## Trump wants to loosen Section 106 rules

So I asked her about recent developments at the federal Advisory Council on Historic Preservation, which is now stacked with President Donald Trump’s appointees, including Idaho’s own Michael Boren, who is a U.S. Department of Agriculture undersecretary overseeing the U.S. Forest Service.

The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation is proposing significant revisions to Section 106 by letting federal agencies build their own projects, or grant approval for construction by private developers, without public input, including from states and tribes, according to a [proposed rule and an accompanying document](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/07/20/critics-warn-trump-plan-would-narrow-public-role-historic-reviews/) obtained by The Washington Post.

During a recent meeting of the Advisory Council, Boren said that some efforts to replace unsafe, decades-old infrastructure at ski areas that are on national land had been stymied by the regulations.

“When they’re over 50 years old, they’re historic structures, and when we’re trying to replace them, we sometimes are delayed for years just by the historic piece,” [Boren said](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=20DwFMi9viE&t=1847s).

Younger said she doesn’t support the proposed changes and assured me they were coincidental to the changes going on at the state level.

“Seeing those proposed regulations is, to us, a huge impact,” she said. “I don’t want to say states’ rights, but it’s a privilege that we’ve enjoyed since the inception of NHPA and a really meaningful opportunity for the state to engage. So trying to scale back that type of engagement is concerning to us, and I think across the board, regardless of who’s in charge in states. I think it’s kind of a bipartisan concern.”

## Ensuring historic preservation isn’t forgotten

As for the move of SHPO to the Office of Species, Minerals and Energy Coordination, Younger put some of my fears to rest.

She pointed out that any project that involves the federal government, including federal funding, has to go through a number of federal approvals — including those associated with the National Environmental Policy Act, Clean Water Act, Clean Air Act and the National Historic Preservation Act — as well as all the state requirements.

She said that sometimes NHPA compliance gets forgotten about, and having a one-stop office managing all the approval requirements provides more certainty.

“Rather than doing it piecemeal, going from agency to agency, it’s more about how do we make sure that folks that want to do business here have a full picture of what’s required to do that,” she said.

## What about the historic Assay Office?

Less reassuring was her answer about possibly my favorite building in Boise — the historic [Assay Office](https://www.idahostatesman.com/opinion/from-the-opinion-editor/article288291170.html) at 210 W. Main St., which houses the State Historic Preservation Office.

Younger was actually working out of the office when we did our interview. She noted that OSMEC now has 30 employees spread across two buildings in Boise.

“We’re continuing to have discussions about who works where, how do we make sure that we’re meeting the conditions of the grant, recognizing the significance of this building and its history, and that it’s always been the SHPO office,” she said. “Those are all things that we think about when we think about the next few years … those are ongoing conversations.”

In the meantime, Younger said it’s “very difficult” finding the specialized employees needed for the Historic Preservation Office. She said she is prioritizing the compliance branch right now because they handle most of the Section 106 duties that have deadlines to meet.

“I anticipate this will be a national search,” she said. “We are working through the HR process to get these jobs posted as quickly as we can. We are connecting with our contacts and entities that know this field and trying to get the word out that we have lots of vacancies.”
