As a family physician and a mother raising children in Idaho, I spend much of my day talking with families about health decisions. Few topics prompt as many thoughtful questions as childhood vaccination. While opinions about vaccines may differ, one principle should unite us: families deserve the ability to make informed health care decisions supported by reliable information, trusted medical guidance, and systems that make those choices possible.

Across the country, and here in Idaho, policymakers continue to [debate legislation](https://www.idahostatesman.com/opinion/from-the-opinion-editor/article314983502.html) affecting how vaccines are [delivered](https://www.idahostatesman.com/opinion/editorials/article303277536.html), [documented](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2021/04/09/idaho-gop-leadership-accuses-state-of-unlawfully-using-vaccine-record-system/) and [monitored](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2024/02/05/idaho-bill-that-would-require-children-to-opt-in-to-vaccine-registry-heads-to-house-floor/). These discussions often center on protecting individual freedom. Personal choice is an important value, but it is equally important to recognize that meaningful choice depends on having access to healthcare, accurate records and practical tools that help families follow through on the decisions they make.

One of those tools is Idaho’s [Immunization Reminder Information System](https://iris.dhw.idaho.gov/IRIS/portalHeader.do;jsessionid=Z-LYinxQHD2AZr9j29R3uopQasu8EeQyZQGFv6oF.master:ir-server), or IRIS, the state’s secure and confidential vaccine registry. For physicians like me, IRIS is not an abstract government program; it is part of everyday patient care. When families move, change physicians, or misplace immunization records, IRIS allows clinicians to verify vaccine histories, avoid unnecessary repeat vaccinations and identify immunizations that may have been missed. It also helps clinics send reminders when vaccines are due, making it easier for families who choose vaccination to stay on schedule.

Policies that weaken or eliminate systems like IRIS risk creating unintended barriers for families. Fragmented records, lost documentation and reduced coordination between healthcare providers can make it more difficult for parents to access the care they want for their children. Preserving these systems is not about limiting choice; it is about ensuring that families who choose vaccination have the support they need to act on that decision.

Vaccines remain among the most thoroughly studied medical interventions in modern medicine. Decades of research involving millions of children have consistently shown that routine childhood immunizations are far safer than the diseases they prevent. Before widespread vaccination, illnesses such as measles, polio and [whooping cough](https://www.idahostatesman.com/opinion/from-the-opinion-editor/article288152285.html) caused significant disability and death in children. Until recently in Idaho, successful immunization programs have resulted in most physicians rarely encountering these diseases in clinical practice.

I understand why parents have questions. Medical science evolves, and recommendations occasionally change as new evidence becomes available. That process of continuous review is one of the strengths of modern medicine, not a weakness. Vaccine safety is monitored continuously through multiple independent systems, and recommendations are updated when the evidence supports doing so.

The healthcare professionals who care for children every day continue to support the recommended childhood immunization schedule. Organizations including the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Academy of Family Physicians carefully evaluate the available scientific evidence and consistently conclude that routine vaccination remains one of the safest and most effective ways to protect children from preventable diseases.

History offers important perspective. Among the advances that have contributed most to longer, healthier lives are clean water, improved sanitation, antibiotics, safer childbirth and vaccines. Immunization has prevented countless illnesses, hospitalizations, and deaths while reducing the burden of infectious disease on families and communities.

As both a physician and a parent, I strongly believe in individual freedom. Parents should have the opportunity to make informed decisions about their children’s healthcare. But freedom is meaningful only when families have access to qualified healthcare professionals, reliable medical information, and the practical systems that support the choices they make.

When legislation removes or weakens the infrastructure that supports vaccination, whether through recordkeeping, care coordination, or access to services, it can unintentionally make it harder for families who want to vaccinate to do so. Protecting informed choice means protecting the systems that allow every family to access healthcare according to their own decisions.

Idaho families deserve thoughtful public policy grounded in evidence, practical experience, and respect for both individual decision-making and public health. These conversations should include parents, physicians, nurses, and other healthcare professionals who work with these systems every day and understand how they affect patient care.

Most importantly, healthcare decisions should begin where they matter most: in conversations between families and their healthcare providers. Legislatures can shape policy, but the best medical decisions are still made in exam rooms, where parents ask questions, physicians listen, and together we pursue the same goal: healthy children, informed families, and strong communities.

*Crystal L. Pyrak MD, is a family physician in Coeur d’Alene, Idaho.*
