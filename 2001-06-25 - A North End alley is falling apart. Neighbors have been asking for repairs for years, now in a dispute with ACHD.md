BOISE, Idaho — Kelly Paananen and her family have called Boise's oldest neighborhood, the North End, home for nearly 15 years.

"It's a fabulous neighborhood, we love it here," she said. "It's beautiful."

But now Paananen and her neighbors find themselves in a dispute with the Ada County Highway District over the paved alley behind their homes. Which is a strip of crumbling asphalt off Harrison Boulevard that serves 16 homes, provides garage access and is where their trash is picked up. It's also a path neighbors use to walk their dogs and let their kids ride bikes.

"You'll see some of the holes here are huge," Paananen told KTVB. "It's just worsening over time."

She said the pavement is falling apart, and that some neighbors have resorted to filling potholes with their own bags of gravel. She has been asking ACHD to fix it for the last two years.

"I'm afraid someone's going to get hurt out here," she said.

### A paved alley with no paper trail

ACHD told KTVB 96 of the North End's 216 alleys are paved, most of them by private dollars during development, while the rest are gravel. But how this particular alley came to be paved is a mystery.

Tom Otte, ACHD's deputy director of operations, said the district's records don't go back far enough for this alley specifically. The leading theory, he said, is that a resident or group of residents paid to pave the alley themselves roughly two decades ago.

"You'll see that the alley... it's more crowned than it would be graded to our normal spec," Otte said, noting the uneven surface when KTVB met him for an interview in the alley.

Even so, Otte acknowledges it is an ACHD right-of-way alley, that the district is responsible for maintaining it, and that ACHD tries to address problems as soon as they're reported. That maintenance, he said, includes "pothole filling, alley regrading, things of that nature."

Paananen said that's not how it has felt.

"The last few inquiries that I've sent have fallen on deaf ears," she said.

When asked about not answering reports Otte said, "We really rely on the commuting public to let us know, given that we have over 5,000 lane miles to maintain. Our teams just can't be everywhere all at once, so when we get an issue reported, then we want to go out and fix that as quickly as possible."

### A records request and a second neighbor

Frustrated by what she describes as a lack of response, and confusion over who is responsible for repairs on the alley, Paananen filed a public records request in April, hoping to find any maintenance plan or history on the alley. In the documents, she found a neighbor a few doors down, Andrew Bello, who had been fighting the same fight for about eight years.

"The city of Boise and ACHD, it's time for somebody to take responsibility and get this done," Bello told KTVB.

Bello said his calls and trouble trace back to sewer work done in the alley about a decade ago. When we asked Otte about the sewer work he said it wasn't ACHD, but possibly a contractor for the city of Boise. He said, "They're required to return the road to its original condition. So, if that wasn't done and since it's not an alley paved by ACHD, we wouldn't be able to repair it to spec without ripping out the entire alley."

Otte said the contractor would've needed to be contacted for those specific repairs.

KTVB contacted the city's public works department about the work and a spokesperson for the department said:

*"The City of Boise completed a sewer rehab project in this alley in 2016 to replace a failing pipe. There has not been construction on the sewer line in this alley since the 2016 project.*

*The city used a rehabilitation method that was minimally intrusive for the construction. This required only patches of surface restoration, instead of repaving the entire alley. The patches must match the previous condition of the roadway as required by Ada County Highway District and must satisfy ACHD's conditions after completion of the project."*

The spokesperson added that this was the first time the city had heard about the concerns. When asked if there is anything the city could do now to get it repaired, the spokesperson said: *"Not directly, as ACHD is responsible for the repair and maintenance of the alley."*

KTVB went back to ACHD to ask if they deemed the alley's conditions appropriate after the competition of the project. A spokesperson for ACHD said she is waiting on a response from the team that would have approved the work, and will get back to us when she knows the answer.

The ACHD person added: *"We can at least say that seeing the patch in person, it did not raise any red flags indicating it was done improperly or is in any way contributing to the issues in the alleyway."*

Bello said that ever since the sewer work, which he claims contributed to the several different elevations in the alley, water has run into his garage.

"My garage is constantly wet from the rain," he said. "It takes sometimes months before it dries out, and it rains again, and we get water back there again."

While Bello said ACHD has done some patchwork over the years, he said he has struggled to get the district to make lasting repairs, with crews at times telling him the time of year was bad for this type of work needed to repair areas, or the asphalt plants were closed. Bello said he has already spent about $2,000 re-cementing his garage floor.

"I'm probably concerned that at some point in time I'm going to have to make a major repair to that garage to keep it from falling down," he said.

### What the records showed

Paananen's records also showed that ACHD's own crews had walked the alley in October after Bello raised concerns about the state of the alley. Crews in their report said, "Took a look at this alley and it is in very rough shape," and "Essentially the whole alley could be torn out and repaved."

Otte agreed with those conditions.

"There you've got really thin pavement, you can see that's less than an inch of pavement thickness," Otte said during a walk-through of the alley.

The internal emails obtained through the request show ACHD staff working through who should bear responsibility for the alley. In one October 8 2025, message, a pavement management engineer laid out two options for the agency: "Make this issue the Residents' issue," or "Make this issue an ACHD issue." He noted the first path "may lead to some customer service issues, but it also may set precedence," and asked whether executives wanted to make a "business decision" before the agency opened what he called a "legal can of worms."

If there were an incident to happen, like someone getting hurt in the alley, KTVB asked ACHD if the district would be liable. Otte didn't answer specifically, but said if an alley is dangerous, they'd close it and address the issue. KTVB followed up and asked if this alley is to that point, to which Otte said, "Bad road condition isn't dangerous, it just means that the asphalt is kind of crumbly, as you can see kind of around in spots it's broken up, you can see what the subgrade is underneath, but it's certainly not dangerous to travel on."

KTVB questioned ACHD about the years of delays, calls, and reports from the neighbors.

"I'm very sorry it took this long," he said. "Had I known about it sooner, I would have gotten on it sooner, but now that I know about it, I'm going to make sure they're taken care of to the best of our ability to do as a highway district," Otte said.

Otte, who has been with the district less than two years, said he can only speak on the last several months and that he was working to find a solution since he found out about the alley in December.

"I'm not saying they didn't raise issues before, but I don't have any record of any issues until last December. So, I don't really have any. I don't - I'm not trying to pass the buck, but as soon as I got involved, we made sure that we were able to address those concerns. So, once I can, once we know about something, and I can tell you very sincerely, once I knew about this, we got on it to give the residents the best options we could," Otte said.

An ACHD spokesperson added: "In October, after the ticket was submitted, one of our crew chiefs went out to the alleyway to take a preliminary look. After that, the crew chief began working with our maintenance, planning, and legal teams. They spent the next few months trying to determine the history of this alleyway. It was escalated in December to Tom Otte (who oversees the crew chief as the Deputy Director of Operations)."

As the North End's other paved alleys continue to age, Paananen and Bello are calling for a clearer maintenance plan and possibly a new program to identify these issues. Otte said it is evaluating exactly that and other programs since they got a new director about two years ago.

"We're doing a lot of re-analysis to make sure that we're doing the right way to handle alleys, given how many alleys there are," Otte said.

### Who pays

Ultimately, ACHD told the neighbors they would have to foot the bill for repaving. Residents contacted KTVB in May about what they were told.

"We pay enough for taxes here," Bello said in reaction to having to pay for the paving. "I mean, we're up to six grand a year."

Otte said it is ACHD policy that the district does not pave alleys.

"There's a lot, a lot of capital output to get them done, and there's really not a lot of benefit to most of the commuting public," he said.

He pointed instead to a rarely used paving program, in which ACHD covers the cost of the asphalt and neighbors cover the cost of labor and other expenses. For this alley, Otte estimated the work would cost anywhere between $100,000 and $250,000.

"I don't think it should be put on the residents," Paananen said. "Everybody uses this, not just the people that live here."

But when an inspector measured the alley within the last month, Otte said it wasn't wide enough to qualify for the paving program.

### Back to gravel

Now, instead, Otte said the district's top option is to tear out the failing asphalt and return the alley to gravel, with the costs covered by ACHD, which would make it the first paved alley in the North End that the district converts back to gravel. He added that returning the gravel is less expensive than repaving.

Paananen and Bello said that was the first they had heard of a gravel option, from KTVB, and they worry it won't solve the flooding, and just create more issues with mud and dust.

"If they're not maintaining the asphalt, they're not going to maintain the gravel," Bello said. "That's how I look at it."

Paananen said she isn't convinced either: "I don't know that I would at this point, because then you're left with mud and dust, and all the other problems that come along with it just being dirt or gravel … you would probably have better drainage quality than you would with it being fully paved, but I don't know that that's the answer."

Otte said the process is designed to address exactly those concerns. Rather than an old dusty dirt road, he said, crews would remove the top layer of asphalt, rebuild the base with compacted three-quarter road mix and grade the surface flat so water drains away from homes instead of pooling and crowning as it does now.

"We can get that alley flattened out, we can grade it properly and make sure that we're not putting water into people's garages," Otte said.

### Not a done deal and maybe not the last

After KTVB's interviews, ACHD said it had received additional feedback from residents. Some who don't see a problem with the pavement, and others who do. As a result, the district said, returning the alley to gravel remains its top option but is no longer a guarantee.

ACHD said it plans to reach out to every neighbor on the alley through the summer before making a final decision, and that any work would come after chip seal season ends in late September. The district said it is also weighing whether to leave the alley as is, with targeted improvements for residents dealing with flooding. What hasn't changed, ACHD said, is that it has no plans to repave.
