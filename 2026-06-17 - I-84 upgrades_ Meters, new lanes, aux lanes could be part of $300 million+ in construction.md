Auxiliary lanes, ramp metering, widening projects, and hundreds of millions of dollars in upgrades could eventually come to I-84 as the Idaho Transportation Department attempts to keep things moving.

It’s no secret that getting on I-84 in the valley at 5 p.m. will usually find commuters sitting in traffic. With more people moving to the valley each year, traffic congestion is projected to get worse. According to District Engineer Jason Brinkman, by 2055, if nothing is done, travel times are expected to nearly double. And that’s not the only impact.

“Not only do the travel times during the peak hour double, but the peak hour becomes the peak hours,” Brinkman said in his report to the ITD board on Wednesday. He said the congestion would continue to bleed further out onto local roadways.

That’s why, Brinkman said, ITD decided to look at how it might make the interstate run smoother through the I-84 mobility study.

## Not just about increasing capacity

The I-84 mobility study was done with the goal of finding ways to keep traffic moving at a consistent rate, rather than simply increasing the roadway’s capacity. Brinkman says ITD wants to ultimately avoid having certain areas where cars can drive at the speed limit and other areas where traffic flow quickly slows to stop-and-go at chokepoints.

“We have to accept some congestion. 50 or 60 miles per hour during an hour or two in the morning or the evening is not a problem if it operates consistently at that,” Brinkman said. “The goal of this study is not to identify ways to build general capacity. There is a little bit of an axiom that you can never entirely build your way out of capacity in a situation like this.”

The study looked at several scenarios and solutions to help improve traffic flow. A buildout of any of the interventions would have to go through the design process and secure funding before any construction could occur.

## Auxiliary lanes, metered ramps, more

One option was to keep the interstate to four lanes each way but add auxiliary lanes that would connect on-ramps to the next off-ramps, [as BoiseDev reported it did between Eagle Rd. and Meridian Rd.](https://boisedev.com/news/2024/02/22/itd-planning-additional-eastbound-lane-on-i-84-between-meridian-and-eagle-exits/) These lanes would help by eliminating the need for cars going short distances to merge into other lanes. The auxiliary lanes would be converted from the existing shoulders.

The study also looked at ramp metering, which is where traffic signals are placed on freeway on-ramps to control the frequency at which vehicles merge into highway traffic. Brinkman said ramp metering is fairly low-cost and easy to implement. He said, however, that he was unsure of public acceptance of the concept and that ITD staff would not feel comfortable implementing this approach without “at least approaching the legislature for enabling legislation that’s specifically named ‘ramp metering.’”

While the study found that ramp metering and four lanes plus auxiliary lanes were effective in easing congestion for the eastbound commute, Brinkman said for the westbound commute, the auxiliary lane impact was minimal and the ramp metering did “almost nothing.”

The engineer told the board that increasing the interstate to five lanes each way with auxiliary lanes was found to solve the congestion problem almost entirely. But, Brinkman said, it comes at a cost ITD is not willing to pay.

“It comes at a very high cost and very long implementation time,” Brinkman said. “Five lanes plus auxiliary lanes would require the replacement of almost every one of the brand new interchanges and structures in the corridor, which we believe is a step too far.”

![Watkins Carter - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/f002ca13-0527-4831-9254-99ce5fa33461.jpg)

However, the study found that for some areas, the only option for relief is to build more lanes. Brinkman pointed to the I-184/I-84 merge at the Flying Wye as the best example of this.

Given this data, ITD staff is recommending a hybrid plan. The department wants to increase I-84 to five lanes plus auxiliary lanes each way between the Flying Wye and Eagle Rd. Then, the interstate would move down to four lanes plus the auxiliaries everywhere else. Brinkman said the existing bridges and the design for the new bridge at Five Mile Rd. would fit this new configuration with “a minor exception” at Cloverdale Rd. that would allow them to “just squeeze through.”

## Predicted pricetag

All told, Brinkman said the anticipated cost for the project would be $300-400 million and would take four to eight years to build out. The Flying Wye to Eagle Rd. segment would be the most expensive part of the project, as Brinkman said it would require right-of-way acquisition, sound walls, and drainage improvements.

### Right of way

Right of way is land owned and maintained by a jurisdiction, often a state, city or highway district. The land is reserved for public use, like roadways.  
  
Often, when an area requires roadway improvements, such as widening, and the responsible jurisdiction needs additional space, they begin a right-of-way acquisition process. This includes working with property owners to purchase the needed property.

ITD staff’s estimated time of build for these projects is the mid 2030s but is “very much dependent on funding.”

The ITD Board approved $20 million later in the meeting to fund early design for “high priority” portions of the project. ITD’s Amy Schroeder said the $20 million was “part of a larger funding plan for the I-84 mobility improvements.”

## Boise to Mountain Home

Mobility improvements between the Flying Wye and Garrity Blvd. interchanges are not the only I-84 upgrades ITD is considering for the future. Brinkman told the ITD board that the District 3 team is also moving forward with a widening study of I-84 between Boise and Mountain Home.

“We know that that needs to be at least a three-lane roadway, and some portions of it is looking like will need to be four in our planning horizon,” Brinkman said. “It’s not so much a matter of what is needed there, but how to do it.”

Brinkman said growth from Micron, large planned housing developments, and proposed casinos were all driving factors that led ITD to look into the widening.
