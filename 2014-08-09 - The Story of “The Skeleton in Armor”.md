**Stories of Famous Poems**

**“The Skeleton in Armor.”**

**Henry W. Longfellow**

“Speak! speak! thou fearful guest!

Who, with thy hollow breast

Still in rude armor drest,

Comest to daunt me!

Wrapt not in Eastern balms,

But with thy fleshless palms

Stretched, as if asking alms,

Why dost thou haunt me?”

Then, from those cavernous eyes

Pale flashes seemed to rise,

As when the Northern skies

Gleam in December;

And, like the water’s flow

Under December’s snow,

Came a dull voice of woe

From the heart’s chamber.

“I was a Viking old!

My deeds, though manifold,

No Skald in song has told,

No Saga taught thee!

Take heed, that in thy verse

Thou dost the tale rehearse,

Else dread a dead man’s curse;

For this I sought thee.

….

“Thus, seamed with many scars,

Bursting these prison bars,

Up to its native stars

My soul ascended!

There from the flowing bowl

Deep drinks the warrior’s soul,

Skoal! to the Northland! skoal!”

Thus the tale ended.

“The Skeleton in Armor” was suggested to Longfellow while taking a drive along the famous seashore road at Newport, R.I. Some time previous to this drive a [skeleton had been dug up at Fall River](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Skeleton_in_Armor), a large manufacturing town due north of Newport and a short distance to the east of Narraganset Bay. This skeleton was clad in broken and corroded armor.

The finding of this skeleton was considerably talked about at the time, and the incident was fresh in the memory of the poet, when he visited Newport, and spent considerable time examining the famous Round Tower in Truro Park. In the fashionable city by the sea. During his drive Longfellow began associating the skeleton with the Round Tower, and the idea occurred to him that the two might, in some way, be connected. At least, he felt there was enough connection between the two for the purpose of a ballad.

When the workmen of Fall River disinterred the skeleton, they found it in a sitting position and wrapped in a thin bark, which immediately crumbled when exposed to the air. The skeleton was apparently that of a man of large stature and sturdily built, suggesting the physique of the Norsemen, who are credited with having built the Round Tower at Newport.

Of more significance even than its rude covering was the discovery that pieces of crude armor still clung to it. A thin plate of brass covered the breast, curiously patterned and carved, and about the waist was girdled a broad belt made of short brass tubes fastened by cords of rawhide.

At the time of the finding of this old skeleton, Longfellow was a young professor at Harvard College. He took much interest in the discovery, and while the public generally supposed that it was the skeleton of an Indian, Longfellow claimed that as Indians never wore armor, it must be the remains of one of those adventurous Norsemen who visited the coast of New England many years before the time of Columbus.

“Who knows,” queried the young professor, “whether the skeleton discovered at Fall River is not that of one of the same bold seamen who built the Newport Tower? These thoughts emphasized themselves in the poet’s mind and soon formed themselves into a romantic story of a bold Viking and a fair young maiden whom he steals from her kingly father and brings over the sea to the strange western land. This story he put into musical verse, and thus the poem which he called “The Skeleton in Armor” came into being.
