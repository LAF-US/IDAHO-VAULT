## What is witnessed

A stanza-by-stanza pass reading *Shall Rome* through the **tria nomina** — the Roman three-part
naming convention (praenomen, nomen, cognomen) — and through **reputation and inheritance** as the
axis it exposes. The pass corrected itself twice in the course of being made; both corrections are
kept on the record below rather than smoothed out, per the vault's Repair axis (`.claude/CLAUDE.md`):
witness the error, don't excise it.

## The closing tercet, read literally

> (I am a typical man) *What makes us different?*
> (And there are plentiful more) *What makes me so different?*
> (Who wonder) **How is his name greater than mine?**

First pass at this line reached for outside apparatus — the real praenomen/nomen/cognomen of Cassius,
Cinna, Casca, Brutus, and Caesar, their gentes, the Julian claim of descent from Venus via Aeneas —
and called that "the literal reading of the stanza." Corrected on challenge: **none of that is in the
three lines.** Counted plainly, the tercet contains zero proper nouns. No praenomen, no nomen, no
cognomen — not even "Caesar," who is only "his." The entire line is built on the bare abstract noun
**name**, unattached to any specific one of the three registers a Roman name actually has.

The reason the line doesn't need to specify — supplied in conversation, not by me — is that the
speakers aren't doing administrative naming. They're looking at the man. The tria nomina is a scribal
apparatus: praenomen, nomen, and cognomen exist as separate fields on a census roll or a legal
document. Watching a living man walk into a room, none of that separates — his bearing, his family's
standing, and his own reputation arrive on the eye as one fused impression. "Name" in this line is the
correct word for what's actually perceived when the three registers are re-fused into a body standing
in front of you. That reframes the envy, not softens it: Cassius isn't complaining about an
abstraction he can't parse. He's complaining about something visible, present, and unarguable —
Caesar's whole inherited-and-earned standing, worn on his person, outweighing Cassius's own in the
same room. You can dispute a title on paper. You can't out-argue a presence.

## The method of the whole song, not just the close

Read outward from that tercet, the same embodied logic runs the entire piece. Every charge in the
opening verses is a body observation, never an administrative one: *"on his own, can barely stand,"*
*"feeble, frail physique,"* *"dainty Caesar,"* *"that wife at first command,"* *"I hate how he
dresses,"* *"He's excessive, yet unimpressive."* Nobody argues from Caesar's nomen, his office, or his
record. They argue from what they can see. By the time the song reaches "how is his name greater than
mine," it isn't introducing a new category — it's finally naming what the whole preceding catalogue of
physical description has been assembling the entire time.

`[history]`: this is also, structurally, one of the ways a cognomen could get minted — not the only
way (cognomina also became hereditary family-branch names, or were conferred as honors, like
"Africanus"), but some genuinely arose from an observed trait sticking as a nickname (*Naso* for a
nose, *Cicero*, traditionally, for a wart shaped like a chickpea). `[reading]`: *"Dainty," "delicate,
dimwitted," "I hate how he dresses"* read as the conspirators attempting that same narrower mechanism —
trying to mint a rival cognomen for Caesar out of contempt, because the nomen is untouchable by insult
and the one they've got is losing.

## The refrain unmoors its own noun

> Tyranny dies with Caesar
> …
> It dies with Caesar, dies with Caesar
> *It dies, it dies*

The refrain names its subject precisely on first statement — *tyranny* dies with Caesar — and
degrades it across three repetitions until the noun is gone, leaving only "it" chanted alongside the
name. A chant can kill a grammatical object. It cannot kill a name by repetition, and a name built to
outlive its bearer is exactly what a knife — or a chant — can't touch. `[reading]`.

## Correction — the overlapping voices are simultaneous, not sequential

An earlier pass of this reading, working from the transcript's parenthetical/italic/bold formatting
alone, invented a sequential two-thread structure (a "background" collective thread building toward a
"foreground" personal one) and mapped it onto nomen-vs-cognomen staging. **This was wrong**, and the
tell was available without asking: the vault exhibit's own metadata already states the typography is
*"for simultaneous and emphatic voices."* Supplied with the actual vocal breakdown in conversation —

> **CINNA:** I am a typical man / And there are plentiful more / Who wonder: How is his name greater than mine?
> **CASSIUS:** What makes us different? / What makes me so different? / How is his name greater than mine?
> **CASCA:** I am a typical man / And there are plentiful more / Who wonder: How is his name greater than mine?

— the real structure is a canon, not a sequence. **Cinna and Casca sing identical text to each
other, word for word.** Cassius alone sings an individuated variant. All three converge on the same
closing line at the same instant. Cinna and Casca are, in this moment, functionally one undifferentiated
voice — the lyric performing "there are plentiful more" by literally duplicating a voice rather than
describing duplication — while Cassius alone gets language that's his and no one else's. `[reading]`,
corrected in place per the Repair axis; the wrong first pass is left legible above rather than deleted.

## Dictator perpetuo, and the warrant that is genuinely real

Raised directly in conversation, and it changes the weight of the reading rather than merely
complicating it: as Roman senators, Cassius, Cinna, and Casca carry a real, structural, class-inherited
abhorrence of kingship — not a manufactured pretext.

`[history]`: Caesar was named **dictator perpetuo** — dictator in perpetuity — in early 44 BCE, shortly
before the Ides of March; the precise month is debated among historians working from the coin and
epigraphic evidence, and this document doesn't resolve that debate. The traditional Roman dictatorship
was, by long custom since the early Republic, an emergency magistracy generally capped at six
months — a traditional characterization of the office's expected limit, not a claim that a fixed
constitutional rule of that kind was formally codified. Removing that limit was still a real,
formally conferred, unprecedented break from custom — not rhetorical escalation invented by his
enemies. Weeks earlier, at the Lupercalia, Antony publicly offered Caesar a diadem before a crowd, and
Caesar refused it: that offer and refusal are attested. `[reading]`: that Caesar was visibly testing
the audience's reaction, and that this means the apparatus of monarchy was being assembled around him
in public, are inferences drawn from the attested event, not the event itself. *"Caesar can't be king
for life"* is a compressed but materially grounded description of a real constitutional break in
progress.

`[reading]`: the Senate's abhorrence is also **inherited** in the strict sense this document has been
using the word — not by blood, but by office. Since the traditional founding date of 509 BCE (the same
Brutus lineage now being recruited), the Senate's entire reason to exist was annual, collegial, checked
magistracy, built specifically against permanent one-man rule. *Dictator perpetuo* doesn't only
threaten Cassius's personal standing; it abolishes the inherited premise of the institution he belongs
to. That is a real, collectively-inherited stake — the institution's own nomen, so to speak, held in
common by the whole body, not earned or envied individually.

**But the lyrics never isolate that warrant from the personal grievance riding beside it.** The line
immediately before "Caesar can't be king for life" is "bring honor to **me**"; the line immediately
after the institutional charge is "as long as he lives, **I'll** never be free." The song has a
genuine constitutional warrant available and uses it — without ever separating it from the personal
score being settled in the same breath, by the same mouths.

## The verdict this document does not give

`THE-MASK-ON-THE-THRONE`'s rule — *authenticate by warrant and steering, never by the face* — does not
mean the warrant here is false. Dictator perpetuo was real; the Lupercalia diadem was real; the
Senate's inherited stake in checked magistracy was real. It means a real warrant does not retroactively
purify everyone carrying it. This song is the harder case precisely because it will not let a reader
cleanly separate the two: a legitimate, inherited, institutional alarm and a personal, individual,
cognomen-shaped envy are sung by the same three voices, in the same lines, converging on the same
knife. `[reading]`.
