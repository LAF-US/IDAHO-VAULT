A federal appeals court ruled Thursday that a class of immigrants detained within the United States is not subject to mandatory detention, rebuffing both a Trump administration policy and the Tacoma Immigration Court’s practice of denying bond hearings to many people held at the Northwest ICE Processing Center.

In a published 2-1 decision, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit affirmed a prior ruling from Tacoma-based U.S. District Court Judge Tiffany Cartwright. Cartwright found the Tacoma Immigration Court was unlawfully denying people the chance to be released on bond to fight their deportation cases in the community, where some have lived for years.

Matt Adams, an attorney for the Northwest Immigrant Rights Project, was one lawyer representing the man whose immigration case led to the decision, Ramon Rodriguez Vazquez.

In an email to The News Tribune on Thursday, Adams said the appeals court’s decision applies to everyone locked up in immigration-detention centers in the Ninth Circuit, which covers nine western states, Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands.

The Ninth Circuit opinion noted that the circuit courts are divided on whether immigrants detained in the U.S. are subject to mandatory detention. Adams said he expects the Supreme Court to take up this question next year. In the meantime, he said, the ruling provides thousands of people the chance to get a bond hearing.

“Before this, the only way for people like our class members to get a bond hearing was to file a habeas petition in federal court, but the majority of detained people do not have an attorney who can file a habeas petition for them, and so they were stuck locked up,” Adams wrote. “As a consequence, many gave up on their cases and were deported.”

Jason Chudy, a spokesperson for the Department of Homeland Security, told The News Tribune in an email Thursday that DHS “strongly disagrees” with the Ninth Circuit panel and is confident in its legal position on mandatory detention. He said that was why DHS recently sought Supreme Court review of a similar decision in the Sixth Circuit, and that opinions from the Board of Immigration Appeals and two other federal appeals courts reflect “the soundness of DHS’s legal position.”

“This legal position was the nail in the coffin for Biden’s catch and release policies,” Chudy wrote. “That is why the left and their activist proxies on the judiciary are resisting it so vigorously. President Trump and Secretary Mullin are now enforcing the law as it was actually written to keep America safe.”

“As our record in the U.S. Supreme Court demonstrates, the law is on our side,” he added.

Kathryn Mattingly, the press secretary for the Executive Office for Immigration Review, said the office does not comment on decisions. She said it speaks for itself. Rodriguez Vazquez entered the United States illegally in 2009. Until 2025 he lived in Grandview in Yakima County, where he owned a home and had no criminal history.

Last year U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers detained Rodriguez Vazquez at his home with a warrant and brought him to the detention center in Tacoma to face removal proceedings. Tacoma Immigration Judge John Odell then denied him a bond hearing.

Rodriguez Vazquez filed a habeas petition to challenge his detention and a class-action complaint. After a court ruling he was given a bond hearing but was denied bond. Circuit Judge Daniel A. Bress — who President Donald Trump appointed in 2019 — wrote in the majority opinion that historically, the law regarded immigrants present in the interior of the U.S. who entered without admission as subject to release on bond while their removal proceedings were pending.

He said that understanding persisted after 1996 amendments to the Immigration and Nationality Act. A different provision requiring mandatory detention was understood to apply to immigrants seeking to enter the U.S. at the border, Bress wrote.

The government recently changed its approach, arguing that immigrants detained within the U.S. are subject to mandatory detention without bond based on those 1996 amendments.

A majority of the appeals panel concluded that the text, context and structure of the Immigration and Nationality Act did not direct such a significant change in law.

Bress said the government’s change in policy seems to have originated in 2022 from the practice of certain immigration judges in Tacoma denying bond to unadmitted immigrants present in the country. He said that was done on the theory that a section of the Immigration and Nationality Act, § 1225(b)(2)(A), mandated their detention.

In July 2025, ICE issued guidance that adopted that policy. The change, according to Bress, resulted in detained immigrants filing thousands of habeas petitions in federal courts across the country to seek bond hearings and possible release from detention.

Bress said the implication of the government position was that Congress made a major change to immigration laws in 1996 that subjected millions of unadmitted immigrants in the U.S. to mandatory detention, but that the change went unnoticed and unheeded for the next three decades.

“We are aided by the commonsense principle that Congress would have spoken more clearly if it had intended the dramatic expansion of mandatory detention authority that the government’s new interpretation portends,” Bress wrote.

The dissenting opinion was written by Senior Circuit Judge Carlos Bea, who was appointed by President George W. Bush in 2003. He wrote that everyone deemed to be “applicants for admission” to the country are necessarily “seeking admission” and thus subject to mandatory detention under § 1225(b)(2)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act.
