[Micron Technology](https://www.micron.com/) paid $7.35 million in local property taxes last year but escaped paying tens of millions more thanks to a property tax exemption covering roughly $6.3 billion worth of its Ada County property.

It’s hard to say exactly how much [Idaho’s New Capital Investment Act](https://legislature.idaho.gov/sessioninfo/2008/legislation/H0562/#billtext), passed in the 2008 legislative session, saves the state’s largest company each year, but the cap on Micron’s taxable value means that the chipmaker paid taxes on about one ninth of the value of its properties last year, according to data from Ada County. That leaves some $6.3 billion in property untaxed.

The rarely used law was designed to draw big corporate developments into the state. It caps a single taxpayer’s liability at $400 million in property value, provided it invests at least $1 billion in Idaho. To date, few companies have hit that mark.

With stated plans to invest [$50 billion into its Boise campus](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/business/article315725387.html), Micron easily qualifies. Because of Micron’s corporate structure, the company needed to take the exemption two times, requiring it to pay on a maximum of $800 million in assessed value, according to Brad Smith, chief deputy at the [Ada County Assessor’s Office.](https://adacounty.id.gov/assessor/)

Even doubled, the exemption reduces Micron’s taxable property — and, with it, its tax bill — by about 89%.

Only two companies in Ada County make use of the exemption: Micron (twice) and [Meta](https://datacenters.atmeta.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Meta_s-Kuna-Data-Center.pdf), which is building a 960,000-square-foot [data center in Kuna](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/business/article304464416.html), Smith told the Statesman.

[Valued at around $1 trillion](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/business/article315907963.html), Micron held roughly $7.1 billion in property last year, according to market-value appraisals from the county. The figure stands to grow in the coming years as the company wraps up the build-out of its south Boise campus, a $50 billion project crowned by two state-of-the-art chip manufacturing plants.

Micron’s $7.35 million payment to Ada County covered taxes on 58 properties countywide, according to data provided by the Treasurer’s Office and compiled by the Statesman. Even capped, that’s just under 1% of the roughly $774 million in total property taxes levied by the county last year.

As a practice, the [Ada County Treasurer’s Office](https://adacounty.id.gov/treasurer/calculation-of-property-taxes/) takes in all property taxes before the county distributes the money to the overlapping taxing districts that underpin a property: the county, city, school district and any special districts that exist, such as fire protection, ambulance or pest abatement districts.

Different properties sit at the nexus of different taxing districts, which pay different percentages of their land value to the county. That makes it difficult to come up with a value of what Micron would have paid if not for the law. Rough math puts the figure around $66 million per year, about the same as the annual budget for the [city of Eagle.](https://www.cityofeagle.org/360/Financial-Reports)

## Micron can save on sales tax, too

![Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra, center, is joined by Idaho Gov. Brad Little and Boise Mayor Lauren McLean, placing a ceremonial plaque at the construction site of a new fab facility on Thursday, Oct. 5, 2023, in East Boise.](https://www.idahostatesman.com/public/latest-news/edwz5v/picture280186769/alternates/FREE_1200/BOI_1005micronfabplaque)

Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra, center, is joined by Idaho Gov. Brad Little and Boise Mayor Lauren McLean, placing a ceremonial plaque at the construction site of a new fab facility on Thursday, Oct. 5, 2023, in East Boise. Darin Oswald doswald@idahostatesman.com

Micron is also eligible for a pair of exemptions from the state’s 6% sales and use tax as they build out their campus and bring manufacturing online.

Idaho offers a broad production exemption on equipment used to manufacture products in the state. In Micron’s case, that means the intricate — and expensive — machines the company uses to fabricate the microchips in its Boise clean rooms.

It is also eligible for a narrower exemption through a 2022 law called the [Idaho Semiconductors for America Act](https://legislature.idaho.gov/sessioninfo/2022/legislation/H0678/). Passed five months before [Micron announced its first wave of Boise development](https://in.micron.com/manufacturing-expansion/boise), the law exempts construction and building materials directly used to develop, expand or modernize semiconductor manufacturing, testing, packaging or research and development facilities across the state.

To qualify, the law sets as a prerequisite that companies must be eligible for incentives under federal [CHIPS and Science Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/4346), a massive funding package designed to spur domestic chip production. Two other companies operating in Idaho receive CHIPS Act funding, according to the [Semiconductor Industry Association: Lam Research and Air Liquide, both in Boise.](https://www.semiconductors.org/chip-supply-chain-investments/)

Micron issued a statement in response to questions from the Idaho Statesman. A spokesperson for the company would neither confirm whether the company utilizes these sales tax exemptions, nor would it corroborate the property tax figures the Statesman compiled based on county data.

“The tax incentive programs we participate in are broadly available to qualifying businesses in Idaho and were an important factor in locating this project here,” a Micron spokesman stated. “As the only U.S.-based manufacturer of memory, we are proud to build the leading-edge memory manufacturing capacity America needs to maintain technological leadership and strengthen its economic and national security.”

The company declined to answer follow-up questions.

Through a spokesman, the company emphasized its importance to the state as a “significant and growing source of income for Idaho’s economy.”

Micron’s $50 billion development is the largest private investment Idaho has ever seen, according to the company. Today, it employs 7,000 people in southeast Boise, the company stated; ultimately, it estimates the plant will generate 17,000 new jobs — including 3,500 people working directly for Micron — “driving billions of dollars in economic activity across the state.”

Founded in Boise in 1978, Micron also pointed to its Idaho roots and in-state growth “thanks to the strong business environment the state has fostered.”

“As a leading company in the state, we are pleased to partner with all levels of government and the community as good corporate citizens,” the company said.

## ‘Is it worth it?’

![Construction of a new $15 billion fab plant at Micron progresses in East Boise, Thursday, Feb. 26, 2026.](https://www.idahostatesman.com/public/latest-news/iulk0e/picture314897277/alternates/FREE_1200/BOI_0303micron.JPG)

Construction of Micron’s first of two manufacturing plants continues in 2026. Darin Oswald doswald@idahostatesman.com

Idaho Rep. John Gannon, D-Boise, voted for the Idaho Semiconductors for America sales tax exemption four years ago. Lately, though, he has struggled to get a handle on how much Micron pays in taxes — and how much constituents feel it should.

Property and sales taxes don’t tell the full story. Micron has paid the city of Boise and the Ada County Highway District [more than $32 million](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/business/article316148199.html) throughout the construction process so far in planning, development and impact fees, according to records provided to and obtained by the Idaho Statesman. Those will wane when construction ends; the city expects fee revenue to slow around 2030, according to budget documents.

Micron is also subject to [Idaho’s 5.3% corporate income tax](https://tax.idaho.gov/taxes/income-tax/business-income/online-guide/#BITRates) on its Idaho business, though it is unclear how much that is worth. The firm paid a total of $8.2 billion in income taxes through the first nine months of fiscal 2026, according to [quarterly income statements](https://investors.micron.com/quarterly-results). But the multinational operates in at least eight states and 14 countries, and it doesn’t break out what portion was paid to each.

Gannon said he hasn’t been able to find that information for Idaho, which he says is key to the calculus.

“My view is that the community needs to consider whether such a large industry should or should not pay taxes,” Gannon said. “The first thing is, the community needs to know what kind of tax contribution they’re making, and what kind of impact they’re having — on housing, traffic and state services.

“Their big thing was that they create jobs, and they’re critical for national defense,” Gannon added. “People have to weigh that — does that entitle them to the generous tax exemptions Idaho has offered?”

## Idaho, other states pay in AI arms race

![A Micron Technology Inc. employee moves through a clean room at a fab on the memory-chip maker’s Southeast Boise campus on Monday, Sept. 12, 2022.](https://www.idahostatesman.com/public/latest-news/gxxls0/picture265700161/alternates/FREE_1200/BOI_0912micronworker)

A Micron Technology Inc. employee moves through a clean room at a fab on the memory-chip maker’s Southeast Boise campus on Monday, Sept. 12, 2022. Darin Oswald doswald@idahostatesman.com

Micron’s Boise development doesn’t exist in a vacuum. Other states are scrambling to get a piece of the AI boom with massive promises.

Michigan offered Micron nearly $28 billion in incentives, including $2.5 billion in direct payments to move to the state in 2023, [according to reports from The Detroit News](https://www.detroitnews.com/story/business/2023/07/25/micron-technology-semiconductor-plant-syracuse-new-york-tax-incentives-michigan/70464860007/?gnt-cfr=1&gca-cat=p&gca-uir=true&gca-epti=z1177xxe1177xxv003647d--40--b--40--&gca-ft=187&gca-ds=sophi). That was more than the budgets of a dozen state departments at the time, and about three times what Michigan paid for its entire state police force. Over the life of the deal, Michigan would have paid about $3 million per job Micron promised, according [to an analysis by the Mackinaw Center for Public Policy](https://www.mackinac.org/blog/2023/michigan-offered-one-company-more-subsides-than-the-budget-of-a-dozen-state-departments), a free-market think tank based in Midland, Michigan.

The bid fell billions short.

[New York offered $5.5 billion in refundable tax-credits](https://esd.ny.gov/sites/default/files/Micron-Term-Sheet-Fully-Executed.pdf) — a dollar-for-dollar reduction in its tax bill that can be converted directly to cash if the figure exceeds tax liability. The subsidy headlined a gaudy incentive package offering property and sales tax exemptions, discount electricity and payments for infrastructure in exchange for about 9,000 jobs.

“Idaho essentially agreed to forgo the sales tax receipts on activity that otherwise might not happen. New York offered considerably more,” wrote Ken Girardin, an adviser at the [non-profit Empire Center for Public Policy,](https://www.empirecenter.org/publications/the-micron-test/#_edn49) a free-market think tank based in Albany.

In a 2025 report, Girardin estimated the state’s total offering could exceed $11 billion. [Micron plans to invest more than $100 billion](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/promise-progress-governor-hochul-celebrates-major-milestone-microns-100-billion-investment) to build a plant in Clay, 15 miles north of Syracuse, according to multiple reports and the Governor’s Office.

To Alex LaBeau, that’s what it takes to compete.

LaBeau is the president of the [Idaho Association of Commerce and Industry](https://www.iaci.org/staff.php), a pro-business lobby. He worked to pass the New Capital Investment Act in 2008. Passed while sliding into the Great Recession, the law didn’t envision Micron; it was written to attract Areva, a defunct French nuclear power company that was considering Eastern Idaho for a $2 billion [uranium enrichment plant](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/EIS-0471-FEIS-2011.pdf). Areva never broke ground and is now out of business. But the law remained intact when Micron negotiated its expansion plans nearly 15 years later.

So did it work as intended?

“Yes,” LaBeau said. “Every incentive you can bring to the table is part of the discussion.”

To LaBeau, Idaho doesn’t bring enough to that table, putting the state at a “competitive disadvantage when it comes to business diversification.”

“To be very honest, Idaho has never been very good at the incentives game,” he said. “There was this ethic, that Idaho is a great place to be, and so (companies) are just going to be here. That’s not reality, and it’s never been reality.”

With multinational chipmakers like Micron, Idaho isn’t just competing with other states like Michigan and New York.

“It’s bigger than that,” he said. “It’s us versus Singapore. It’s us versus Qatar. It’s us versus Japan.

“Idaho is doing better, but we’re still behind,” LaBeau said in an interview. “There are a number of states that will straight up write a check to a company to move them there, with the anticipation that they’re going to get some tax revenue on the back end.”

LaBeau said he discusses new tax incentives with the Legislature every year.

“We need to keep moving forward,” he said. “If you want to give your kids a chance to stay here and work here, we’ve got to keep diversifying our economy.”
