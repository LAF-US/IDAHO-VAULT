## The hidden costs of going month-to-month on your lease in Idaho are growing

In Boise, a worker must earn roughly $35 an hour to afford the average two-bedroom apartment, writes guest columnist Heather Owens.

What would you do if your rental costs doubled next month? In Idaho, some landlords and management companies impose steep month-to-month lease fees that can do just that.

A month-to-month fee is an additional charge added to monthly rent in exchange for the flexibility of a short-term lease agreement. Often, these fees take effect once a standard 12-month lease ends if the tenant does not sign a new long-term lease.

In a rental market that has become increasingly unaffordable for many Idahoans due to stagnating wages and the state’s recent housing boom, these fees may become the breaking point for some of Idaho’s most vulnerable renters, leading to housing insecurity and even homelessness.

## Idaho renters already under pressure

According to [the National Low Income Housing Coalition](https://nlihc.org/gap/state/id), nearly 50% of Idaho’s low-income renters are cost-burdened, spending more than 30% of their monthly income on housing. Those struggling with high rents include working families, seniors on fixed incomes, and people living with disabilities.

In Boise, a worker must earn roughly $35 an hour to afford the average two-bedroom apartment. For someone earning minimum wage, that would require working 154 hours per week to afford the same apartment.

## When a lease renewal costs hundreds

For Idaho tenants, the realities of month-to-month leasing can be devastating. These fees often range from $200 to $500 per month, with some renters reporting charges nearly equal to their monthly rent. For many already struggling to keep up with rising housing costs, these additional fees are simply unaffordable.

Many Idaho leases automatically convert to month-to-month agreements after the initial lease term ends. Under Idaho law, landlords may increase rent with just 15 days’ notice before the lease expires. At the same time, landlords have full discretion in setting lease fees and rental increases.

Landlords do face legitimate concerns when tenants are not locked into long-term leases.

Turnover costs, uncertainty and administrative work are real considerations for landlords and property managers. However, charging hundreds of dollars each month goes far beyond covering administrative costs and instead becomes one more expense many Idaho tenants simply can’t afford.

## The repercussions of month-to-month fees on Idaho tenants

At [Jesse Tree, a nonprofit focused on homelessness prevention](https://www.jessetreeidaho.org/) through eviction intervention, staff have seen an increase in tenants citing month-to-month fees as a major source of financial hardship. One tenant living on a fixed income had consistently paid rent on time before a hospitalization prevented them from signing a new 12-month lease. Because their lease automatically converted to month-to-month, they were charged an additional $500 monthly fee they could not cover, and subsequently ended up in eviction court on account of it.

There are many reasons why someone may not immediately sign a new lease agreement: health emergencies, hospitalization, work schedules, transportation barriers, family crises or simple miscommunication. Yet for many Idaho renters, failing to complete that additional administrative step can trigger hundreds of dollars in added housing costs almost overnight.

## Idaho can do better

Idaho is a place where many people still believe hard work should be enough to stay housed. However, when renters who are already struggling face hundreds of dollars in additional fees because of a missed appointment, a hospitalization or a delayed lease renewal, we must question whether our housing system is serving our communities fairly.

Month-to-month lease fees may be legal, but that does not always make them reasonable. At a time when so many Idaho families are already one emergency away from losing their housing, costly fees on top of already unaffordable rents pushes more people toward housing instability.
