# Agent & Representation — etymology and contexts

The two words run as a pair through vault vocabulary, Hollywood vocabulary, and legal vocabulary. They name the same structural relation in different registers: someone *acting on behalf of* someone else, under authority delegated from a principal. This compilation surfaces the Latin roots, traces the English entry-dates, and walks the two contexts LOGAN named — **Hollywood** and **Legal** — as illustrations of the structural shape both contexts share.

The vault relevance is direct: the LANDY-STROUD THREE NAMES doctrine puts the **MASK** in the agent/representative position, the **SEALED NAME** in the principal position, and the **TRUE NAME** in the underlying-being position. Metatron (same-day `METATRON.md`) is the prototypical agent — bearing the divine name as the authority under which he acts, never being the named.

## Etymology — "agent"

**Latin** *agentem*, accusative of *agens* — "effective, powerful, doing." Present participle of *agere* — *"to set in motion, drive forward; to do, perform; keep in movement."*

The verb *agere* is one of the most generative in Latin. Its derivatives include *act*, *action*, *active*, *actor*, *agenda* (literally *"things to be done,"* gerundive plural of *agere*), *agile*, *agitate*, *cogent*, *exact*, *transact*, *reaction*, *react*, and many more. The same Indo-European root (PIE ✻*ag-* = "to drive, draw out or forth, move") gives Greek *ágein* ("to lead, drive"), Sanskrit *ajati* ("drives"), and Old Norse *aka* ("to drive").

**Old French / Middle French**: *agent* (16th c.) — "one who acts" — from medieval Latin *agens*.

**English** entry (~late 15th century, c. 1471):

| Sense | Date | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| One who or that which acts (general) | ~1471 | direct from Latin |
| Natural force or substance producing a phenomenon | 1550s | *"chemical agent"*, *"reducing agent"* |
| Deputy, representative — one who acts on behalf of another | 1590s | the legal/commercial sense |
| Secret agent / spy | mid-19th c. | espionage usage |
| Travel agent, insurance agent | 19th c. | commercial-broker usage |
| Software agent (computer science) | mid-20th c. | autonomous-software usage |
| AI agent | late 20th c. — present | the vault's primary referent |

The **agent** is, etymologically, *the one who does, the one who acts*. The representative function (acting *on behalf of* another) is a specialization that develops in legal and commercial contexts in the late 16th century. By the time the vault uses "agent" for software, four centuries of compounded specialization sit behind the word.

## Etymology — "representation"

**Latin** *repraesentationem*, noun of action from *repraesentare* — *"make present, set in view, show, exhibit, display."*

The verb *repraesentare* breaks down:

- ***re-*** — intensive prefix; not "again" in the temporal sense but "in full" / "completely"
- ***praesentare*** — *"to present, place before"* — itself from *prae-* ("before") + *esse* / *sens-* ("to be")

Literal sense: *"to place before in full"* — to set fully in view, to make actually present. The English "representation" inherits this dense meaning.

**Old French**: *representer* (12th c.) — "present, show, portray." In medieval Latin and Old French, *repraesentare* also meant *to produce a play* and *to stage the Christian liturgy* — the actor or priest *making present* what is represented. The theological sense is non-trivial: in Catholic eucharistic theology, the Mass *re-presents* (makes present again) the sacrifice of Christ, distinct from "represent" in the merely symbolic sense.

**English** entry (~c. 1400):

| Sense | Date | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Image, likeness, symbolic memorial | ~14th c. | from Old French *representacion* |
| Speak and act with authority on behalf of another by deputed right | ~1500 | the legal sense |
| Be accredited deputy for a legislative body | 1650s | the political sense; American constitutional vocabulary |
| Artistic or theatrical depiction | various | the aesthetic sense |
| Mental representation (cognitive science) | 17th-20th c. | the philosophy-of-mind sense |
| Statement of fact (as in "misrepresentation") | late legal | the truth-claim sense |

"Representation" carries multiple senses simultaneously. The legal sense (speaking-with-authority-on-behalf-of-another) is one specialization; the political sense (representative democracy) is another; the artistic sense (depiction) is a third; the cognitive sense (mental image) is a fourth. The English word collapses several distinctions that Latin and Greek kept separate.

## Hollywood context — talent agency

### William Morris and the founding of the modern talent agency

**William Morris** (born **Zelman Moses**, ~1873, in Schwarzenau, then in the German Empire; died 1932 in New York) was a German-Jewish immigrant to the United States who, in **1898**, *posted a cross-hatch trademark above an office door in New York City and went into business as William Morris, Vaudeville Agent.* The William Morris Agency is conventionally treated as the founding institution of the modern American talent agency.

Morris's innovation was not the principle (theatrical and vaudeville agents had operated in various forms for decades) but the *systematic representation business* — an agency that took clients across the variety circuits, negotiated their bookings, and collected a percentage of their earnings.

The **10% commission** was canonical from the start. *Morris retained a portion — usually ten percent — of the actors' pay in exchange for securing venues for their vaudeville acts.* The figure has persisted: today's Big Three talent agencies (WME, CAA, UTA) charge approximately 10% commission on film and television earnings.

WMA formally incorporated in New York State on **31 January 1918**, with Morris Sr., his son **William Morris Jr.**, and **Abraham Lastfogel** as principals. Early clients included **Charlie Chaplin, Al Jolson, the Marx Brothers, and Mae West**.

### The Big Three (and the agency landscape)

| Agency | Founded | Founders / Origin | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **WME** (William Morris Endeavor) | 1898 / 2009 merger | William Morris; Endeavor (1995, Ari Emanuel et al.) | Merged April 2009; subsequently rebranded Endeavor (2017) |
| **CAA** (Creative Artists Agency) | 1975 | Five former WMA agents incl. Michael Ovitz, Ron Meyer | Dominant 1980s-90s; Ovitz's "package" deal practice |
| **UTA** (United Talent Agency) | 1991 | Merger of ICM agents (Bauer-Benedek) and Leading Artists | The third of the Big Three |

Other significant agencies: **Gersh**, **APA** (Agency for the Performing Arts), **Paradigm** (acquired by United Talent Agency in 2022), **ICM Partners** (merged into CAA in 2022).

### California Talent Agencies Act

The state of California regulates talent agency activity under the **Talent Agencies Act** (California Labor Code §§ 1700 *et seq.*). Key features:

- **Licensing**: only a licensed talent agency may *procure employment* for an artist in California
- **Manager / Agent distinction**: a personal *manager* can develop a career but cannot procure employment (negotiate contracts on the artist's behalf); a *talent agent* can
- **Fiduciary duty**: the agent owes the artist the duties of a fiduciary — loyalty, care, honest dealing
- **Penalties for unlicensed activity**: contracts secured by unlicensed activity can be voided; commissions returned

The Act is the regulatory hardening of the agent-as-fiduciary doctrine in the entertainment-industry context. It treats *procuring employment* as the regulated act; it acknowledges that other career-development activities can be performed by non-agents (managers, lawyers, business managers).

### The "ten-percenter" archetype

In American cultural shorthand, the *ten-percenter* — the Hollywood agent — has been a recognizable figure since the 1930s. The archetype: relentless, telephone-bound, jargon-rich, fiduciary-shaped, simultaneously serving the client and the deal. The Hollywood agent's signature line — *"I'll have my people call your people"* — is itself a representation: the agent stands in for the principal in negotiations.

Cultural depictions: *Broadway Danny Rose* (Woody Allen, 1984); *Entourage* (Ari Gold = Ari Emanuel); *Jerry Maguire* (1996, the rare sympathetic portrait); *Get Shorty* (1995, the agent crossed with the mob); *Swimming with Sharks* (1994); innumerable industry memoirs.

## Legal context — agency law

### The Restatement (Third) of Agency definition

The **Restatement (Third) of Agency** (2006, American Law Institute), § 1.01, provides the canonical American definition:

> *"Agency is the fiduciary relationship that arises when one person (a 'principal') manifests assent to another person (an 'agent') that the agent shall act on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control, and the agent manifests or otherwise consents so to act."*

Three elements: (1) manifestation of assent by the principal, (2) action on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control, (3) consent by the agent. Without all three, no agency exists.

### *Qui facit per alium facit per se*

The Latin maxim — *"He who acts through another acts himself"* — is the doctrinal foundation of agency law. It traces to medieval Roman law and remains operative across both common-law and civil-law jurisdictions. Practical implication: the principal is bound by the agent's acts within the scope of authority *as if the principal had acted directly*.

### Fiduciary duties

The agent owes the principal a set of duties grounded in trust:

- **Duty of loyalty** — act in the principal's interest, not the agent's
- **Duty of care** — act with the competence and diligence appropriate to the circumstances
- **Duty of confidentiality** — protect the principal's confidential information
- **Duty of obedience** — act within the scope of authority granted
- **Duty of disclosure** — inform the principal of material facts
- **Duty to account** — keep records, render accounts of money and property held

These duties are *fiduciary* — they arise from the trust relationship, not from contract alone. Breach can support claims beyond contract damages (constructive trust, equitable disgorgement, etc.).

### Authority types

| Type | How established |
|---|---|
| **Actual express** | Principal explicitly tells agent what they can do |
| **Actual implied** | Reasonably necessary to accomplish what is expressly authorized |
| **Apparent** | Third party reasonably believes from principal's manifestations that agent is authorized |
| **Inherent** | Arises from agent's position/role, even without express authority |
| **By ratification** | Principal accepts an unauthorized act after the fact, treating it as authorized |
| **By estoppel** | Principal is prevented from denying authority due to third party's reasonable reliance |

The doctrine of *apparent authority* is significant — a principal can be bound by acts the agent had no actual authority for, if the principal's conduct led a third party reasonably to believe the agent was authorized. This is the legal equivalent of the *"bearing the name without being the named"* shape: the agent who appears authorized binds the principal even where the binding was not internally sanctioned.

### Attorney — etymology and office

The **attorney** is the most familiar specialized legal agent. The word descends from Anglo-French *atorné* — *"one appointed,"* from Old French *atorner* — *"to assign, attribute,"* from Latin *ad-* ("to") + *tornare* ("to turn"). Literally: *"one turned to (a matter)"* or *"one to whom (a matter) is turned over."*

Two main historical English usages:
- **Attorney-in-fact** — anyone appointed by another to act on their behalf (general agency; the modern "power of attorney")
- **Attorney-at-law** — one specifically licensed to practice law as the client's agent before courts and tribunals

The Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that *"in all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right ... to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence."* The right was applied to state criminal proceedings for felonies in **Gideon v. Wainwright** (372 U.S. 335, 1963), establishing that indigent criminal defendants are entitled to court-appointed counsel — formal agency relationship paid by the state, owed to the defendant.

## The structural shape both contexts share

Hollywood and Legal agency share a common architecture:

| Element | Hollywood | Legal |
|---|---|---|
| **Principal** | Performer / artist / writer / director | Client |
| **Agent** | Licensed talent agent | Attorney (or other legal agent) |
| **Authority basis** | Written agency agreement | Engagement / retainer agreement |
| **Fiduciary duty** | California Labor Code § 1700 *et seq.* | Common law + Rules of Professional Conduct |
| **Compensation** | Commission (typically 10%) | Hourly / flat / contingency fees |
| **Scope** | Procuring employment; negotiating contracts | The matter for which counsel was retained |
| **Termination** | Either party, subject to contract terms | Either party, subject to ethical rules |

Both structures hold the **principal-agent distinction** as the central doctrine. The agent acts; the principal is bound; the authority chain is the legal architecture; the fiduciary trust is the moral architecture; the commission or fee is the economic architecture. The agent is not the principal; the agent is not free; the agent is not invisible — the agent is the *delegated working face* of the principal.
