## Star Mayor says legislature is ‘failing’ local communities before vote on big impact fee hike

![](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/star-grants-40-raise-to-mayor-star-city-hall-Large.jpeg)

Star City Hall. Photo: Courtesy City of Star

Star’s Mayor had harsh words for the Idaho Legislature before the booming suburb’s elected officials approved new impact fees to cover the cost of services.

On Tuesday, the Star City Council unanimously approved a long-discussed proposal to increase [impact fees on new development](https://boisedev.com/news/2025/05/29/impact-fees-explaination/) in the suburb spanning the Ada and Canyon County line. These fees, which will add $4,500 for the cost of a new home in Star, will go toward funding new parks and community recreation facilities, along with cars and equipment for new Star Police Officers, as the city continues to grow. This is a slightly scaled-back proposal than what was originally on the table for approval because the park’s impact fees will be phased in at 80%, 90%, and 100% of the new fee over the course of three years. Council members also elected to cut down the police impact fee by removing a figure that would have collected funds toward improving the city’s access to a police training facility.

Homebuilders and the Building Contractors Association of Southwest Idaho argued vehemently against the new $63 million impact fee plan during the more than three-hour public hearing. With the testimony of an outside expert hired from Arizona, the industry group argued that Star was illegally charging fees on builders that go beyond what is allowed under state law. They took issue with how the city calculated the cost to buy new property, the inclusion of leased spaces for community recreation activities in the calculation, and how equipment was valued, arguing the city was breaking the law by charging too high a fee.

During the testimony, homebuilders urged Star to reconsider implementing the fee package and instead work with them on other solutions, like making other deals with developers on infrastructure improvements and access to parks. BCA lobbyist Ken Burgess accused the city of improperly increasing impact fees to compensate for the loss of tax revenue from the [2021 property tax reform bill HB 389](https://boisedev.com/news/2021/05/04/idaho-house-of-representatives-passes-property-tax-reform-package/). This bill, which has been [heavily criticized by local leaders for years](https://boisedev.com/news/2025/06/05/meridian-state-of-the-city-simison-2025/), cuts into how much tax revenue local governments can take in from new growth.

Chadwick, a Republican, fired back with harsh words for GOP leadership at the Idaho Legislature and their refusal to budge on reforming the law. The Idaho Legislature [passed a bill allowing fire and EMS districts to realize more property tax revenue](https://boisedev.com/news/2026/04/02/389-fire-ems/) from growth this year, but left a bill giving cities like Star more leverage to collect taxes on the table. He pushed back on comments from homebuilders that Star should focus more on reforming local government tax policy instead of paying for services by charging developers more fees.

“We need the community’s help to put the pressure on the legislature because the legislature is failing us on every freaking single level in this state, and it needs to be known, and they know I feel this way on that,” Chadwick said, calling on the homebuilders to lobby the legislature for change. “I’m all for it. Join and help us. Help us fix that problem, but kicking the can down the road doesn’t fix that problem, but until there’s a big overhaul in leadership in that statehouse, we’ll never see a change because they penalize and hate local communities. If you look at all the bills they try to throw on cities that make it difficult to manage our communities, that’s a challenge.”

Since the passage of HB 389, Star has also adopted a system of mitigation fees collected from developers to cover the ongoing costs of staffing up the Star-Middleton Fire District and the Star Police Department, [despite questions of the fees’ legality.](https://boisedev.com/news/2026/02/02/star-mitigation-fees/)

## How will the proposal work?

Impact fees can’t be used for just anything.

According to Idaho code, cities and counties can only charge impact fees on new development and use the proceeds to ensure the city’s existing level of service keeps up with the growth, with one-time projects. This means that cities cannot use them to hire new staff or to cover other ongoing costs, but they can build a police station or a park, so the city’s existing level of service stays the same once you add more people. This involves calculating the city’s existing level of service for parks and police officers and then seeing how much each new home would stretch that, which is how cities calculate how much their fees should be.

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

Star has been developing this proposal to increase impact fees over the past year and has tabled the vote on adopting this ordinance multiple times due to objections from the BCA. The homebuilders’ association argued the city was inflating its existing level of service using expensive calculations for how much it costs to buy land, build each square foot of office space, or maintain its existing facilities, some of which are currently leased instead of buildings the city owns.

“We do generally support impact fees, so we’re against supporting impact fees, but only when they’re fair and equitable,” Adam Capel with the BCA said during his testimony. “We do believe in the principle that growth should pay for growth, but what we cannot support are fees where new growth is being asked to correct existing deficiencies or subsidize past underinvestment. This doesn’t meet the standard of fair and equitable impact fees.”

Star’s initial proposal, considered Tuesday night, would have increased park impact fees from $2,050 to $5,869 in a single year. It would have also added a new police impact fee of $1,321 to cover cars, equipment for new officers, improvements to a police training facility, and construction costs on new substations for the Star Police Department. It would have also adopted a.60 impact fee per square foot of commercial space added to the city toward boosting the police department. The city was also considering a third impact fee of roughly $2,204 per home to raise funds to build pathways, but that was tabled for the time being.

In comparison, Boise charges impact fees on a new home ranging from [$1,384 to $14,690](https://www.cityofboise.org/media/20458/fy-2026-impact-fee-schedule_final.pdf), depending on the size of the home and its location in the city.

## Council members: Growth comes with costs

After the testimony from BCA and back and forth with homebuilders, the city’s legal team and consultant Carter Froelich from Launch Development Finance Advisors, council members agreed to phase in the parks improvements over the course of three years and drop the police training facility component of the police fee. But, despite the move to slow the city’s role on increasing fees, council members were not swayed by the homebuilders’ arguments that their initial plan was illegal or would substantially impact the affordability of homes in Star.

Chadwick and multiple council members noted that their children cannot afford to buy a home in Star, even though they would love to live in the community.

![Watkins Carter - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/f002ca13-0527-4831-9254-99ce5fa33461.jpg)

“You guys build beautiful homes in Star, no doubt, but no one is working to downgrade their equipment so that they’re more affordable,” City Council Member Kevan Wheelock said, noting these new homes come with granite countertops, wood floors, and other top-of-the-line features. “They’re building them better because people still want them, and we want to provide a level of service as a city that we have come to see as a standard.”

City Council Member Kevin Nielsen said he strongly believes it’s not the government’s role to try to intervene to make housing more affordable because it goes against the capitalist system of the United States. But, he said, it is the local government’s role to ensure that new residents shoulder their share of the burden to cover the costs of new services needed in the growing city.

“My viewpoint is there’s an impact that growth brings to the city,” he said. “There’s a cost for that. Either the growth pays for that, or someone subsidizes it. The money has to come from somewhere. What am I, as a city council person, willing to adopt as a subsidy for people moving here? And I find myself unwilling to provide subsidies for people moving here.”
