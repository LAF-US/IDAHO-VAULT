## “RUBE ROBBINS: ONE TOUGH HOMBRE”

## — HE FEARED NO ONE —

### Scanning the horizon, Marshal Orlando “Rube” Robbins adjusted his bandana on the back of his neck, hoping for relief from the scorching sun. The 45-year-old lawman had spent two months in the saddle tracking an escaped prisoner across the Dakota and Oregon territories.

![He Feared No One](https://tomrizzo.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/robbins.001-2.jpg)

Robbins finally caught up with outlaw **Charley Chambers** in August 1882 in Portland. He took him into custody and returned him to prison in Boise, completing a journey of about **1,200 miles**.

### During his law enforcement career, Robbins achieved a reputation as the “man most responsible for bringing law and order to Idaho Territory.”

Born in Maine, he left home at **age 17** following a quarrel with his father and briefly made his way to the California gold fields.

In his mid-20s, Robbins moved to **Idaho** after discovering **gold** in the Salmon River area.

He began working as a lawman in **1864** when Boise County Sheriff Sumner Pinkham appointed him a **deputy**. When Pinkham lost the next election, Robbins joined a stagecoach line and rode shotgun.

### In 1865, Robbins won an appointment as a Deputy U.S. Marshal and spent the next twenty-five years bringing outlaws to justice.

During his law enforcement career, he served as **police chief** of Boise, Ada County Sheriff, and supervisory warden of [Idaho’s Territorial Prison](http://newenglander.smugmug.com/Travel/Idaho-2011/Idaho-April-6-16-2011/16658305_r79mGx/1429113307_7CzwRzp#!i=1429113307&k=7CzwRzp). Robbins was elected to the **Idaho Legislature** from Ada County.

A colonel in the Idaho **militia**, he worked as a scout and Indian fighter and joined the command that followed the fleeing [Nez Perce](http://www.rootsweb.ancestry.com/~idreserv/nphist.html) across the Idaho mountains.

The lawman also became a **local hero** for his role in the **Bannock War of 1878** and for surviving several close encounters with **death**. Robbins owned a **cattle ranch** where he also raised thoroughbred racehorses. He also founded a temperance lodge in Ada County.

### Robbins feared no one, a point illustrated by the editors of True West Magazine in True Tales and Amazing Legends of the Old West.

For example, on the day before he became a Boise deputy, a group of **Confederate sympathizers** gathered in a local saloon on July 4, 1864.

They vowed not to tolerate a Yankee singing **“The Star-Spangled Banner”** during the day.

According to the story, **Robbins** walked into the saloon, hoisted himself atop a billiard table to get everyone’s attention, and **drew** his two pistols.

### He glared at the crowd and sang the song in a loud voice, then holstered the pistols, jumped down to the floor, and left.

**No one** said or did anything.

The well-respected lawman, considered the toughest in Idaho, **died on May 1, 1908**, of a heart attack at the age of 72.

At the time, he was still on the job, working as a **traveling guard** for prisoners.
