JEROME, Idaho —

More than 13,000 Japanese Americans passed through the Minidoka War Relocation Center during World War II. Most were American citizens – none had been convicted of a crime.

In 1942, they were forced from their homes and brought to a remote stretch of southern Idaho, where they lived behind barbed wire.

But the [story of Minidoka](https://www.ktvb.com/video/news/local/208/echoes-of-minidoka-remembering-a-dark-chapter-in-american-history/277-24388803-f594-4a0f-a89b-4165ff546069) is more than a chapter in American history. It is a reminder of what can happen when fear, prejudice and government power collide and why the echoes of that history still matter today.

As the country continues to debate immigration, national security, government authority and who is seen as belonging, survivors warn the lessons of Minidoka remain deeply relevant.

### The Timeline That Led to Incarceration

**December 1941 | Pearl Harbor**

On Dec. 7, 1941, Imperial Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, bringing the United States into WWII.

The attack fueled fear and suspicion toward Japanese Americans, particularly those living on the West Coast.

But anti-Asian sentiment in the United States existed long before Pearl Harbor.

“There’s a lot of people who like to think that the Japanese American incarceration began with the Imperial Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor,” said Hanako Wakatsuki-Chong, executive director of the Japanese American Museum of Oregon. “But a lot of the anti-Asian sentiment existed prior to that and helped lead to the conditions that led to the forced removal of Japanese Americans on the West Coast.”

**August 1942 | Minidoka opens**

In August 1942, the Minidoka War Relocation Center opened in southern Idaho.

Many of those sent to Minidoka came from the Seattle and Portland areas.

At its peak, nearly 10,000 people lived at the camp. More than 13,000 people passed through Minidoka during its operation.

Thousands of Japanese Americans arrived as families — parents, children and grandparents — and were forced to build new lives in a place they never chose to call home.

**1943 | Life at Minidoka**

There was no single experience of life at Minidoka.

For children, memories could include school, games and time spent with friends. For older generations, the experience often carried the weight of losing their homes, businesses, communities and freedom.

“There’s a variety of experience – there’s no one narrative that tells this experience,” Wakatsuki-Chong said.

The contrast is part of what makes Minidoka's history complicated. Children could find moments of normalcy in a place where their families had been forcibly incarcerated.

**1944 | Mitsuye Endo challenges incarceration**

As the war continued, Japanese Americans challenged the government's authority to keep them incarcerated.

One of them was Mitsuye Endo, who had been incarcerated at the Topaz camp in Utah.

Endo challenged her continued detention, arguing that she had been denied due process.

In December 1944, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in *Ex parte Endo* that the government could not continue to detain a loyal American citizen.

By then, the federal government had already announced plans to close the camps.

**October 1945 | Minidoka closes**

Minidoka closed in October 1945, months after the end of World War II.

For many families, leaving the camp did not mean returning to the lives they had before incarceration.

Some had lost their homes, businesses and savings.

The government provided returning families with $25 and a one-way train ticket to help them begin rebuilding their lives.

For many, that was the extent of the assistance they received.

**1988 | Federal apology and reparations**

More than four decades after the camps closed, the federal government formally acknowledged the injustice.

In 1988, President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act, offering an official apology and authorizing reparations for surviving Japanese Americans who had been incarcerated during World War II.

“We gather here today to right a grave wrong,” Reagan said at the signing ceremony. “We must recognize that the internment of Japanese Americans was just that — a mistake.”

The law marked a significant step toward acknowledging the harm caused by the government's wartime policy.

**2001 | Minidoka becomes national historic site**

In 2001, Minidoka became a national historic site through a presidential proclamation under President Bill Clinton.

The designation helped preserve the site and its history for future generations.

In the years since, former incarcerates, their families and others have returned to Minidoka to remember and honor the community that was forced to live there.

For some descendants, those pilgrimages have also become a way to reconnect with a history that was not always discussed within their families.

“As an adult, I started to find these pockets of Japanese Americans and be able to explore my identity through these pilgrimages and provide a sense of healing, because my family did not talk about it,” Wakatsuki-Chong said.

More than eight decades later, Minidoka remains a place where history is remembered and where generations continue to ask what the past can teach us about protecting civil liberties today.

### Stories that Remain

The story of Minidoka did not end when the camp closed. For many Japanese Americans, the years that followed meant rebuilding lives disrupted by forced removal. Some returned home. Others started over somewhere new. And for many families, the memories of incarceration were passed down from one generation to the next.

**Born at Minidoka**

Karen was born inside the Minidoka camp on June 26, 1943.

Her parents and extended family including her grandparents, aunts and uncles were living together in the same barracks.

“I was born (in) Block 13, Barrack Seven, June 26, 1943,” Karen recalled.

She was too young to remember life inside the camp.

But its history was part of her family.

A farmer eventually offered Karen’s father a job, allowing the family to leave Minidoka when she was 18 months old.

Her parents rarely talked about what had happened.

Karen said she grew up hearing occasional references to “camp,” but as a child, she understood the word differently.

“I did not know what the incarceration really was until I volunteered to the Manzanar pilgrimage,” she said.

It was there, talking with former incarcerates, that Karen began to understand what her family had experienced.

She returned home and confronted her mother about why they had never been told.

Her mother’s answer stayed with her: They did not need to know. Those were the lost years, she said, and the family’s goal was to make sure the children did not have to experience anything like it.

As Karen learned more, she also learned about the pain her family had endured.

Her grandmother once described the way they were treated as being like animals.

![](https://media.ktvb.com/assets/KTVB/images/acbdc38f-fe0e-4dd6-bf69-51c4e96d1365/20260812T174850/acbdc38f-fe0e-4dd6-bf69-51c4e96d1365_1920x1080.jpg)

Credit: AP Photo/Lindsey Wasson

An attendee attaches an "ema" to a symbolic barbed wire display during a pilgrimage closing ceremony at Minidoka National Historic Site, July 9, 2023.

That history became more personal as Karen grew older, and as she watched younger generations begin to ask questions.

One conversation with a young family member especially stayed with her.

The child asked where the people who had done “the bad thing” to their family were.

Then came a question Karen said stopped her in her tracks:

“But Auntie Karen, we’re still Japanese.”

Karen hugged her.

“It's because you're Japanese that we have to go there,” she told her.

For Karen, that moment captured why the stories of Minidoka must continue to be told.

“People need to know this history because they need to know what the Japanese Americans went through,” she said.

And, she said, the lesson goes beyond Japanese American history.

“We're not Japanese American. We're not whatever Mexican, Black. I would, you know, I think that the first thing we need to acknowledge is our humanity.”

**Rebuilding in Oregon**

Jeanne Shioshi was 19 when her family was forced from their home in Redmond, Oregon, and sent to Minidoka.

Her mother had one priority throughout the experience: keeping the family together.

“My mother always said, ‘our family has to stay together,’ so we always stayed together,” Jeanne said.

Minidoka functioned much like a small town, with schools, stores and other facilities.

But it was surrounded by barbed wire and armed guards.

Jeanne's daughter, Judy Shioshi, said describing Minidoka as a “relocation camp” can obscure what it really was.

“Relocation camp? Don't you just think of summer camp or something fun? It's really a misnomer. It was prison,” Judy said. “And the only saving grace was it was prison with your family and people that you love.”

Despite the conditions, Jeanne remembers how people responded.

She saw people work, build communities and try to create better lives instead of allowing incarceration to define them.

“Even the camps, I think it was kind of interesting to see when I look back, how everybody worked instead of being negative about things and saying, ‘Feel sorry for themselves,’” Jeanne said. “They worked on getting on for better times.”

Jeanne, now 103, has carried that perspective throughout her life.

![](https://media.ktvb.com/assets/KGW/images/a567480b-586d-47b7-804c-a47661e3d63f/20260811T041954/a567480b-586d-47b7-804c-a47661e3d63f_1920x1080.jpg)

Credit: KGW

Last year, she and her family returned to Minidoka on a pilgrimage.

The visit brought back memories of discomfort and hardship, but also memories of resilience.

For Judy, remembering Minidoka is more than looking backward.

“So that they can remember and not repeat,” she said.

She sees parallels between the past and the present, saying the history of Japanese American incarceration should serve as a warning about what can happen when people are detained because of who they are.

Jeanne, meanwhile, remains focused on the future.

“I think we've come quite a long way,” she said. “Of course, we can go a lot more.”

And at 103, she still has one more lesson to share:

“If you're all the same, wouldn't that be a dull community?”

**Returning Home to Washington**

Fujiko Gardner was a child growing up on her family’s farm in Fife, Washington, when the attack on Pearl Harbor changed her life.

She remembers hearing the news on the radio and being told that Japanese Americans would have to leave their homes.

Gardner packed a small metal suitcase with clothes and belongings before her family made the journey to the Puyallup Assembly Center, where thousands of Japanese Americans from the Seattle area were temporarily held at the fairgrounds.

“I’ve always looked forward to the fair because of their hamburger,” Gardner recalled. “It was a shock to see the mass of Japanese people and to see all the soldiers in their uniform carrying a rifle.”

From Puyallup, Gardner and her family were sent by train to Minidoka in southern Idaho.

Coming from the green landscape of Washington, Gardner remembers the dry Idaho landscape and rows of black barracks.

“It was a sad, sad train ride,” she said.

At Minidoka, Gardner said incarceration felt like being in jail. Families had little privacy, and the harsh conditions took a toll.

Her father also changed dramatically during their time in camp, leaving the family concerned for his well-being.

After the war, Gardner’s family rebuilt their lives in Washington.

Decades later, she began returning to Minidoka with her granddaughter, Kelsea. They have attended eight pilgrimages together.

![](https://media.ktvb.com/assets/KTVB/images/a0ae3cff-82f6-4b74-8eac-9edf179ad041/20260812T175820/a0ae3cff-82f6-4b74-8eac-9edf179ad041_1920x1080.jpg)

Credit: AP Photo/Lindsey Wasson

FILE - A reconstructed entry guard tower is silhouetted by the sunset at Minidoka National Historic Site, July 8, 2023, in Jerome, Idaho.

For Kelsea, those visits transformed Minidoka from her grandmother’s history into her own.

“I’m a quarter Japanese, so my only connection to the Japanese American community was through my grandma walking that space together,” Kelsea said. “It might have just been my grandmother’s history, but now that we’ve experienced together, it’s our history and it’s my history.”

Vincent Schleitwiler, who teaches American Ethnic Studies at the University of Washington, said the experiences of Japanese Americans during World War II remain important when examining how the country treats communities today.

Schleitwiler, Gardner, and Kelsea sees parallels between the incarceration of Japanese Americans and current immigration enforcement, including the use of the Alien Enemies Act.

“It’s just unbelievable to me what immigrants are faced with today,” Kelsea said. “All of the experiences of the World War II Japanese American incarceration leave me with just questioning: What does it mean to be an American, and how do you prove your Americanness?”

For Gardner, remembering the past is also a call to action.

She says people should not lose hope or accept the dehumanization and scapegoating of immigrants.

“If history repeats itself over and over, we need to make sure that it will never happen again,” she said.

**History That Still Echoes Today**

Today, much of Minidoka is quiet.

Most of the original barracks are gone, and the rebuilt guard towers now stand as reminders of what once happened here.

But the camp’s history remains.

Minidoka is a reminder of what can happen when fear, uncertainty and prejudice influence public policy.

That history also continues to shape conversations happening today about immigration, national security, government authority and due process.

Hanako Wakatsuki-Chong, executive director of the Japanese American Museum of Oregon, says she sees similarities between the experiences of Japanese Americans during World War II and the rhetoric surrounding some communities today.

“There is so much remnants of what's happening today with what happened to my community,” Wakatsuki-Chong said. “You're watching the same thing happen over and over again, almost using the same kind of footprint.”

She acknowledges that some people may argue the situations cannot be compared.

Her response is to look at the history.

“If you do your research, it is the same. It's just a different community being targeted with the same rhetoric,” she said.

For Wakatsuki-Chong, remembering Minidoka is not about avoiding difficult conversations. It's about confronting them.

“If we don't sit with that discomfort, we could never acknowledge it and move on,” she said. “And that's part of the healing process.”

The lessons of Minidoka remain because its history is not meant to be forgotten.

“We shouldn't be ashamed that we have these hard histories here,” Wakatsuki-Chong said. “But we should be ashamed if we want to forget it.”
