Artificial Intelligence “training” Poses New Threat in Already Onerous Work-for-Hire Contracts in Photojournalism

by NPPA • April 16, 2026

For decades, freelance photojournalists have pushed back on publishers’ desire to own copyrights through work-for-hire contracts, but the potential for “training” in artificial intelligence is raising new concerns as work for hire affects authorship, control, and the future integrity of visual journalism. This has been highlighted by a recent contract offered to photographers by the company that publishes the Wall Street Journal.

Freelance “ [work made for hire](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf),” or “work for hire” contracts create a legal status where a company is considered the author of the photos and video. “When a photographer or videographer is an employee, the law says that their employer is considered the author and thus the owner of the copyright. But when they are a freelancer, the default is that the freelancer owns the copyright” Alicia Calzada, NPPA Deputy General Counsel explained. “A work-for-hire freelance contract gives a client the benefit of treating the freelancer as an employee without paying the benefits of employment, such as health insurance.”

Freelancers often push back against copyright transfers because they rely on copyright ownership for added income from licensing valuable images, even years later. While some clients have listened and been open to change, others have not.

“These changes strike at the heart of authorship and control of the intellectual property of the photojournalists,” said NPPA President Alex Garcia. “Photojournalists need to understand what rights they are giving up, and the possible detrimental impact on their career and the entire community. Intellectual capital has the power to raise the economic prospects of families for generations. The damage of giving up this form of capital can’t be underscored enough. ”

**Expanding Scope of Work-for-Hire**

The inclusion of the images in clients’ AI “training” datasets under a work for hire agreement takes consequences even further. AI training signals a troubling shift in how news photos may be used long after initial publication. In today’s landscape, training artificial intelligence systems might lead to downstream applications not anticipated in assignment agreements.

**AI, Ethics, and Public Trust**

From the perspective of accuracy and truth, NPPA President Alex Garcia emphasized that “Allowing AI systems to train on real news photos allows programs to create even more realistic deepfakes that falsely represent historic events. This means the work of photojournalists would be contributing to the fog of disinformation and undermining their mission of truth and accuracy.”

We saw this recently in an AI-generated image that falsely purported to show the rescue of an American airman in Iran. The image was recirculated on social media by prominent politicians. “The ethical foundation of visual journalism depends on context, accuracy, and trust,” said NPPA General Counsel Mickey H. Osterreicher. “When images are reused outside their original purpose or without clear understanding by the creator, it raises real concerns about how those images may be interpreted, manipulated, or relied upon.”

**NPPA’s Position**

NPPA underscores that as small business owners, photographers should consider the impact of work-for-hire and copyright transfer agreements with clients, with a clear understanding of their implications.

When contracts also include broad indemnification clauses, it adds another layer of risk, because without the copyright, a photographer cannot control how the image is used and yet they might have liability for the misuse of the photograph.

“Photographers have to do what is best for them and for their business,” Garcia said. “But these are very consequential choices which photojournalists ignore at their peril. Photojournalists should go into them fully informed about what impact their decisions could have on their colleagues, subjects and their own economic viability.”

As technology and business models continue to evolve, the intersection of journalism, copyright, and AI will remain a critical issue.

**Some lesser-understood legal issues related to work for hire**

A work-for-hire agreement can affect the authorship, copyright ownership, copyright term, and termination rights in that work. Under a work for hire, the employer or commissioning party is considered the “author” of the photograph, instead of the actual photographer.

- Outside of the employment context, the Copyright Act only recognizes “work for hire” in a limited set of situations, including a work commissioned for use in a collective work; an audiovisual work (video), and [a few other circumstances](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf). If a work-for-hire contract is signed, but it doesn’t involve those specific situations, it doesn’t qualify as a work for hire. There still could be other ways the copyright might be transferred, but it’s not a work for hire.
- Freelance still photographers who register their copyright with the Copyright Office do so almost exclusively under the “group registration” option. When the photographer is no longer considered the “author” because of a work-for-hire contract,, the photographer won't be able to register those images in the same group registration as their other photos because of [the registration requirements for work-for-hire images](https://www.copyright.gov/eco/help/group/grpph.html).
- A work-for-hire contract also can result in a freelancer becoming an employee, depending on the state. For example, California labor Labor Code § 3351.5 explicitly [defines “employee”](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/labor-code/lab-sect-3351-5/) to include a photographer (and other creative professionals) working under a work-for-hire contract. The client becomes an employer under California law, and subject to a variety of state-law requirements.
- Under U.S. copyright law, a visual creator who transfers their copyright via an “assignment of copyright” can actually terminate [the copyright transfer,](https://www.copyright.gov/recordation/termination.html) and regain control of their image thirty-five years later. That’s not the case with a work for hire. Because a work for hire makes the client the author, photographers cannot terminate the transfer of copyright.

**In Short – Understanding Work-for-Hire and AI Licensing**

**What is “Work Made for Hire”?**

A work made for hire means:

- The employer or client is considered the author, and owns the copyright. If you are an employee this is the default.
- If you are a freelancer, a signed written agreement is required to create a work for hire
- The photographer does not retain any rights to their image in a work for hire situation
- The client can control how the images are used, transferred or modified.

**Why It Matters**

If your work is classified as work for hire:

- You may not be able to resell or license your images and there might be limits on your own use of your photos
- You may not control how your images are reused
- Your work could be used in ways you did not anticipate
- Even if a joint copyright is transferred back it affects the copyright registration process and the [length of the copyright](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ15a.pdf)

**What About AI?**

Some companies are licensing large collections of images as data sets to train AI systems. This could include:

- Generating new images, including fake news images
- Analyzing visual data
- Training recognition systems

**Questions to Ask Before Signing**

- Who owns the copyright?
- Can I reuse my images without permission from my client?
- Can my client license my work to third parties?
- Can my client include the work in AI data sets or future uses?
- If I don’t own the work then why should I indemnify how the company uses the work?

**Bottom Line**

Understanding your contract is critical. These decisions can affect both your income and how your work is used over time.
