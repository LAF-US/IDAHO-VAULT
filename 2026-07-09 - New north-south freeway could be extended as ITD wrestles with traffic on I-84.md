As more and more people move to the Treasure Valley, commuters are feeling the squeeze.

The area is predicted to grow by more than half a million people in the next 25 years. By 2055, if no significant roadway projects are made on state roadways, it could take 30% longer to get from the Blacks Creek Interchange to Caldwell on I-84, according to traffic models.

These numbers were calculated assuming local connectivity projects, such as the 5-lane extension of Lake Hazel Rd. from Orchard St. to Eisenman Rd., will proceed.

The Idaho Transportation Department wants to avoid this increase in congestion. The solution, it says, is to build beltways and expand the area’s connectivity. And Highway 16 may be the ticket.

## The process

![](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/itd-pivots-beltway-focus-looks-at-pushing-highway-16-past-i-84-Screenshot-2026-07-0-1.jpeg)

[As BoiseDev reported,](https://boisedev.com/news/2025/06/04/treasure-valley-betway-routes-kuna-meridian-i-84-boise-highway-16-2/) ITD has been working on the South Ada and Canyon County Connectivity Study to look at creating a “major corridor” in the area and what type of roads would be most needed and most viable to ease traffic in a growing valley served by one major freeway.

“I wouldn’t say so much that our goal is no congestion,” ITD District 3 Engineer Jason Brinkman said. “Our goal is to make sure that we can continue to enable the growth and prosperity that Idaho has enjoyed in the last few decades.”

![Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/6f251bf1-00e6-48d7-8365-570820fe299b.gif)

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

Beltways and expanding overall connectivity in the area, Brinkman said, have been shown to be the preferred method over a “bypass” strategy. I-84 does not run strictly east-west; as it crosses the Treasure Valley, it moves from the southeast to the northwest and vice versa. Any bypass constructed would travel a longer distance than the main corridor.

ITD has been meeting with the Community Planning Association of Southwest Idaho and several officials from cities in both Ada and Canyon counties for the past several months as part of the connectivity study. The organization contracted with Kittleson and Associates to gather projected growth data, conduct traffic modeling, and determine which areas have the greatest demand and which type of roadway makes the most sense. ITD’s contract with Kittleson came with an over $300,000 price tag.

The study’s boundaries include I-84 to the north, the Blacks Creek interchange to the east, Highway 45 to the west, and Kuna-Mora Rd. to the south. Due to discontinuities on Kuna Mora Rd., the southern limit is represented as a polygon rather than a straight line. According to Kittleson and Associates’ memorandum, of the approximately 660,000 trips on an average weekday originating in the study area, about 53% remain within the study area, while 47% leave.

![Morrison Center - July 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/37e7ef09-855c-4668-ba54-b9eb50afe557.png)

Morrison Center - July 2026 - 2:1

“This shows a large dependency and regional connectivity between South Ada and Canyon County,” the memorandum said.

A finalized report of the study was planned for August, but Brinkman said this could be pushed back a month or two to allow the department to prepare two different population scenarios — one that accounts for high growth, and one that accounts for low growth.

Last year, the focus of the connectivity study was largely on a southern beltway, likely around Kuna Mora Rd., with potential connections at Meridian Rd. and Highway 16 — both extended southward.

According to Brinkman, while the department is still looking at the Meridian Rd. extension and a southern beltway for the area, the main focus of the study has shifted. Now, extending Highway 16 south of I-84 is ITD’s new priority project.

“The 16 extension had even more need than we anticipated it would, but perhaps not surprisingly, because there is a lot of growth in South Meridian and Nampa and Canyon County generally,” Brinkman said. “It makes sense that it would draw a lot of traffic. So, in hindsight, we were glad we did this, because it really did shine a light on that need.”

High-growth models show that the Canyon County and Kuna area populations will increase by 70% by 2055. Southeast Ada County is projected to grow by 340% under the same growth scenarios.

Brinkman said that while the study shows a need for the Meridian Rd. extension, the southern beltway near Kuna Mora Rd., and the Highway 16 extension by 2055, looking at Highway 16 is ITD’s “first order of business,” in part because the department has local partners who are interested in helping.

## High need, highly complicated

The highway has already been the subject of ITD’s attention for much of the past 20 years as the department has worked to extend it to I-84.

But this highway extension is also the most complicated of the three projects. Brinkamn said this is because the other two roadways have most of their alignment predetermined. However, the best route on Highway 16 still needs to be studied.

“The Highway 16 extension is probably the highest need portion, and also the biggest endeavor, Brinkman said. “That would be the priority route in terms of its traffic need. It’s probably the toughest one in that that would be a mostly new facility, whereas something like the Kuna Mora, you’ve got some existing roads that could be utilized if that were the selected route.”

Brinkman said any path by McDermott Rd., Robinson Rd., and Black Cat Rd. could become the route for the extension. This alignment study, as well as an environmental impact study, is the next step in any plans to move the highway extension forward.

“There’s probably a couple-mile-wide swath that will look at where the road would go specifically,” he said.

Alignment and environmental impact studies are also needed for Meridian Rd. and the Kuna Mora beltway. These projects are all considered “long-range” and would take years to complete. For example, the current Highway 16 expansion began planning in 2006.

As for what type of roadway the new Highway 16 extension would be, Brinkman said the study determined that a freeway, with designated on- and off-ramps, would be ideal for at least a portion of the road south of I-84.

## What about funding?

Idaho has faced challenges balancing its budget in recent years, and government funding has taken a hit as a result. This makes finding state funding for large infrastructure projects a challenge.

[As the Idaho State Journal reported,](https://www.idahostatejournal.com/news/local/yellowstone-garrett-corridor-south-fifth-i-15-ramps-among-pocatello-projects-delayed-in-1-billion/article_48a14b31-0e9b-445a-bb5b-d0d43b75183a.html?fbclid=IwY2xjawSznadleHRuA2FlbQIxMQBicmlkETFWUUVMVWlDa29CSkRvU1E2c3J0YwZhcHBfaWQQMjIyMDM5MTc4ODIwMDg5MgABHnTU12kjziA4B_cX_dSmoPyvgA-pBEYLN8faIaWACaqhCE3Daspq4MtGu3I__aem_jCoqb-Izr-h_AmTASLjO2g#tncms-source=article-nav-next) ITD recently delayed over $1 billion in planned road construction projects across the state after the legislature cut the department’s funding in the most recent legislative session.

One line item that was not delayed was $2 million to go toward the needed alignment study for the new Highway 16 extension. The funding is written into the department’s Idaho Transportation Investment Program, [which recently went out for public comment.](https://itd.idaho.gov/funding/itip/)

ITD and COMPASS have also applied for two different federal grants to help fund more studies of the connectivity projects, but both grant applications were denied.

Brinkman said other methods for funding large-scale projects like these include pursuing public-private partnerships, bonds, and federal revenue that could come from a future highway bill.

Brinkman also said the current highway bill is scheduled to expire this fall, and the federal government is tasked with replacing it. When this happens, the states often get new funding opportunities for roadway projects.
