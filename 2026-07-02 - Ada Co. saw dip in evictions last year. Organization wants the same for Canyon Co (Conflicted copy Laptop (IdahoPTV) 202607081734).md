A new report is shedding light on evictions in the Treasure Valley.

Jesse Tree, a Boise nonprofit organization that offers assistance to families facing eviction, released its 2025 Treasure Valley Eviction Report in April. The report highlights some of the factors driving evictions, where higher frequencies of evictions are happening, and how the organization’s work is preventing evictions.

Jesse Tree leaders told BoiseDev that they are exploring expanding the organization’s services in Canyon County.

## High home values, high rents

The Treasure Valley’s lower-income residents are still bearing the brunt of the area’s housing crisis.

According to the eviction report, in 2025, the median price of a home in Ada County was $541,000, about 50% higher than the median for the U.S. While the home prices are far above the national median, the local median income is only about 12% higher than the rest of the country, the report said.

That difference makes it difficult for residents to purchase a home. In March 2025, only 6% of Ada County real estate listings qualified as affordable for people making $75,000 per year or less, the report said. At the same time, some people who are priced out of homes can still often afford higher rents than lower-income renters, which drives the prices of rent up, the report said. Home prices are also driving up property taxes, an expense that landlords often pass on to tenants in the form of higher rent, the report said.

Declan North, an advocacy fellow with Jesse Tree who authored the report, said that though home values in the Treasure Valley have stabilized somewhat in recent years, the benefit has not necessarily extended to renters.

“As the rate of change starts to cool off, it doesn’t change the fact that there’s just a monumental gap between what people can pay and those home values,” North said. “I’m sure there are some positive consequences to home values starting to stagnate, but to me at least, it just speaks to the fact that those prices have been stretched as much as they can within the market.”

Jesse Tree Executive Director Evan Stewart said that as rents stay high, middle-income families — those who earn too much to receive government benefits, but not enough to comfortably afford rents or purchase homes — feel squeezed. A general rule of thumb is that about 30% of one’s gross income should go toward housing costs. Instead, evictions that Jesse Tree intervenes in tend to affect working-class families, such as those in the service industry. Oftentimes, many such families pay over 50% of their income toward housing, Stewart said. Another term for these households is Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed (ALICE).

“People doing working-class jobs can’t afford to sustain their rent if an unexpected financial emergency happens,” such as a vehicle needing repair or missing work to stay home with a sick child, Stewart said.

Sometimes, tenants may withhold rent if a landlord has not been responsive in making repairs to a home. When they receive an eviction notice, they may believe that, by allowing their case to go to court, a third-party judge would evaluate the home’s habitability.

But Jesse Tree’s report said a case going before a judge is no guarantee of a favorable outcome for the tenant. While withholding rent to motivate repairs is legal in some states, in Idaho, there are no circumstances under which a tenant can legally withhold rent, North said. Even if a tenant has ample photo evidence of inhospitable conditions, such photos can’t be considered in Idaho possession disputes unless the landlord knew the home was uninhabitable prior to the tenant signing a lease, the report said.

Instead, evictions can deepen a family’s financial hardship and may not address subpar living conditions. Landlords may pass on court costs and attorneys’ fees to the tenant, and tenants may leave court with no guarantee from their landlord that repairs will be made, the report said.

## What does local eviction data show?

Though Ada and Canyon counties are home to about 40% of Idaho’s population, they account for about 60% of the state’s evictions, the report said.

In 2025, there were 1,349 eviction filings in Ada County, a decrease from over 1,500 in 2024 and 2023, the report said.

Jesse Tree’s report credits the dip with its emergency rental assistance program, and its eviction court assistance program.

Nearly 85% of the county’s evictions occurred in Boise and Meridian.

![](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/jesse-tree-2026-eviction-report-Screenshot-2026-06-1.jpeg)

A figure from Jesse Tree’s 2025 eviction report shows how evictions are distributed in Boise. Map: Courtesy Jesse Tree

In Boise, evictions were concentrated around the downtown area and a northwest-southeast line that runs through districts 1, 2, 3, and 4. The city also saw outlier evictions in District 6, which includes parts of the city north of State St., and in District 1, which includes the western- and southwestern-most parts of the city.

Areas with the highest frequencies of evictions — the same evictor filed more than one eviction in the same area — occurred in some Downtown Boise areas, near Boise State University, and along the northern border between Garden City and Boise, the report said. North said he and staff did the analysis by dividing the city into a city block-sized grid and looking at where there were repeat evictors within the same blocks.

![](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/jesse-tree-2026-eviction-report-Screenshot-2026-06-1-2.jpeg)

In Meridian, evictions were more evenly distributed throughout the city, with some hotspots sharing proximity to Interstate 84 and areas with higher-density zoning. However, the report emphasized that higher density zoning does not lead to more evictions.

![](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/jesse-tree-2026-eviction-report-Screenshot-2026-06-1-1.jpeg)

“Instead, evictions are more prevalent where there are more renters, and renters are more likely to be concentrated in more densely zoned areas,” the report said.

Canyon County saw 666 evictions in 2025, a 10% increase from 2024, the report said. Filings have more than tripled since 2020 and continue to increase at a steady pace, the report said.

## How does Jesse Tree prevent evictions?

Jesse Tree has two main tools for intervening in eviction cases: emergency rental assistance and eviction court assistance. In 2025, the organization kept 2,400 families housed across both programs, an increase of 297% from 2020, according to the report. North said Jesse Tree’s case managers are serving increasingly more clients per case manager, with each case manager serving an average of 130 families per year.

Tenants who have received a three-day notice to pay rent or leave but haven’t been served court paperwork can apply for emergency rental assistance with Jesse Tree, the report said. The organization provided a total of $1.28 million to 567 households through that program in 2025. The majority of Jesse Tree’s interventions, both by number of households and the amount of funding, happen through this program, Stewart said.

The other portion of the organization’s interventions happen when a person or household has been served court paperwork. In Ada County, a judge can guide a case toward mediation, though either party can refuse it and go to trial, North said. When a case goes to mediation, Jesse Tree’s eviction court assistance program can provide third-party mediation between tenants and landlords or their legal representatives, North said.

Stewart said that Jesse Tree is able to intervene in 75% of evictions in Ada County, with 80% of cases not resulting in default. North said that means tenants avoid an eviction judgment on their record and are either able to remain in their units or move out on an agreed-upon timeline.

Canyon County, on the other hand, does not currently have a mediation program. That means that Jesse Tree has a narrower window to intervene in cases prior to them being heard by a judge and is only able to intervene in about 8% of eviction cases in the county, Stewart said.

North said that the county had a mediation program during the COVID-19 pandemic when court proceedings took place via video conferencing. The county announced it was discontinuing the program in 2023, [as BoiseDev reported](https://boisedev.com/news/2023/06/29/canyon-county-eviction-mediation-program/).

To help more people in Canyon County, Stewart said he is working on talking with the trial court administrator and judges to discuss the benefits of the organization’s work. He said that a mediation program in Ada County has offered mediation services in Canyon County for free. The program has a pool of volunteer mediators.

“What I’m working on is really to find opportunities to meet with judges and convince them that our model and a partnership with the county is a really good recipe for success, and it’s a really cost-effective way to prevent people from losing their homes and having an eviction on their record, which makes it harder to find affordable, stable housing in the community,” Stewart said.

So far, North said decisionmakers have responded with some “good interest” on creating a new mediation program.

North said that part of Jesse Tree’s success in Ada County’s program comes from the county scheduling eviction hearings on the same days and times each week. This allows Jesse Tree staff to be available to help when cases move from the courtroom to mediation in conference rooms. North said that in Canyon County, eviction cases are scheduled across more days and times, and there is currently no regularly scheduled time that eviction cases are heard. However, Jesse Tree is interested in figuring out ways to work with the court.

“We’re very much willing to see what the needs of the court are right now and to collaborate with them,” North said.

## Preventing homelessness

Stewart said that 95% of people served by the organization have not become homeless five years later. He and North emphasized that Jesse Tree’s work to keep people housed is more cost-effective for the community compared to providing services or securing housing for people experiencing homelessness.

Stewart estimated that it costs Jesse Tree an average of $2,000 to keep a family housed. In contrast, it can cost $20,000 to secure housing for a family that does not have it. Stewart said that the community at large ends up paying for services to care for people experiencing homelessness, such as funding emergency responders, hospital stays, and other services.

“It’s much more costly for the community to react to homelessness rather than preventing homelessness from happening,” he said.
