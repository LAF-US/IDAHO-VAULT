What decides whether a wildfire becomes a manageable burn or a catastrophe is simple: What was standing ready before the wind picked up.

Right now, Idahoans are living through the cost of being left unprepared.

In Spokane County, just minutes from Post Falls, fires recently destroyed hundreds of homes and displaced 60,000 neighbors. Near Weiser, the Tartar Fire burned 138,000 acres. In Owyhee County, a massive blaze exceeding 350,000 acres has scorched critical grazing lands and sage-grouse habitat, leaving ranching families facing devastating losses.

These are not distant headlines. They represent smoke over our towns, lost livelihoods and local volunteer departments stretched beyond their limits.

Our local firefighters are doing heroic work. Departments across the region, including mutual aid from my hometown of Eagle, answered the call. But no rural fire department can handle wind-driven blazes of this magnitude alone. They rely on federal backup, regional coordination and trained surge capacity.

Washington, D.C. has spent years dismantling that safety net.

Last year, I sat with Forest Service workers in Idaho and asked what kept them up at night. Their answer was immediate: they no longer had enough staff to supply relief crews during peak fire season. That crisis extends directly to the Bureau of Land Management (BLM). Unfunded positions, layoffs and forced departures did not just cut headcount; they walked decades of local land knowledge out the door. Today, I know personnel forced to manage workloads meant for six people, alone, in fire country, in August.

When a blaze breaks out, red-carded Forest Service and BLM staff serve as the vital surge capacity needed to relieve exhausted crews. Without them, logistics fail, seasonal firefighters work unsustainable hours, and safety is compromised.

Compounding the problem is administrative red tape that halts common-sense response. When the Big Grass Fire crossed state lines from Oregon into Owyhee County, initial attack efforts were delayed by rigid administrative boundaries. Oregon sits in the Pacific Northwest region, while Idaho falls under the Great Basin region. While fires do not respect state borders, these bureaucratic silos stopped real-time communication when every minute mattered.

Add to this the cancellation of basic equipment grants for the rural volunteer fire departments protecting our smallest towns, all in the name of saving money.

Here is the truth about that math. Washington saved pennies on staffing and grants, and Idahoans paid the price in burned acreage, lost structures and destroyed range.

The politicians who cut those crews and drew those administrative lines will never smell the smoke. Ranching families in Owyhee County and residents across our district are living in it.

We need a representative who understands what government is for: protecting communities during crises no family or town can handle alone.

That means treating wildland fire readiness as a life-or-death priority, not political leverage. It means properly staffing the BLM and Forest Service, eliminating bureaucratic barriers that delay fire response at state borders, and ensuring our volunteer departments have the equipment they need.

Right now, our priority must be supporting the families and crews on the front lines. But when the smoke clears, we must remember what this season taught us, and elect leadership that keeps Idaho protected before the sparks fly.

*Kaylee Peterson is the Democratic candidate for the U.S. House of Representatives in Idaho’s First Congressional District.*
