**Native American religions**,[^1] [^2] [^3] [^4] [^5] **Native American faith** [^6] or **American Indian religions** [^7] [^8] [^9] [^10] [^11] are the [Indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_religion "Indigenous religion") spiritual practices of the [Indigenous peoples of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas "Indigenous peoples of the Americas"). Ceremonial practices involve sacred, spiritual, cultural traditions that can differ slightly among the indigenous groups of the continent. Early European explorers describe some communities, now labelled [Native American tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe_\(Native_American\) "Tribe (Native American)") in the USA and small First Nations or bands in Canada, as each having unique religious practices. Theology may be [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism"), [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"), [henotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism "Henotheism"), [animistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism "Animism"), [shamanistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism "Shamanism"), [pantheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism "Pantheism") or any combination thereof, among others.[^12] [^13] [^14] Traditional beliefs are usually passed down in the [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition") forms of [myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythologies_of_the_Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas "Mythologies of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas"), [oral histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_history "Oral history"), stories, allegories, and principles.[^15] [^16] [^17] Nowadays, as scholars note, many American Natives are having a renewed interest in their own traditions.[^18]

## Overview

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/82296943-SLD-001-050.jpg/250px-82296943-SLD-001-050.jpg)

Totem pole in Vancouver.

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/00/North_American_cultural_areas.png/250px-North_American_cultural_areas.png) ![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/91/South_American_Cultural_Areas.png)

Areas of the Native Americans in the pre-Columbian era in North America (top) and [Mesoamerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica "Mesoamerica") and South America (bottom)

Native American religions were prevalent in the [pre-Columbian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_era "Pre-Columbian era"), including [state religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion "State religion"). A common concept is the [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural "Supernatural") world of [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity"), spirits and wonders, such as the [Algonquian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquian_peoples "Algonquian peoples") *[manitou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manitou "Manitou")* or the [Lakota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakota_people "Lakota people") ʼs *[wakan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakan_Tanka "Wakan Tanka")*,[^19] [^20] [^9] as well as [Great Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Spirit "Great Spirit"),[^21] [Fifth World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_World_\(mythology\) "Fifth World (mythology)"), [world tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_tree "World tree"), and [the red road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_red_road "The red road") among many Indians.[^9]

Before Christian influence occurred,[^9] in most American religions was known High God—a supreme [Creator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity "Creator deity") and Teacher, such as the [Inca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire "Inca Empire") god [Viracocha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viracocha "Viracocha") in South America,[^10] [^22] or [sky deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_deity "Sky deity") /Great Spirit who represents all other spiritual beings, for instance, the [Pawnee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pawnee_people "Pawnee people") god Tirawa in North America.[^23] [^9] [^21] The supreme being is closely associated with the World Tree, or world pole/pillar—the central cultic symbol in the great rites of the main regions especially of Northern America.[^9] Other traditional pre-Modern religious rites were hunting, gathering, [planting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_and_agriculture "Religion and agriculture"), and [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_violence "Religious violence") ceremonies.[^24] Today, many practitioners of Native American religions do not consider religion to be a separate sphere from everyday life and include politics, law, health, education, and environmental stewardship as part of their religious life.[^25]

During the 16th–21st centuries, Native American spirituality had numerous new [indigenist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenism "Indigenism") [revitalization movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revitalization_movement "Revitalization movement") that divided to [fundamentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamentalism "Fundamentalism") and reform.[^26] [^27] [^28]

Generally fundamentalist movements favoured a return to traditional ways and rejected the changes brought by the modern states; in North America they include the [Pueblo Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pueblo_Revolt "Pueblo Revolt") (1680s), the [Shawnee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shawnee "Shawnee") Prophet Movement (1805–1811), the [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee "Cherokee") Prophet Movement (1811–1813), the [Red Stick War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creek_War "Creek War") (1813–1814), White Path's Rebellion (1826), the [Winnebago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ho-Chunk "Ho-Chunk") Prophet Movement (1830–1832), the first Ghost Dance (1869–1870) and the second [Ghost Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_Dance "Ghost Dance") (1889–1890), and the Snake movements among the Cherokee, [Choctaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw "Choctaw"), and [Muscogee Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscogee "Muscogee") Indians during the 1890s.[^28]

Generally, [syncretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_syncretism "Religious syncretism") reform movements include the Yaqui Religion (1500–present), the [Longhouse Religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longhouse_Religion "Longhouse Religion") (1797–present), the Munsee Prophetess Movement (1804–1805), the Kickapoo Prophet Movement (1815–present), the [Cherokee Keetoowah Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keetoowah_Nighthawk_Society "Keetoowah Nighthawk Society") (1858–present), the [Washat Dreamers Religion](#Washat_Dreamers_Religion) (1850–present), the [Indian Shakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Shaker_Church "Indian Shaker Church") (1881–present), the [Native American Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_Church "Native American Church") (1800s–present), the Shoshoni [Sun Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Dance "Sun Dance") (1890–present), the New Tidings religion or *[Wocekiye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wocekiye "Wocekiye")* of the Canadian [Sioux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sioux "Sioux") (1900–present), and Ojibwe Drummer Movement (contemporary).[^28]

Beginning in the 1600s, European Christians, both [Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") and those of various [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism "Protestantism") denominations, sought to convert Native American tribes from their pre-existing beliefs to Christianity. After the [United States gained independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War "American Revolutionary War") in the late 1700s, its government continued to suppress Indigenous practices and promote [forcible conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_conversion#Christianity "Forced conversion"). American and Canadian government agencies and religious organizations often cooperated in these forcible conversion efforts. In many cases, violence was used as a tool of suppression, as in the government's violent eradication of [Ghost Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_Dance "Ghost Dance") and [Sun Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Dance "Sun Dance") practitioners in 1890s.[^29] [^30] Thus, Canada lifted its prohibition against the practice of the Sun Dance full ceremony only in 1951.[^30]

By the turn of the 20th century, the American government began to turn to less violent means of suppressing Native American religious beliefs. A series of federal laws was passed banning traditional Indigenous practices such as feasts, [Sun Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Dance "Sun Dance") ceremonies and the use of the [sweat lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweat_lodge "Sweat lodge"), among others.[^31] This government persecution and prosecution officially continued until 1978 with the passage of the [American Indian Religious Freedom Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Indian_Religious_Freedom_Act "American Indian Religious Freedom Act") (AIRFA), although it has been argued that the AIRFA had little real effect on the protection of Native religious beliefs.[^32] [^33]

Another significant system of religious suppression was the removal of Native American children from their families into a system of government-funded and church-operated [American Indian boarding schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Indian_boarding_schools "American Indian boarding schools") (also known as residential schools). In these schools, Native children were forced through violence and oppression to learn European Christian beliefs, the values of mainstream white culture, and the English language, while simultaneously being forbidden to speak their own languages and practice their own cultural beliefs. This system of forcible conversion and suppression of Indigenous languages and cultures continued through the 1970s.[^34] [^35] [^36]

Some non-Native anthropologists estimate membership in traditional Native American religions in the 21st century to be about 9000 people.[^37] [^38] Since Native Americans practicing traditional ceremonies do not usually have public organizations or membership rolls, these "members" estimates are likely substantially lower than the actual numbers of people who participate in traditional ceremonies.[^39] Native American spiritual leaders also note that these academic estimates substantially underestimate the numbers of participants because a century of US Federal government persecution and prosecutions of traditional ceremonies caused believers to practice their religions in secrecy. Many adherents of traditional spiritual ways also attend Christian services, at least some of the time, which can also affect statistics. Since the 80 years of those prior legal persecutions ended with AIRFA, some sacred sites in the United States are now [protected areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_area "Protected area") under law.[^40]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Klawock-dancing.jpg/250px-Klawock-dancing.jpg)

The living in the [North American Arctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Arctic "North American Arctic") and [sub-Arctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subarctic "Subarctic") [Alaska Natives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Natives "Alaska Natives") and many [Indigenous peoples in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_in_Canada "Indigenous peoples in Canada") mainly practice [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer "Hunter-gatherer") religious cults. Traditional Alaskan shamanism involve mediation between people and spirits, souls, and other immortal beings.[^41] [^42] [^14] [^43]

### Western North America

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ec/Doctor%27s_Headdress_%28guk-tsu-shua%29%2C_Pomo_%28Native_American%29%2C_1906-1907C.E.%2C_08.491.8952.jpg/250px-Doctor%27s_Headdress_%28guk-tsu-shua%29%2C_Pomo_%28Native_American%29%2C_1906-1907C.E.%2C_08.491.8952.jpg)

Pomo doctor's headdress

Western North America includes such cultural areas as Northwest Coast,[^44] Plateau, [Great Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Basin "Great Basin"), and a region of the [Indigenous peoples of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_California "Indigenous peoples of California").[^45]

#### Earth Lodge Religion

The Earth Lodge Religion was founded in northern California and southern Oregon tribes such as the [Wintun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wintun "Wintun"). It spread to tribes such as the [Achomawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achomawi "Achomawi"), [Shasta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shasta_\(tribe\) "Shasta (tribe)"), and [Siletz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siletz_\(tribe\) "Siletz (tribe)"), to name a few. It was also known as the "Warm House Dance" among the [Pomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomo "Pomo"). It predicted occurrences similar to those predicted by the Ghost Dance, such as the return of ancestors or the world's end. The Earth Lodge Religion impacted the later religious practice, the Dream Dance, belonging to the [Klamath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klamath_people "Klamath people") and the [Modoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modoc_people "Modoc people").[^45] [^28] [^46]

#### Washat Dreamers Religion

The Washat Dreamers Religion,[^28] also called the Waashat Religion/Faith, the Waasaní (Washani) Religion, Seven Drum Religion, and the Longhouse religion (separate from the Iroquois [Longhouse religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longhouse_Religion "Longhouse Religion")) is a faith still persisting in some modern Native communities.[^28] Washani, meaning "dancers" or "worship",[^47] was a reform response to pressures from American settlers at the time for Natives to relocate or become "civilized". The [Wanapum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wanapum "Wanapum") Indian [Smohalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smohalla "Smohalla") (c. 1815 – 1895) originally built the religion in 1850 in the [Columbia River region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okanogan_County,_Washington "Okanogan County, Washington") of modern-day [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_\(state\) "Washington (state)"), and over time it has spread across the [Pacific Northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest "Pacific Northwest"). Smohalla claimed that he had received visions in his dreams, where he had visited the spirit world and had been sent back to teach his people.[^47] He preached a reform return to the original way of life of hunter-gatherer lifestyle as opposed to agriculturalism, before the influence of colonizers, emphasizing abstinence from alcohol and cleansing themselves of white influences. Smohalla was sometimes called Yuyunipitqana, meaning "Shouting Mountain", because it was said that his wisdom came from a mountain speaking within his soul.[^48]

The religion combined elements of Christianity with Native beliefs, teaching similar origin stories as Catholicism and holding Sunday as a holy day, while still ultimately pushing away from the so-called civilized man that white people idealized.[^48] Despite this, Smohalla rejected the Christian Puritanical ideal of a strong work ethic and plowing the land.[^49] Two notable quotes of Smohalla's "You ask me to plough the ground! Shall I take a knife and tear my mother's bosom?" [^48] and "My young men shall never work. Men who work can not dream, and wisdom comes to us in dreams," demonstrates these tenets. The Native-inspired elements of the religion involved the Waashat Dance, a dance which involves seven drummers, a salmon feast, use of eagle and swan feathers and a sacred song sung every seventh day.[^50]

The Dreamer Faith foreshadowed the later Ghost Dances of the plains peoples, another religion which sought to rid Natives of white people and their influences through peaceful religion.[^47] These kinds of religions made it difficult to assimilate or control the tribes by the United States, as the U.S. was trying to convert the [Plains tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plains_Indians "Plains Indians") from hunter-gatherers to farmers; farming was believed to be more civilized and a better use of the land.[^49] They wanted to remake the Natives, but found a problem with those who followed the Waashat Religion: "Their model of a man is an Indian; They aspire to be Indian and nothing else." ([T. B. Odeneal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._B._Odeneal "T. B. Odeneal")) [^47] Smohalla was eventually jailed in an effort to quell a potential uprising of Native peoples, but his religion has continued to survive without him.[^49]

#### Indian Shaker Church

Also known as Tschida, the [Indian Shaker Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Shaker_Church "Indian Shaker Church") was influenced by the Waashat Religion and founded by [John Slocum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Slocum "John Slocum"), a Squaxin Island member. The name comes from the shaking and twitching motions used by the participants to brush off their sins. The religion combines Christianity with traditional Indian teachings.[^28] [^50]

### Great Plains

The [Great Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains "Great Plains") is home to religious traditions of more than fifty [Plains Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plains_Indians "Plains Indians") nations that are some of the most varied and complex in Northern America.[^51] [^21] [^52]

#### Ghost Dance

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Ghost_dance.jpg/250px-Ghost_dance.jpg)

1891 Sioux Ghost Dance. The Ghost Dance movement influenced many Native American communities.

The Ghost Dance movement of the late 1800s was a religious revitalization movement in the Western United States. Initially founded as a local ceremony in Nevada, by the [Paiute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Paiute "Northern Paiute") prophet [Wodziwob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wodziwob "Wodziwob"), the movement did not gain widespread popularity until 1889–1890, when the Ghost Dance Religion was founded by [Wovoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wovoka "Wovoka") (Jack Wilson), who was also [Northern Paiute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Paiute "Northern Paiute"). The Ghost Dance was created in a time of genocide, to save the lives of the Native Americans by enabling them to survive the current and coming catastrophes, by calling the dead to fight on their behalf, and to help them drive the colonists out of their lands.[^53] [^28] [^54]

In December 1888, Wovoka, who was thought to be the son of the medicine man Tavibo (Numu-tibo'o), fell sick with a fever during an eclipse of the sun, which occurred on January 1, 1889. Upon his recovery, he claimed that he had visited the [spirit world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_world_\(Spiritualism\) "Spirit world (Spiritualism)") and the Supreme Being and predicted that the world would soon end, then be restored to a pure state in the presence of the Messiah. All Native Americans would inherit this world, including those who were already dead, in order to live eternally without suffering. In order to reach this reality, Wovoka stated that all Native Americans should live honestly, and shun the ways of whites (especially the consumption of alcohol). He called for meditation, prayer, singing, and dancing as an alternative to mourning the dead, for they would soon resurrect. Wovoka's followers saw him as a form of the [messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messianism "Messianism") and he became known as the "Red Man's Christ."

Tavibo had participated in the Ghost Dance of 1870 and had a similar vision of the Great Spirit of Earth removing all white men, and then of an earthquake removing all human beings. Tavibo's vision concluded that Native Americans would return to live in a restored environment and that only believers in his revelations would be resurrected.

This religion spread to many tribes on reservations in the West, including the [Shoshone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoshone "Shoshone"), [Arapaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arapaho "Arapaho"), [Cheyenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheyenne "Cheyenne"), and [Sioux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sioux "Sioux") (Dakota, Lakota, and Nakota). In fact, some bands of Lakota and Dakota were so desperate for hope during this period of forced relocation and genocide that, after making a pilgrimage to the Nevada Ghost Dance in 1889–1890, they became more militant in their resistance to the white colonists. Each Nation that adopted the Ghost Dance way provided their own understanding to the ceremony, which included the prediction that the white people would disappear, die, or be driven back across the sea. A Ghost Dance gathering at [Wounded Knee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wounded_Knee_Massacre "Wounded Knee Massacre") in December 1890 was invaded by the Seventh Cavalry, who massacred unarmed Lakota and Dakota people, primarily women, children and the elderly.[^55]

The earliest Ghost Dance heavily influenced religions such as the Earth Lodge, Bole-Maru Religion, and the Dream Dance. The [Caddo Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddo_Nation "Caddo Nation") and several other communities still practice the Ghost Dance today, though usually in secret.[^56]

#### Wocekiye

*Wocekiye* is a faith and practice among [Dakota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakota_people "Dakota people") and [Lakota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakota_people "Lakota people") peoples engaged in both the traditional Dakota and [Lakota religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakota_religion "Lakota religion") as well as forms of Christianity.[^57]

### North-Eastern America

The most prominent tribes of the Northeast Woodlands in the U.S. and Canada are [Algonquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquin_people "Algonquin people"), [Iroquoian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iroquoian_languages "Iroquoian languages") and [Siouan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siouan_languages "Siouan languages") -speaking peoples.[^58]

#### Hudson Bay Interior/Subarctic

The [Cree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cree "Cree"), including [Omushkego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swampy_Cree "Swampy Cree"), beliefs similar to [Anishinaabe traditional beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anishinaabe_traditional_beliefs "Anishinaabe traditional beliefs"), different structure.[^59]

#### Great Lakes Region

*Midewiwin* or the Grand Medicine Society and *Wabunowin* or the Dawn Society are a traditional religious organisations of the [Anishinaabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anishinaabe "Anishinaabe") ([Ojibwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ojibwe "Ojibwe")) and some others of the Natives of [the Maritimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Maritimes "The Maritimes"), New England and [Great Lakes regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_region "Great Lakes region").[^60] [^61] [^3]

#### Longhouse Religion

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f2/Iroquois_longhouse.jpg/250px-Iroquois_longhouse.jpg)

This replica of a Six Nations (Haudenosaunee) longhouse represents where the traditional practices take place.

The Longhouse Religion is the popular name of the religious movement known as The Code of Handsome Lake or Gaihwi:io ("Good Message"), founded in 1797/1799 by the [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_people "Seneca people") prophet [Handsome Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handsome_Lake "Handsome Lake") (Sganyodaiyo <sup>ʔ</sup>). He combines and reinterprets elements of traditional [Seneca beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_mythology "Seneca mythology") with a revised code such as must refrain from drinking, selling of land, intensive animal farming and witchcraft meant to revive traditional consciousness after a long period of cultural disintegration following colonization.[^28] [^62] [^50]

### South-Eastern North America

The native religions of the U.S. region are held in common among the [Indigenous peoples of the Southeastern Woodlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Southeastern_Woodlands "Indigenous peoples of the Southeastern Woodlands"), who mainly practiced the [Green Corn Ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Corn_Ceremony "Green Corn Ceremony") and other [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_and_agriculture "Religion and agriculture") cults.[^63] [^64] [^65]

#### Keetoowah Society

The [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee "Cherokee") Keetoowah Society is a reform religious and political organisation formed in 1858 and re-established ca. 1900 that intended to preserve and practice traditional [Cherokee spiritual beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_spiritual_beliefs "Cherokee spiritual beliefs") or "old ways" of tribal life, based on religious nationalism.[^28] [^64]

#### Four Mothers Society

The Four Mothers Society is a religious in nature traditionalist organisation of [Muscogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscogee "Muscogee"), [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee "Cherokee"), [Choctaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw "Choctaw") and [Chickasaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickasaw "Chickasaw") peoples that formally founded about 1895 and remains active today.[^66]

### South-Western North America

The native nations of the Southwestern U.S. and Mexico mainly practiced maize and other [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_and_agriculture "Religion and agriculture") cults.[^67]

#### Mexicayotl

Mexicayotl ([Nahuatl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahuatl "Nahuatl") word meaning "Essence of the [Mexican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_people "Mexican people") ", "Mexicanity"; [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language "Spanish language"): *Mexicanidad*; see *[\-yotl](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/-yotl "wiktionary:-yotl")*) is a movement reviving the Indigenous religion, philosophy and traditions of [ancient Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_chronology "Mesoamerican chronology") ([Aztec religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_religion "Aztec religion") and [Aztec philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_philosophy "Aztec philosophy")) amongst the [Indigenous peoples of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Mexico "Indigenous peoples of Mexico").[^68]

The movement came to light in the 1950s, led by [Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City "Mexico City") intellectuals, but has grown significantly on a [grassroots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassroots "Grassroots") level only in more recent times, also spreading to the [Chicanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_American "Mexican American") of North America.[^69] Their rituals involve the mitotiliztli.[^70] The followers, called *Mexicatl* (singular) and *Mexicah* (plural), or simply *[Mexica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexica "Mexica")*, are mostly urban and suburban people.[^69]

The Mexicayotl movement started in the 1950s with the founding of the group *Nueva Mexicanidad* by [Antonio Velasco Piña](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Velasco_Pi%C3%B1a "Antonio Velasco Piña"). In the same years, Rodolfo Nieva López founded the *Movimiento Confederado Restaurador de la Cultura del Anáhuac*,[^71] the co-founder of which was Francisco Jimenez Sanchez who in later decades became a spiritual leader of the Mexicayotl movement, endowed with the [honorific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorific "Honorific") *[Tlacaelel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlacaelel "Tlacaelel")*. He had a deep influence in shaping the movement, founding the In Kaltonal ("House of the Sun", also called Native Mexican Church) in the 1970s.[^72]

From the 1970s onwards Mexcayotl has grown developing in a web of local worship and community groups (called *[calpulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calpulli "Calpulli")* or *kalpulli*) [^69] and spreading to the [Mexican Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Americans "Mexican Americans") or [Chicanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicanos "Chicanos") in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"). It has also developed strong ties with Mexican national identity movements and [Chicano nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicano_nationalism "Chicano nationalism").[^73] Sanchez's Native Mexican Church (which is a confederation of calpullis) was officially recognized by the government of [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico "Mexico") in 2007.[^74]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Llullaillaco_mummies_in_Salta_city%2C_Argentina.jpg/250px-Llullaillaco_mummies_in_Salta_city%2C_Argentina.jpg)

Religious traditions of the [Indigenous peoples of South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_South_America "Indigenous peoples of South America") from different regions are very varied. The most outstanding contrast appears between the highly developed [Andean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes "Andes") faiths with priesthood and empire-ruling cults [^22] [^78] and the religious beliefs of the tribes in the eastern lowlands.[^10] [Caral–Supe civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caral%E2%80%93Supe_civilization "Caral–Supe civilization") in Peru has been recognized as one of the [cradles of civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_civilization "Cradle of civilization") and oldest-known civilization in the Americas.

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ab/Carreta_Diablada_de_Pillaro.jpg/250px-Carreta_Diablada_de_Pillaro.jpg)

One of many carts of a parade during the Diablada de Pillaro, Equador.

An example of modern syncretistic events is the dance and parade *[Diablada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diablada "Diablada")* ("Dance of the Devils") which traditionally honored the Indigenous [Uru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uru_people "Uru people") terrible god [Tiw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiw_\(Uru_god\) "Tiw (Uru god)").[^79]

### Pan-Native traditions and ceremonies

Especially among native North Americans, can be found several pan-Native ceremonies and traditions, both all-North American, such as *[powwow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powwow "Powwow")* meetings, and regional, like the [Sun Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Dance "Sun Dance").[^80]

#### Native American Church

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Flowering_peyote_cactus.jpg/250px-Flowering_peyote_cactus.jpg)

A flowering peyote cactus.

The Peyote Religion (legally termed and more properly known as the [Native American Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_Church "Native American Church")), also sometimes called the "Peyote Road" or the "Peyote Way", is a reformed religious tradition involving the ceremonial and sacred use of *[Lophophora williamsii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lophophora_williamsii "Lophophora williamsii")* ([peyote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyote "Peyote")).[^81] [^82] [^28] [^83] Use of peyote for religious purposes is thousands of years old and some have thought it to have originated within one of the following tribes: the Carrizo, the [Coahuiltecan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coahuiltecan "Coahuiltecan"), the [Lipan Apache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipan_Apache_people "Lipan Apache people"), the [Mescalero Apache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mescalero_Apache "Mescalero Apache"), the [Karankawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karankawa "Karankawa"), or the [Caddo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddo "Caddo"), the [Navajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo "Navajo"), the [Tonkawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonkawa "Tonkawa"), with the Plains [Cree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cree "Cree"), Carrizo, and the Lipan Apache being the three most likely sources. In Mexico the [Huichol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huichol "Huichol"), [Tepehuán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tepehu%C3%A1n "Tepehuán"), and other Native Mexicans use peyote.[^84] [^85] [^86]

#### Sun Dance

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bd/Fort_Hall_Reservation._Shoshone_Indian_Sun_Dance_-_NARA_-_298649.tif/lossy-page1-250px-Fort_Hall_Reservation._Shoshone_Indian_Sun_Dance_-_NARA_-_298649.tif.jpg)

Shoshone Sun Dance at Fort Hall, 1925.

The Sun Dance is a religious ceremony and reform movement, 1890 the [Shoshone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoshone "Shoshone") tribe in origin,[^28] practiced by a number of Native American peoples in the U.S. and Canada, primarily those of the [Plains Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plains_Indians "Plains Indians").[^87] [^88] [^89] Each tribe that has some type of sun dance ceremony that has their own distinct practices and ceremonial protocols. In many cases, the ceremony is held in private and is not open to the public. Most details of the ceremony are kept from public knowledge out of great respect for, and the desire for protection of, the traditional ways. Many of the ceremonies have features in common, such as specific dances and songs, the use of drums, the [ceremonial pipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceremonial_pipe "Ceremonial pipe"), praying, fasting and, in some cases, the piercing of the skin as a sacrifice.

In Canada, the Plains [Cree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cree "Cree") call this ceremony the Thirst Dance; the [Saulteaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saulteaux "Saulteaux") call it the Rain Dance; and the [Blackfoot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfoot_Confederacy "Blackfoot Confederacy") (*Siksika*, *Kainai*, and *Piikani*) call it the Medicine Dance. It is also practiced by the Canadian [Dakota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakota_people "Dakota people") and [Nakoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakoda_people "Nakoda people"), and the [Dene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dene "Dene").[^88] [^28]

## Religious leaders

Leaders in Native religions include [Popé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop%C3%A9 "Popé"), who led the [Pueblo revolt in 1675](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pueblo_Revolt "Pueblo Revolt"), [Quautlatas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quautlatas "Quautlatas"), who inspired the [Tepehuan Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tepehuan_Revolt "Tepehuan Revolt") against the Spanish in 1616, [Neolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolin "Neolin"), [Tenskwatawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenskwatawa "Tenskwatawa"), Kenekuk, [Smohalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smohalla "Smohalla"), [John Slocum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Slocum "John Slocum"), [Wovoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wovoka "Wovoka"), [Black Elk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Elk "Black Elk") and many others.

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a1/Ten-sq%C3%BAat-a-way.jpg/250px-Ten-sq%C3%BAat-a-way.jpg)

Tenskwatawa, by George Catlin.

From time to time important religious leaders organized revivals. In Indiana in 1805, [Tenskwatawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenskwatawa "Tenskwatawa") (called the Shawnee Prophet by Americans) led a religious revival following a smallpox epidemic and a series of witch-hunts. His beliefs were based on the earlier teachings of the [Lenape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenape "Lenape") prophets, [Scattamek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattamek "Scattamek") and [Neolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolin "Neolin"), who predicted a coming apocalypse that would destroy the European-American settlers.[^91] Tenskwatawa urged the tribes to reject the ways of the Americans: to give up firearms, liquor, American style clothing, to pay traders only half the value of their debts, and to refrain from ceding any more lands to the United States. The revival led to warfare led by his brother [Tecumseh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tecumseh "Tecumseh") against the white settlers.[^92] Juan de la Cruz Puc became seen as a prophet between the Yucatec Maya during the Caste War. Juan's sermons and prophecies were preserved in the A'almaj T'aan (Cruzo'ob Bible) and are still relevant between the Yucatec Maya people.

## Congressional legislation

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/45/PeyoteWayCleanHwy.jpg/250px-PeyoteWayCleanHwy.jpg)

Sign for a Native American church

### American Indian Religious Freedom Act

The American Indian Religious Freedom Act is a United States federal law and a joint resolution of Congress that provides protection for tribal culture and traditional religious rights such as access to sacred sites, freedom to worship through traditional ceremony, and use and possession of sacred objects for Native Americans, [Inuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inuit "Inuit"), [Aleut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleut "Aleut"), and [Native Hawaiians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Hawaiians "Native Hawaiians"). It was passed on August 11, 1978.

### Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act

The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), Pub.L. 101–601, 104 Stat. 3048, is a United States federal law passed on 16 November 1990 requiring federal agencies and institutions that receive federal funding to return Native American cultural items and human remains to their respective peoples. Cultural items include funerary objects, sacred objects, and objects of cultural patrimony.

### Religious Freedom Restoration Act

The Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (also known as RFRA), is a 1993 [United States federal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_law "United States federal law") aimed at preventing laws that substantially burden a person's free exercise of [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion"). It was held unconstitutional as applied to the states in the *[City of Boerne v. Flores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Boerne_v._Flores "City of Boerne v. Flores")* decision in 1997, which ruled that the RFRA is not a proper exercise of Congress's enforcement power. However, it continues to be applied to the federal government – for instance, in *[Gonzales v. O Centro Espirita Beneficente Uniao do Vegetal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzales_v._O_Centro_Espirita_Beneficente_Uniao_do_Vegetal "Gonzales v. O Centro Espirita Beneficente Uniao do Vegetal")* – because Congress has broad authority to carve out exemptions from federal laws and regulations that it itself has authorized. In response to *City of Boerne v. Flores*, some individual states passed [State Religious Freedom Restoration Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Religious_Freedom_Restoration_Acts "State Religious Freedom Restoration Acts") that apply to state governments and local municipalities.

### Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples was adopted by the [United Nations General Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly "United Nations General Assembly") during its 61st session at [UN Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Headquarters "UN Headquarters") in New York City on 13 September 2007. Article 31, in particular, emphasizes that Indigenous Peoples have the right to their cultural heritage, including ceremonial knowledge, as protected [intellectual property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_intellectual_property "Indigenous intellectual property").

## Among non-Natives

Among non-American Native [modern pagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_paganism "Modern paganism") in a number of countries, there is a loosely formed movement called Indianism or *[Powwow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powwow "Powwow")*. If in American countries, non-Natives by origin can somehow join authentic ceremonies and, at least, can revitalize same components in their own traditions,[^95] then in other parts of the world, such as Russia, neo-pagans are uniting into their own [new religious movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement "New religious movement"). Its adherents, based on romanticized ideas drawn from classic adventure literature and other sources, strive to follow the worldview and way of life of American Natives. Each participant personally chooses a favorite tribe, takes a new spiritual name, and rallies (*powwow*) are organized in summer country camps.[^96]

In addition, there is [Neoshamanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoshamanism "Neoshamanism"), a broad [universalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universalism "Universalism") new religious movement, which based on some practices derived from Native American shamanisms and associated with teachings and organisations of [Carlos Castaneda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Castaneda "Carlos Castaneda") and [Michael Harner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Harner "Michael Harner") in the U.S. and [Mestre Irineu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mestre_Irineu "Mestre Irineu") and [Mestre Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mestre_Gabriel "Mestre Gabriel") in Brazil.[^97] [^98]
