## Believing in the goodness of people: Idaho human rights center celebrates 30-year anniversary

Boise-based Wassmuth Center for Human Rights commemorates anniversary, seeks change through education

![new Philip E. Batt building is home to the Wassmuth Center for Human Rights](https://idahocapitalsun.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/IMG_4559-2048x1365.jpg)

new Philip E. Batt building is home to the Wassmuth Center for Human Rights

The Philip E. Batt building located in Boise and home to the Wassmuth Center for Human Rights. (Photo by Mia Maldonado/Idaho Capital Sun)

The [Wassmuth Center for Human Rights](https://www.wassmuthcenter.org/about-the-wassmuth-center/), a staple of the Boise nonprofit scene, will celebrate its 30 year anniversary Saturday in commemoration of its community growth and impact.

The event, held at the Wassmuth educational center in downtown Boise, will feature choral performances by the [Common Ground Chorus](https://commongroundboise.org/), speeches, art projects and an outdoor museum exhibit teaching visitors about human rights.

Since its founding, the center has developed into an important landmark in the state – hosting the only Anne Frank memorial in the country. Its programs, largely active in education circles, bring historical human rights teachings – as outlined in the [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights), a document displayed at its memorial – to practical use.

The event creates a space to celebrate this long history of human rights struggles, their complicated present and how the community, brought together under the umbrella of education, may re-center human rights in everyday life.

“The memorial opened to the public in 2002, and the core mission of the center ever since then has been around education,” Christina Bruce-Bennion, executive director of the center, said in an interview. “There are lots of ways that we can try to make change in the world, and the center has always leaned into the importance, the power, the role, of education.”

The 30-year anniversary offers an opportunity for the center to reinforce teachings that were pivotal to its founding, Bruce-Bennion said.

## From traveling exhibit to educational center

In 1995, a traveling Anne Frank exhibit known as “Anne Frank in the World” touched down in Idaho: bringing at least 40,000 visitors, according to Bruce-Bennion.

The exhibit came at a troubled time for Idaho’s history. Factions of the Neo-Nazi group Aryan Nations operated from their then-active headquarters in North Idaho, and Proposition 1, an initiative on the November 1994 ballot to prevent homosexuality from being a recognized minority in the state, had been defeated by a small [0.8% margin](https://canvass.sos.idaho.gov/eng/ballot_questions/view/64/).

“There were a couple of things (in the exhibit) that really seemed to resonate with people,” Bruce-Bennion said. “One was this idea that Anne Frank, even though she and her family were in incredibly difficult circumstances, still believed in humanity and the goodness of people.”

![Christina Bruce-Bennion, executive director of the Wassmuth Center for Human Rights](https://idahocapitalsun.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/IMG_4582-scaled.jpg)

Christina Bruce-Bennion, executive director of the Wassmuth Center for Human Rights, gives members of the media a tour of the new Philip E. Batt building. (Photo by Mia Maldonado/Idaho Capital Sun)

Inspired by this exhibit, and seeing the possibility of establishing a longer-lasting project in the state, Bruce-Bennion said a group of women came together one year later to form the Wassmuth Center for Human Rights.

The center owes its name to Bill Wassmuth, a priest in Coeur d’Alene who headed the [Kootenai County Task Force on Human Relations](https://www.idahohumanrights.org/) and their struggle against the Aryan Nations. Wassmuth’s position as a priest, and later as community organizer, allowed him to resist the Aryan Nations by organizing diversity-based events that challenged discrimination, according to an [article](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2025/01/08/refusing-to-let-hate-have-the-final-word-the-hidden-history-of-human-rights-in-idaho/) written by education director for the Wassmuth Center Jessica Westhoff.

Today, the center continues Wassmuth’s legacy through education targeting what they call the “pyramid of injustice.”

The pyramid functions as a funnel whereby discrimination, starting at language through artwork or propaganda, can grow to “Other” and repress entire groups of people, according to Bruce-Bennion. At the bottom of this spiral is “elimination,” a term used by the center to describe the final phase of cultural and group erasure.

“What we don’t want is for people to land at the bottom of that (spiral) and be like ‘oh, it’s heavy and hard (to change)’,” Bruce-Bennion said. “The idea (behind the center) is how do we interrupt that spiral.”

## Bridging education and practice

The center undertakes more than [20 educational programs](https://www.wassmuthcenter.org/about-the-anne-frank-memorial/#) for learners across the city and state, including networks with educators to promote community-oriented classrooms. These programs range from student summer camps, book clubs, speaker series to educator discussion sessions.

In 2024, the construction of the center’s educational building opened new opportunities for in-person meetings. The [RIPL collective](https://www.wassmuthcenter.org/ripl-collective/), one such initiative, brings together teachers for discussions around community-building in the classroom.

“We don’t often think about a classroom as a community, but how different is the learning, and what can happen in that classroom, if every child comes in and knows that they matter and that someone sees them, their opinion and their voice,” Bruce-Bennion said. “A lot of our work with educators is to support their thinking about how to set up their classrooms.”

![Jeff Westhoff](https://idahocapitalsun.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Westhoff-Headshot-1891x2048.png)

Jess Westhoff is the education programs director for the Wassmuth Center for Human Rights. (Photo courtesy of Jess Westhoff)

Despite their respective differences, each program uses a set of six principles, called the [“Wassmuth Powerful Principles,”](https://www.wassmuthcenter.org/powerful-practices/) to incorporate human rights into daily activities, according to Westhoff. The principles lay out different ways for people to uphold human rights.

This focus on practice alongside education makes up an important part of the center’s work, according to Westhoff.

“People often expect us to teach history, ‘here’s what happened, don’t let it happen again,’ and we do teach history, but we really can’t stop there,” Westhoff said in an interview. “I think the problem with treating human rights as history alone is that it leaves people informed but still powerless, so folks know what went wrong, they see what’s still not going right here in our community, but they don’t know what to do about it tomorrow morning when they wake up.”

The [H-RISE Leadership Program](https://www.wassmuthcenter.org/h-rise/), for example, takes the center’s dual approach toward education and practice to create future “youth leaders.”

Molly Marie Steenhoven, a high school junior enrolled in the H-RISE program and active with Wassmuth since eighth grade, said it allowed her to experience the world in new ways, including a new interest in books and movies that detail human rights issues.

By meeting with teachers, students and community leaders, she also said the program gave her “the community to see human rights as something that I have the capacity to change, impact and support.”

As the center heads into its 30 year celebration, Bruce-Bennion said organizers seek to “widen the circle.”

“The history of this place is very much rooted in Idaho, but the work is relevant everywhere,” she said. “… Our hope is to, in a measured and impactful way, continue to grow the work.”
