## By failing to hold Iran War hearings, Idaho’s U.S. Sen. Jim Risch abdicates his constitutional duty

The Senate Foreign Relations Committee has a role to play to hold the executive branch to account, write guest columnists Noah Carter and Marie Osborn.An Iranian flag is planted in the rubble of a police station, damaged in airstrikes on March 3, 2026, in Tehran, Iran. The United States and Israel have continued the joint attack on Iran that began on February 28, resulting in the death of Iran’s Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. Iran retaliated by firing waves of missiles and drones at Israel, and targeting U.S. allies in the region. (Photo by Majid Saeedi/Getty Images)

By failing to hold hearings on the Iran War, U.S. Sen. Jim Risch, R-Idaho, who chairs the U.S. Senate Foreign Relations Committee, abdicates his constitutional duty. Only Congress can declare war. Further, Idaho’s legacy in American foreign policy epitomized by U.S. Sens. William Borah and Frank Church is forfeited when Risch doesn’t do his job.

On Saturday, Feb. 28, President Donald Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu attacked Iran. Four days earlier, the president stood before Congress and our nation to deliver his State of the Union address. Not a word was spoken about the coming war.

War places Americans in military service at risk of the ultimate sacrifice. War adds a fresh wave of veterans with service-connected conditions entitled to compensation and health care. War impacts affordability by increasing fuel prices fueling inflation. Meanwhile, the national debt approaches an unprecedent $40 trillion.

War is our elected leaders’ most important decision. Our Constitution’s Article I explicitly grants Congress the power “to declare War.” Why? Our Constitution’s framers established a collective decision process for entering conflict: No single person is empowered to take America to war.

Under the Constitution, Congress’s Senate Foreign Relations Committee should hold public hearings and, on behalf of the American people, bring the executive branch to account for attacking Iran. And that brings us to Sen. Jim Risch.

U.S. Sen. Jim Risch, R-Idaho, looks on during a Senate Foreign Relations Committee Hearing on the Fiscal Year 2023 Budget at the U.S. Capitol on April 26, 2022, in Washington, D.C. (Photo by Bonnie Cash/Getty Images)

Elected by Idahoans, Jim Risch swore an oath to uphold the Constitution. Now chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, he is empowered with “gatekeeping” authority to decide timing and issues. Chairman Risch decides when and how the Foreign Relations Committee engages in oversight of the executive branch’s decision to take America to war in violation of the Constitution.

Given the extraordinary gravity of the U.S. attack on Iran, you might assume the committee is doing its job and asserting congressional oversight on behalf of the American people. But no. Chairman Risch refuses to hold hearings on the Iran War.

Chairman Risch argues that classified briefings are adequate and that public hearings risk exposing sensitive information. Instead, focus should remain on unquestioning support for the administration’s war decisions. However, that a president, alone, can take us to war, is contrary to the Constitution and to our history.

In the past, Idaho has held enormous influence over American foreign policy. Sen. William Borah, the “Lion of Idaho” for whom Idaho’s tallest mountain is named, chaired the Senate’s Foreign Relations Committee for 10 years, from 1924-1933. He played a major role in American foreign policy between WWI and WWII. Sen. Borah vigorously opposed foreign entanglements.

Sen. Frank Church served on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee for 24 years, including as chair from 1979-1981. Sen. Church initially supported the Vietnam War before becoming highly critical of it. He stood up to a president in his own party, sponsoring Senate amendments to curtail the war.

This year we commemorate the 250th anniversary of the USA and our freedoms: “All men are created equal,” “unalienable rights,” “life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness,” and “consent of the governed.” From the July 4, 1776, Declaration of Independence flows a Constitutional order that holds our nation together.

Sen. Jim Risch, representing Idaho, swore an oath to uphold the Constitution.

Chairman Risch: Honor your oath. Do your job. Put America first over party as Sens. Borah and Church did before you. Hold public hearings on the Iran War.

Our laws and ethics matter.
