BOISE, Idaho — A Boise man found a snapping turtle in Five Mile Creek, adding another unusual wildlife sighting to a summer of odd discoveries in Idaho.

Robert Filiatreaux, who goes by Robert, said he was checking the creek when he spotted the turtle in the water.

“First I couldn't believe it when I saw it because the carapace was jet black,” Filiatreaux said.

Filiatreaux said he waded in, grabbed the turtle and put it on the ground after it hissed.

![](https://media.ktvb.com/assets/KTVB/images/0f6734cd-bdb5-47a1-9480-7f64663feb0a/20260724T235331/0f6734cd-bdb5-47a1-9480-7f64663feb0a_1920x1080.jpg)

He said the turtle appeared to be hunting for fish, salamanders, frogs and tadpoles.

The turtle, which he estimated weighed 20 to 25 pounds, was a snapping turtle, a species not native to Idaho.

“They don't breed here, so he was released probably 15 to 20 years ago,” Filiatreaux said.

The Idaho State Department of Agriculture says it receives only about five reports of snapping turtles each year, and officials believe the state’s population remains very small.

Still, the turtles can threaten native wildlife because they have few natural predators in Idaho, the agency says.

The state’s invasive species program says snapping turtles can compete with native species and prey on fish, amphibians and other wildlife.

Officials say people should never release nonnative animals into the wild.

When snapping turtles are reported, the state works to remove them and protect native ecosystems.

Filiatreaux said he wanted a photo more than anything else.

“His neck was probably out 11 or 12 inches, so I knew he could turn around and bite me,” he said.

After taking the picture, he said he got a workout trying to handle the turtle.

Eventually, he let the animal go.

“Don't follow me. Water’s over here,” Filiatreaux joked as the turtle crawled back toward the creek.

He said he released it because it appeared healthy and was simply doing what it had been doing in the creek.

“I felt like he was pretty successful doing what he was doing in there, and he wasn't bothering anybody because this is just a ditch. So I figured just let him go,” he said.

The state says its goal is to keep Idaho’s snapping turtle population small and prevent more nonnative species from becoming established.
