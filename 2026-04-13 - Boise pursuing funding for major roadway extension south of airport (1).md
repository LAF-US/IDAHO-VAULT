## Boise pursuing funding to design commuter, industrial roadway south of the airport

![Lake Hazel Road](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Image-from-iOS-2048x1536.jpeg)

Lake Hazel Road. Photo: Gretchen Parsons/BoiseDev

With some outside funding, a roadway linking Boise’s residential southwest with its industrial southeast could be built in the coming years.

Bre Brush, the City of Boise’s Senior Policy Advisor, said that the city applied for a $7 million grant from the U.S. Department of Transportation that would allow the city to complete 95% of the design work for a six-mile extension of Lake Hazel Rd. from its current intersection with Orchard St. out to I-84 near Memory Ln. and Federal Way.

The roadway has the potential to connect residents and industrial users south of I-84 with industry and future development in the vicinity of the Boise Airport, as well as to Micron further east. The city has considered building the roadway for decades, but has set the project aside several times, citing a lack of studies and funding sources, [as BoiseDev reported](https://boisedev.com/news/2024/09/24/lake-hazel-extension/https://boisedev.com/news/2024/09/24/lake-hazel-extension/). Micron’s plans to spend $200 billion to expand its US manufacturing operations, including building two new fabrication plants in Boise, makes questions of how that workforce will move between home and work all the more urgent.

![Watkins Carter - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/f002ca13-0527-4831-9254-99ce5fa33461.jpg)

Watkins Carter - 2:1

Brush said that city staff, about a year ago, began working with Kittelson & Associates, a consulting firm, to study how the area that the road would potentially go through is already developing and being built out. In January, the Boise City Council met for a strategic planning workshop and directed city staff to apply for the $7 million grant. The grant program, operated through the U.S. Department of Transportation, is called Better Utilizing Investments to Leverage Development (BUILD) and funds transportation projects “with significant local or regional impact,” [according to the program’s website](https://www.transportation.gov/BUILDgrants).

Brush said the city can expect to hear whether it received the award by the end of June. Even so, it could take a total of 3-5 years for a consultant to complete the grant work, she said. She said that the city had received twelve letters of support from “nearly every employer, landowner, and stakeholder in the area,” including Micron.

Boise Mayor Lauren McLean said seeking grant opportunities in tandem with area stakeholders reflects the economic development opportunities that the city sees in the area, and is key to getting the roadway built.

“Putting this back on the list, chasing federal funds, working with willing landowners is the formula for getting this done,” McLean said. “There’s lots of questions and interest in moving forward, and I know that there will be many stakeholders brought into this if we have the good fortune of winning the grant” and later, seeking additional federal funding “to make this road a reality.”

## A 35 mph speed limit?

Brush said that one of the key recommendations from Kittleson is for the extension to follow property boundaries “as much as is feasible.” A map of the proposed project area shows several stair-like bends in the future roadway between Orchard and the area around I-84, with three construction phases.

Brush said they are proposing designing the roadway for a 35 mph speed — “nothing unique for a mobility arterial.” The roadway would also have shared-use pathways on both sides, she said.

Some members of the ACHD Board of Commissioners and the Boise City Council wondered if the 35 mph speed limit would be adequate, saying it seemed low for a larger corridor.

Boise Council Member Colin Nash said that he has viewed the road extension as both an opportunity for economic development and a commuter alternative to I-84.

“Something that’s that long at that slow speed makes me think that that might not be as practical … I just have a hard time seeing someone use this as an alternative to 84,” Nash said.

![Twilight Crit 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/7a9153dd-9b20-425c-8428-1affff0ec02d.png)

Brush said that going forward, the city is going to have to consider what types of vehicles will use the roadway, which could be some combination of local traffic, industrial users, and commuters.

“One thing we struggled with as a team as we were looking at this is we’re looking at this road to serve 3,000 homes on the western end, industrial traffic, commuter employees getting to Micron from Meridian, and how do we balance all of those needs?” Brush said. “So as we move forward, we’re seeing more development applications, we’ll know what the traffic mix is going to be, and we’ll be able to better define what that true speed will be.”

ACHD Commissioner Alexis Pickering said, “It’s not (the city’s) job or ACHD’s job to try to reduce traffic on the ITD system, right? We’re trying to manage the flow of traffic within our system. I think (extending) Lake Hazel alone would do a lot for the roads we manage and maintain.”

Some leaders present at the meeting said that the Idaho Transportation Department is exploring other ways to relieve congestion on I-84. As BoiseDev reported, the state is considering the possibility of [adding major perimeter roads, or beltways, to offset traffic on the interstate](https://boisedev.com/news/2025/06/04/treasure-valley-betway-routes-kuna-meridian-i-84-boise-highway-16/).
