**Mary Caroline Richards** (July 13, 1916, Weiser, Idaho – September 10, 1999, Kimberton, Pennsylvania) was an American poet, potter, and writer best known for her book *Centering: in Pottery, Poetry and the Person*.[^1] Educated at [Reed College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_College "Reed College"), in Portland, Oregon, and at the [University of California at Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), she taught English at the [Central Washington College of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Washington_University "Central Washington University") and the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), but in 1945 became a faculty member of the experimental [Black Mountain College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mountain_College "Black Mountain College") in [North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina "North Carolina") where she continued to teach until the end of the summer session in 1951.[^2]

Her teaching experience and growth as an artist while at Black Mountain College prepared the foundation for most of her work in life, both as an educator and creator. Later in life, she discovered the work of [Rudolf Steiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Steiner "Rudolf Steiner") and lived the last part of her life at a [Camphill Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camphill_Movement "Camphill Movement") in Kimberton, PA. In 1985, while living at the Kimberton Camphill Village she began teaching workshops with [Matthew Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Fox_\(priest\) "Matthew Fox (priest)") at the University of Creation Spirituality in [Oakland, CA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland,_California "Oakland, California") during the winter months. Mary Caroline Richards died in 1999 in Kimberton, PA.[^1]

## Early life and education

M.C. Richards was born in Weiser, Idaho on July 13, 1916. As an infant her family moved to Portland, Oregon where she spent the early part of her life. In 1935 she attended high school at the [Oregon Episcopal School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_Episcopal_School "Oregon Episcopal School") (then called St. Helen's Hall Junior College).

She earned a Bachelor of Arts degree at [Reed College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_College "Reed College") in Portland, in Literature and Languages. She earned a MA degree in 1939 in English and a PhD in 1942 in English and linguistics, both degrees were from [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley").

In 1943 she taught English at the [Central Washington College of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Washington_College_of_Education "Central Washington College of Education") in Ellensburg, Washington and married Vernon Young (marriage later dissolved). From there she taught briefly at the [University of California at Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") and at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"),[^3] but became disillusioned with the traditional academic environment. While teaching at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), she met the social scientist [Albert William Levi Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_William_Levi "Albert William Levi"), and they were married in 1945 (their marriage was dissolved while she was teaching at Black Mountain College).

## Black Mountain College years (1945–1952)

In 1945 Richards joined the faculty of the English Department at [Black Mountain College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mountain_College "Black Mountain College"), where she taught writing and literature, and where she served as Faculty Chair from 1949 to 1951.[^4]

At [Black Mountain College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mountain_College "Black Mountain College"), M.C. Richards was one of the most popular teachers with the students.[^5] She grouped her courses under the general heading of "Reading and Writing" while including important elements such as literary criticism, creative writing, and dramatic literature.[^6] It was here that she began to make the transition from the academic career for which she had been trained and moved into taking a more creative approach in her teaching methods.[^7]

Here began many of the associations which connect her to the music and art worlds, through friendships with [David Tudor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Tudor "David Tudor"), [Lou Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lou_Harrison "Lou Harrison") and [John Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage "John Cage") in music, [Merce Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merce_Cunningham "Merce Cunningham") and [Remy Charlip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remy_Charlip "Remy Charlip") in dance, [Charles Olson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Olson "Charles Olson"), [Robert Creeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Creeley "Robert Creeley"), and [Robert Duncan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Duncan_\(poet\) "Robert Duncan (poet)") (loosely known as the " [Black Mountain Poets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mountain_poets "Black Mountain poets") ") in literature, and [Lyle Bongé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyle_Bong%C3%A9?action=edit&redlink=1 "Lyle Bongé (page does not exist)") and [Joe Fiore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Fiore?action=edit&redlink=1 "Joe Fiore (page does not exist)") in the visual arts. Her involvement with theater began at Black Mountain College with her translation of plays by [Jean Cocteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Cocteau "Jean Cocteau") and [Erik Satie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Satie "Erik Satie").[^3]

In 1948 Richards and her students founded the [Black Mountain Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mountain_Press_\(founded_1948\) "Black Mountain Press (founded 1948)").[^3] [^8] The purpose was to give students experience in typesetting and publishing.[^8] In addition they published literary works, broadsides, and booklets.[^8] The Black Mountain Press published M.C.'s first book of collected poems, titled *Poems* [^4] as well as the short-lived *Black Mountain College Review*, not to be confused with the later *[Black Mountain Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mountain_poets "Black Mountain poets")* edited by [Robert Creeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Creeley "Robert Creeley").

Though intended as a series, only one issue (Vol. 1, No. 1, June 1951) of *The Black Mountain College Review* was printed, featuring a Noh play by [Nick Cernovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Cernovich?action=edit&redlink=1 "Nick Cernovich (page does not exist)"), "High Speed Computing Machines" by [Natasha Goldowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natasha_Goldowski_Renner "Natasha Goldowski Renner"), poems by [Fielding Dawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fielding_Dawson "Fielding Dawson") and [Joel Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Oppenheimer "Joel Oppenheimer"), linoleum cuts by H. Roco, and other contributions by [Mary Fitton Fiore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Fitton_Fiore?action=edit&redlink=1 "Mary Fitton Fiore (page does not exist)"), [Russell Edson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Edson "Russell Edson"), and Alex Kemeny. The editorial work was shared between M.C. Richards, Alex Kemeny, and [Hazel Larsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazel_Larsen_Archer "Hazel Larsen Archer"). A second issue was set in type but never printed due to lack of funds.[^9]

While at Black Mountain College, Richards befriended student [James Leo Herlihy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Leo_Herlihy "James Leo Herlihy"), who went on to be a noted novelist, playwright and actor.

When she resigned her faculty position at Black Mountain College after the summer of 1951,[^3] she moved to New York City along with pianist and Cage associate [David Tudor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Tudor "David Tudor").[^3] There they joined [John Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage "John Cage") and [Merce Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merce_Cunningham "Merce Cunningham"), where they collaborated with others to include [Ray Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Johnson "Ray Johnson"), [Robert Rauschenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Rauschenberg "Robert Rauschenberg"), [Susan Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Weil "Susan Weil"), [Paul Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Taylor_\(choreographer\) "Paul Taylor (choreographer)"), and [Viola Farber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola_Farber "Viola Farber").[^6]

While in New York, she began to study pottery at the [Greenwich House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_House_Pottery "Greenwich House Pottery") in Greenwich Village.[^10] She also began to work on the first English translation of [Anton Artaud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonin_Artaud "Antonin Artaud") 's [*The Theatre and Its Double*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theatre_and_its_Double "The Theatre and its Double"),[^1] which was published by [Grove Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grove_Press "Grove Press") in 1958 to wide acclaim.[^3] Richards's translation is considered the definitive English version of the essays and was instrumental in introducing Artaud's work in the United States.[^11]

In summer 1952, she participated in *Theater Piece No. 1* at Black Mountain College, an event that came to be known as the first [Happening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happening "Happening"). It was organized by John Cage and also involving Robert Rauschenberg, Charles Olson, David Tudor, and Merce Cunningham.[^3]

In the catalog for her exhibition at the Tampa Museum of Art in 1991 with [John Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage "John Cage"), [Merce Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merce_Cunningham "Merce Cunningham"), and [Irwin Kremen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irwin_Kremen "Irwin Kremen"), M.C. wrote about her experience at Black Mountain College as "challenging the intellectual imagination and being a time for a very alive and vibrant artistic community." During her time there she was able to "think of her work as integrating the soul, the mind, and the muscle." She was able to participate as a writer in the writing classes that she taught, and developed a print shop there where she and her students could print their work.[^8]

## Endless Mountains Farm, pottery, poetry (1953–1984)

In 1953 she returned to Black Mountain College as a student to study pottery with [Karen Karnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karen_Karnes "Karen Karnes") and [David Weinrib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Weinrib "David Weinrib"). During a Summer Institute at Black Mountain College, [Daniel Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Rhodes "Daniel Rhodes"), [Warren MacKenzie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_MacKenzie "Warren MacKenzie"), and [Peter Voulkos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Voulkos "Peter Voulkos") were invited to teach pottery for three weeks each. This sparked a shift for M.C. Richards as a "poet potter" working in clay.[^8]

In 1954 Richards, Tudor, and Cage, among other former Black Mountain faculty, became part of the Gate Hill Cooperative community, also known as The Land, in [Stony Point, Rockland County, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stony_Point,_New_York "Stony Point, New York"), founded by the architect Paul Williams. In Stony Point, she shared a pottery studio with [Karen Karnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karen_Karnes "Karen Karnes") and [David Weinrib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Weinrib "David Weinrib").[^6] She remained in the Stony Point community for ten years and it was here that she wrote *Centering: In Pottery, Poetry, and the Person*.[^12]

In 1964, the same year she left Stony Point, her book *Centering: In Pottery, Poetry and the Person* was published by Wesleyan University Press, followed in 1973 by *The Crossing Point: Nine Easter Letters on the Art of Education* and in 1980 by *Toward Wholeness: Rudolf Steiner Education in America*. These books reveal a very personal view of the development of the individual through art and life and, combined with her extensive teaching and lecturing throughout the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s, were widely influential in the arts education and craft communities.[^3]

In 1965, Richards lifelong friend, potter [Paulus Berensohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulus_Berensohn "Paulus Berensohn") purchased 100 acres of land outside of Scranton, Pennsylvania for the creation of the [Endless Mountains Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endless_Mountains_Farm?action=edit&redlink=1 "Endless Mountains Farm (page does not exist)") art colony.[^13] [^14] M.C. Richards joined Paulus at the Farm a few years later in 1968.[^13] Due to a health scare, Berensohn left Endless Mountains Farm in 1972 however it remained in community operation.[^13] The farm had changed ownership shares over the years, the last owner was M.C. Richards in 1993, and she decided to put her future energy into Camphill Village in Kimberton, Pennsylvania and sold her shares.[^13]

[Mary Beth Edelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Beth_Edelson "Mary Beth Edelson") 's [*Some Living American Women Artists / Last Supper*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Some_Living_American_Women_Artists_\(collage\) "Some Living American Women Artists (collage)") (1972) appropriated [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci "Leonardo da Vinci") ’s *The Last Supper*, with the heads of notable women artists collaged over the heads of Christ and his apostles; M.C. was among those notable women artists. This image, addressing the role of religious and art historical iconography in the subordination of women, became "one of the most iconic images of the [feminist art movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist_art_movement "Feminist art movement")." [^15] [^16]

## University of Creation Spirituality with Matthew Fox (1985–1999)

Later in life she taught art at the Institute in Culture and Creation Spirituality (ICCS) at Holy Names College (now [Holy Names University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Names_University "Holy Names University")) in Oakland, California. ICCS was founded by former [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic "Roman Catholic") and current [Episcopal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Church_\(United_States\) "Episcopal Church (United States)") priest [Matthew Fox (priest)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Fox_\(priest\) "Matthew Fox (priest)"). Matthew invited M.C. Richards to be on his faculty at the University of Creation Spirituality teaching a course on "Art as Meditation." She continued to teach there during the winter and live at the Camphill Village Intentional Community in Kimberton Hills, Pennsylvania during the rest of year, while also continuing to teach workshops at various literary and visual art centers.

## Camphill Village (1984–1999)

She spent the last 15 years of her life living and working as a volunteer at [Camphill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camphill_Movement "Camphill Movement") Village Kimberton Hills, in Pennsylvania, an [intentional living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentional_living "Intentional living") community based on the teachings of [Rudolf Steiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Steiner "Rudolf Steiner"), where the documentary film *M.C. Richards: The Fire Within* (2003) was made.[^17] There she also worked with residents with developmental disabilities.
