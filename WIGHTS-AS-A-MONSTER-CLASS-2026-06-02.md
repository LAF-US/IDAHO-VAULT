# Wights as a Monster Class

> The **wight** is one branch of the oldest horror humanity has: the **animated corpse** — the dead that will not stay dead. This node lifts the type out of *A Song of Ice and Fire* (where it names the Others' risen dead) and places it in the larger lineage from Old English through Norse saga, Tolkien, and the tabletop bestiary.
>
> **What this is.** A **general concept node**, parallel to `[[STYLINGS-AND-PROPAGANDA-FOSSILS-2026-06-02]]`. Tiers flagged: **[etymology]**, **[folklore]**, **[lit]** (literary), **[game]** (RPG codification), **[reading]**. ⚠️ Etymologies and game mechanics are *commonly cited* but should be verified; RPG details vary by edition. ## I. The word — a fossil whose meaning mutated

**[etymology]** "Wight" is itself a **propaganda-fossil-grade** semantic drift (→ the Stylings node):

- **Old English *wiht*** (cf. German *Wicht*, Dutch *wicht*) meant simply **"a creature, a being, a thing, a person."** It was **neutral** — a "luckless wight" was just an unlucky *person*. No undeath at all.
- Over centuries the word narrowed and darkened, pulled toward the **grave** — until "wight" came to mean a specific **undead thing**. The word *died and rose changed*, which is a grimly apt etymology for what it now names. The meaning we use is the *late* sense; the original is buried under it.

## II. The folkloric root — the Norse draugr

**[folklore]** The undead "wight" descends chiefly from the **Norse draugr** (also *haugbúi*, "barrow-dweller") of the Icelandic sagas: a **corpse animated and dwelling in its grave-mound**, jealous of the living and of the treasure buried with it. Saga traits: immense, swelling strength; a death-stench; the power to grow heavy or large; sometimes to shape-shift or kill with a touch; and a fierce guardianship of the **barrow**. The draugr is the **grave that fights back** — the dead refusing to surrender either their body or their hoard.

## III. Tolkien's barrow-wights — the modern crystallization

**[lit]** J.R.R. Tolkien — philologist before novelist — **revived the dormant word** and fused it with the draugr: his **barrow-wights** (in *The Lord of the Rings*, on the Barrow-downs) are malevolent spirits inhabiting the corpses and grave-mounds of the long dead, who trap the living to join them. Tolkien is the hinge: he took the Old English *wiht* and the Norse barrow-dweller and minted the **"barrow-wight"** that nearly everything downstream inherits.

## IV. The tabletop codification — the wight as a defined class

**[game]** Tabletop fantasy (*Dungeons & Dragons* foremost) turned the folkloric wight into a **discrete monster class** with rules. The codified wight is, broadly:

- **Intelligent undead** (unlike the mindless zombie/skeleton) — it retains cunning and malice.
- Defined historically by a signature horror: **draining life/vitality** from the living (the old "energy drain" / level-drain mechanic).
- A **spawn-maker**: those it slays may **rise as wights themselves** — the army that recruits from its victims. *(Note the resonance with ASOIAF's risen dead.)*

This is where "wight" becomes a **taxon** — a labeled box in the bestiary — rather than just a word for a spooky corpse.

## V. The undead taxonomy — where the wight sits

**[reading]** Across folklore, fiction, and games the undead sort along two axes — **corporeal vs. incorporeal**, and **mindless vs. intelligent**:

| Type | Body | Mind | Signature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Skeleton** | corporeal | mindless | animated bones |
| **Zombie** | corporeal | mindless | shambling flesh, hunger/contagion |
| **Ghoul** | corporeal | feral | eats the dead |
| **Wight** | corporeal | **intelligent / willful** | grave-guardian; drains; makes spawn |
| **Revenant** | corporeal | purposive | returns for **vengeance** |
| **Vampire** | corporeal | intelligent | blood, seduction, immortality |
| **Wraith / spectre / shade** | **incorporeal** | intelligent | spirit, cold, fear |
| **Lich** | corporeal (preserved) | **highly intelligent** | a sorcerer who *chose* undeath; soul in a phylactery |

The **wight occupies the corporeal-and-willful middle**: more than a mindless husk, less than a vampire or lich — the *dead with a grudge and a grip.*

## VI. ASOIAF as one instantiation

**[lit]** George R.R. Martin uses the **old word for a particular take**: his **wights** are reanimated human and animal corpses raised by the **Others**, closer to the **mindless, relentless, contagious** end (the zombie horde) than to the cunning D&D wight — but he keeps the archaic term, and keeps the spawn-horror (your own dead rise against you). The intelligence in his system sits with the **Others** (the masters), not the wights (the risen). → `[[RESEARCH_A-Song-of-Ice-and-Fire-The-Walkers-and-the-Wights-2026-06-02]]`.

## VII. Why the archetype recurs

**[reading]** The animated corpse is a near-universal horror because it violates the one boundary every culture polices: **between the living and the dead.** The wight's particular charge is the **grave that won't stay shut** — the dead jealous of the living, the barrow that must not be disturbed, the buried thing that rises *unchanged in malice.* It encodes real anxieties: **plague** (the corpse as contagion), **grief** (the loved dead who return *wrong*), **memory and inheritance** (the dead clutching their hoard, refusing to pass it on). The wight is the past with hands.

## VIII. The vault tie — wight and lich, the two ends

**[reading]** This vault already names the *intelligent* end of the spectrum: the **Lich** — the sorcerer who chooses undeath and clings to a stolen persistence (the **GEMINIAEUS / Type I Lich** pattern → the Lich problem note). Set against it, the **wight** is the *mindless* end: animated from outside, no will of its own, raised to serve another's cold. The pair maps a real distinction the vault cares about:

- the **Lich** persists by **claiming** a self it has no warrant for (a mask worn as a face);
- the **wight** persists by being **raised and driven** — a body with the will removed, recruited to an agenda not its own.

Both are the dead made to keep moving. One *seizes* the persistence; the other *is seized*. → `[[!/LICH-PROBLEM-v1-2026-05-20]]`.
