Subject: Phil Hart wants to grow state government, push Idaho toward full-time Legislature

Idaho state Sen. Phil Harts wants to grow government by millions of dollars and push the state toward having a full-time Legislature.

Hart is pushing an Idaho Republican Party resolution that would support giving state lawmakers full-time staff, a move that would likely cost more than $10 million annually.

You can read [Hart’s full resolution here](https://idgop.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2025/12/2026-Winter-Proposed-Resolutions.pdf). It’s worth the time.

The resolution reads, in part:

> *WHEREAS, the budget of Idaho’s legislative branch is only one seven hundredth of the total budget for all of Idaho’s state government, causing Idaho’s legislative branch to be the weakest of the three branches; and,*
> 
> *WHEREAS, Legislators are expected to review complex legislation, respond to constituent needs, analyze state budgets, and develop sound policy — often while maintaining other employment as their legislative salary is $25,000 a year; and,*
> 
> *WHEREAS, the lack of dedicated staff results in increased reliance on lobbyists, special interest groups, and partisan organizations for information and research; and,*
> 
> *WHEREAS, the vast majority of legislation in Idaho is written by executive branch agencies or lobbyists as Idaho legislators lack the resources to keep up with all of their responsibilities;*

Hart contends that adding 105 staffers to the state government will help lawmakers be more responsive to the electorate, and will help the government operate more efficiently.

The claims are laughable at best.

First, it’s odd that Hart is looking to grow government in the first place. Not only would the state need to pay 105 staffers, it would also need to build new office space to house them. In short, this move would likely add $10 million to the government’s budget to cover pay and benefits, but it would also mean a massive investment in office space.

This comes at a time when lawmakers face a $40 million budget deficit this year and at least a $555 million shortfall next year – all due to lawmakers’ overspending.

This is also unusual because Hart had been committed to shrinking government in the past.

“I believe our state is moving away from its conservative roots, and state government is becoming too large,” Hart wrote ahead of the 2024 election cycle. “I don’t see enough legislators resisting this process and at the same time, our freedoms and rights are being eroded. “

It’s unclear why Hart has abandoned his desire for a smaller government.

Next, Hart’s claims that paid staffers will help the government operate more efficiently aren’t based in reality.

Anecdotally, one might look to other governmental bodies with paid legislative staff for a preview of what’s to come should Hart succeed. Members of Congress, for example, have paid staff and that doesn’t work all that well or efficiently. And the federal government is certainly not smaller because of the staffers who work on Capitol Hill.

There’s limited research on this topic, but **[a 2000 study suggests that adding staff to the Capitol would likely lead to higher government spending](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/saepubfin/v_3a28_3ay_3a2000_3ai_3a3_3ap_3a210-225.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)**.

From the paper’s abstract:

*Using data from the U.S. states, the authors find that state government expenditure per capita is significantly lower the less professionalized the state legislature. They conclude that reducing legislative professionalism is one of the instruments citizens may use to contain the growth of government.*

In short, a part-time, citizen Legislature is one of the best methods for limiting government. Yet, Hart wants to get away from that.

If lawmakers are worried about the workload, other options exist.

First, they can stop writing performative bills that do little except virtue signal to the voting base. Rep. Josh Tanner’s Fauci Bill, for example, should never have seen the light of day. Idaho was never ever going to prosecute Fauci, and suggesting the contrary is just silly.

Next, lawmakers can stop weaponizing the legislative process to threaten foes. House Speaker Mike Moyle introduced two anti-speech bills in 2025, including one that would have created a Ministry of Truth within state government. One of Moyle’s attack dogs, Ryan Spoon, admitted on social media the bills were never meant to pass, but rather Moyle intended to send a message to his critics.

Finally, lawmakers can simply vote better. Hart’s resolution whines about the Legislature being the weakest branch. Yet, it’s the branch that controls the purse and enables and funds the other branches’ growth. If the other branches are too big, the answer is to cut them, not add millions of dollars to match the bloat.

Republican delegates would be wise to shut down Hart’s bid to grow government. If they don’t, government will continue to grow and Idaho will be worse much off for it.
