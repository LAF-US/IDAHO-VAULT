Meridian residents could see an increase in their sewer and water bills this fall.

Staff with the City of Meridian are proposing a combined 4.2% increase in sewer and water rates. Meridian Public Works Director Laurelei Bakken told the Meridian City Council during a workshop on Tuesday that it equates to an increase of $2.71 per bill for the average resident.

Though the city’s residents have some of the lowest sewer and water bills in the Treasure Valley, Bakken said the increase is needed to maintain financial solvency over the next five years.

Finalizing the change requires several steps. A presentation slide said residents will receive notice of the proposed change in their August and September bills, and it will be noticed in the newspaper later this month. A public hearing will be held on September 8 at 6 p.m. If the council approves the change, it would take effect on October 1.

## Rate increases to maintain solvency

Meridian water and sewer customers pay both a base rate and a usage rate for each service. A chart provided by Bakken at the presentation showed the proposed increase to both base rates and use rates that would result in the $2.71 increase for the average customer.

The city is proposing a 9% increase in the base water rate and a 5% increase in the water use rate. For sewer, the city is proposing a 4% base rate and a 3% use rate increase.

Bakken said that the city’s Enterprise Fund’s reserves are fully funded, as are current projects, without having to use debt. Over time, the city will need to consider small increases to maintain financial solvency, she said. Water and sewer utilities and all of the activities related to their operation are accounted for in the Enterprise Fund, which is financed by user fees, [according to a financial statement for the city](https://meridiancity.org/media/dk2lgysz/city-of-meridian-2024-final-financial-statements.pdf).

“We really subscribe to the go slow, do the smallest increases that you need to make sure that we get to the spot that we need to go,” Bakken said.

“We have a balancing act between wanting to remain one of the lowest prices in the valley and also staying solvent,” Overton said. “We have a financial responsibility with the consumers on both ends of that deal. So I appreciate the way you guys are looking at it, and I think your approach is fair. I hope it’s enough to keep us solvent and also keep us very competitive in the valley.”

## Future infrastructure, resident assistance

The treated water that leaves wastewater treatment plants is required to meet certain state and federal standards, as is the city’s drinking water. Bakken said that Meridian, as with all cities, will have to invest in changes to meet new regulatory requirements in the future.

While Bakken said that the city’s wastewater treatment plant is in pretty good shape due to recent investments, future regulations on temperature and other factors would likely require some investments from the city to meet any newly imposed standards. In that vein, the city is looking ahead to saving money in the next decade to help fund any needed infrastructure improvements, she said.

The city also already has a program in place for helping low-income residents pay their water and sewer bill, Bakken said. About 100 residents participate in the program, which is administered by El-Ada Community Action Partnership, she said.

[According to El-Ada’s website](https://www.eladacap.org/utility-assistance), residents must fall within a certain income level to receive emergency assistance for water, sewer, or trash service, and can receive assistance once per year.
