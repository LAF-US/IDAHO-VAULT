In recent months, the installation of cameras at intersections in Meridian has prompted questions about surveillance in the city.

A BoiseDev reader wrote in to ask if the city was installing Flock Safety cameras. One of the company’s products is an automated license plate reader (ALPR) camera that can record license plates and other details, such as color and make, for use in potential law enforcement investigations.

Jordan Robinson, a spokesperson for the Meridian Police Department, told BoiseDev via email that city staff have been installing Axon Outpost Automated License Plate Readers [in compliance with Idaho law regarding the use of such cameras](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title49/T49CH14/SECT49-1432/). The cameras have been placed “in high-traffic areas throughout the city,” he said.

The law says that data from ALPRs can be used for law enforcement, traffic flow purposes, or both. The devices and the data they collect can only be used to investigate felony or misdemeanor crimes, or to search for missing persons. They can only be used to investigate traffic infractions if the infraction was associated with an accident, the law says.

Additionally, the law says that the data can’t be used for any non-law enforcement purpose, such as political activity or personal use. Queries into the data can only be made by authorized personnel, and every query has to be logged in a secure audit system, along with details such as who made the query and its purpose.

## Efficiency, control of data

The city has used ALPRs since 2023, [as BoiseDev reported.](https://boisedev.com/news/2023/07/21/meridian-traffic-cameras/)

![City of McCall - Visiting? 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/57592d17-0c69-4d28-a6e9-c5d629733da1.png)

City of McCall - Visiting? 2:1

Robinson said, “we have had great success solving crimes in our jurisdiction and surrounding areas” more quickly and efficiently. It has also helped the department locate missing or endangered teenagers and vulnerable adults, he said.

One concern with Flock Safety cameras and other such technologies is how long data is stored and who controls it. Robinson said that the city owns and controls the data from the Axon Outpost ALPRs, and it is purged after 60 days unless data is needed as evidence of a crime.

“This data is not sold to any third parties, and we only share our data with agencies with whom we have a memorandum of understanding,” Robinson said.

![City of McCall - Visiting? 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/57592d17-0c69-4d28-a6e9-c5d629733da1.png)

The city’s data storage period will be reduced to 30 days next week, he said.

Robinson noted that “the only Flock cameras we know of in the city are those used by private entities on their private property.”

## Flock lawsuit

Elsewhere in [[Idaho]], communities that have installed Flock Safety ALPRs are facing the threat of lawsuits.

![Commercial Tire - July 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/13cd7680-6f30-4701-82a1-9fb53cf3807b.jpg)

Commercial Tire - July 2026 - 2:1

A group called DeFlock Idaho submitted notices of tort claims, a precursor to a potential lawsuit, to both the City of Wilder and the City of Caldwell regarding the cities’ use of such cameras, [as KTVB reported in June](https://www.ktvb.com/article/news/local/idaho/tort-claims-filed-against-two-canyon-county-cities-flock-camera-agreements/277-88b1dd0f-ab79-4b32-b2dc-a0cf690b764e).

Tyler Cain, a Wilder resident who is part of DeFlock Idaho, told the outlet that the company’s terms and conditions allow it to collect data for training its artificial intelligence systems, which he alleges violates the part of Idaho law prohibiting data collected from ALPRs for use for non-law enforcement purposes.
