The Boise City Council voted earlier this week to regulate the use of what city council members are calling a popular but dangerous type of electric vehicle.

The council approved an ordinance distinguishing e-motorcycles, sometimes called e-motos, from e-bikes, and specifying licensing requirements for the use of e-motorcycles in the city.

Under the ordinance, an e-motorcycle is defined as an electric vehicle that can exceed speeds of 28 mph and has a wattage greater than 750. In contrast, there are three tiers of e-bikes, [the fastest of which provides assistance for up to 28 mph](https://www.cityofboise.org/programs/bike-safety-and-education/e-mobility/), Boise Council Member Jimmy Hallyburton told BoiseDev.

In separating e-motos from e-bikes or e-scooters, the ordinance treats e-motos as a motor vehicle, and specifies that those who operate e-motos on city streets must comply with Idaho’s motor vehicle licensing, titling, registration, and insurance requirements. If the e-moto’s owner can’t obtain such licensing, it can’t be operated on the city’s public roads and highways, the ordinance says. In addition to vehicle licensing requirements, those who operate e-motos have to comply with Idaho law for driver restrictions and requirements.

The change effectively prevents anyone without a driver’s license and an additional motorcycle endorsement from driving an e-moto. To get a motorcycle endorsement, drivers must take a written test, and those under the age of 21 must take and pass a motorcycle training course, according to the [Idaho Transportation Department’s website](https://idahostar.org/faqs/). Those over 21 can take a third-party skills test or can take a rider training course that will waive the skills test requirement, the agency’s website said.

Separately, the city is also launching a micromobility task force to explore and promote the use of e-bikes, e-scooters, and the like while preventing crashes. The task force will have representatives from the Boise School District, the police department, youth and parent representatives, medical professionals, and more.

Emily Allen, policy advisor for the council, said, “The purpose of this task force is to inform any additional policy that may be needed, given the complexity and breadth of the mobility devices that are available today, as well as inform what public education and safety needs are required to clarify this further.”

The task force will meet monthly between August and December, Allen said.

Hallyburton told BoiseDev, “We want people using e-bikes. We think they’re a great form of transportation. We want people using other forms of e-mobility, but we also want to make sure that we’re talking about those separately.”

## Preventing accidents

Hallyburton said that some companies have aggressively marketed e-motos, whose top speeds can exceed 45 mph, to tweens and teens. As use has spread, accidents involving e-motos, including some resulting in deaths, have cropped up around the country, Hallyburton said.

“When you have somebody operating a vehicle that’s just as powerful as a motorcycle, with no training whatsoever, you have the capacity to take an already dangerous vehicle and make it significantly more dangerous,” Hallyburton said.

Boise Chief of Police Chris Dennison told the council during its workshop on Tuesday that the department witnesses youth using the vehicles daily. Bre Brush, a policy advisor with the city, said that over the last two months, the top complaint from residents has been about seeing “folks who pose a danger to themselves and others — they’re weaving in and out of traffic in an unsafe manner, they’re jumping on and off sidewalks, popping wheelies, and really compromising the safety of our roadways.”

Dennison said that the ordinance provides an additional enforcement tool. Officers would need to have a reason to conduct a stop of such a vehicle. Prior to this ordinance change, e-motos were already prohibited on the Boise River Greenbelt and on sidewalks, Dennison said.

## Identifying e-motos

Brush told the council that in tandem with the ordinance’s passage, the city has launched [a new webpage](https://www.cityofboise.org/programs/bike-safety-and-education/e-mobility/) about e-bikes and e-motos as part of a push to educate the public about safety and requirements for their operation.

Brush told the council that if you’re unsure what kind of vehicle you have or your child has, consult the manufacturer you purchased it from, or look at the frame of it — they typically specify the wattage and the top speed, either with a sticker, or by printing it directly on the frame.
