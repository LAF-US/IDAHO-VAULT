## Higher ed cuts revert Idaho’s institutions back to the backwater places found in our literature

Budget cuts threaten to hurt the quality of our education as higher enrollment with fewer professors places undue pressure on educators, writes guest columnist Edward Dorey.

![Idaho State University.](https://idahocapitalsun.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/ISU-3-fall-2020-1536x1024-1.jpg)

Idaho State University.

A student walks across Idaho State University’s campus in Pocatello. (Photo courtesy of Idaho State University)

Last year, Idaho Gov. [Brad Little announced](https://www.idahoednews.org/top-news/budget-cuts-will-force-staff-cuts-at-u-of-i-and-other-campuses/) that he would cut spending on higher education by 3%. The [House bill](https://legislature.idaho.gov/sessioninfo/2026/legislation/H0876/) including these cuts passed in March. Little says the holdback is necessary so that the state’s budget can be balanced — “ [Idahoans expect](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/politics-government/state-politics/article315080216.html) their state government to operate efficiently and effectively, and the balanced budget we approved for the current fiscal year delivers on both fronts.”

These cuts present issues for those planning to get a valuable degree here. Yes, Idaho [has seen historic growth](https://www.idahoednews.org/news/report-idaho-launch-encouraged-more-students-to-attend-in-state-colleges/) in the number of students entering its post-secondary schools, but [Little’s cuts](https://www.idahoednews.org/top-news/state-board-roundup-6-17-26-increasing-class-sizes-wearing-us-down-boise-state-faculty-says/) threaten to hurt the quality of that education as higher enrollment with fewer professors places undue pressure on educators. Like Boise State University is doing, [the University of Idaho](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2025/10/22/budget-cuts-will-force-staff-cuts-at-u-of-i-and-other-campuses/) is decreasing its number of faculty positions due to the cuts, leading to, as Idaho Education Newsreports, “larger class sizes, less individualized learning time and fewer research opportunities for undergrads.”

This likely lower-quality education has an unfortunate historical precedent. Two of the most famous pieces of literature about Idaho exhibit the state as one that people *leave* to receive competent post-secondary educations — Wallace Thurman’s “The Blacker the Berry” (1929) and Tara Westover’s “Educated” (2018).

[“The Blacker the Berry”](https://www.pbsbooks.org/virtual-library/the-blacker-the-berry-a-novel-of-negro-life/) was an important book to the Harlem Renaissance, a Black-led literary movement of the early 20th century. The novel engages with Black interracial prejudice at a time when analyzing such a thing was infrequently done in literature. Its writer, Wallace Thurman, was a Black man who was born in Salt Lake City in 1902. He spent part of his childhood in Boise.

Wallace Thurman’s “The Blacker the Berry” has its main character leave Idaho for an-out of-state university despite the prevalence of colleges in state. “The Blacker the Berry” follows Emma Lou, dark-skinned Black woman who has recently graduated from a Boise public school. Her grandparents had moved to Idaho to escape the horrors of the South in the immediate post Civil-War era.

Because Lou is interested in going to college after her time in high school, her Uncle Joe encourages her to go to the University of Southern California. “There, he reasoned, she would find a larger and more intelligent social circle,” Thurman writes. Lou spends several years at the college before she moves to New York City.

Tara Westover’s “Educated” replicates this theme of Idaho simply not being good enough for those interested in high-quality college education. “Educated” is a memoir that covers Westover’s experiences with her family, a group of isolated, highly conservative, pro-home schooling Latter-day Saints near Clifton, Idaho. Westover’s book was immensely popular, spending [132 consecutive weeks](https://www.nytimes.com/books/best-sellers/2020/09/13/hardcover-nonfiction/) on The New York Times’ hardcover nonfiction bestseller list.

“Educated” critiques the “education” that Westover received in Idaho as a home-schooled, rural child. At home, Westover’s schooling is primarily done with scattered books as, throughout most of the day, the memoirist’s father forces her to work in a junkyard.

It is not until she goes to Brigham Young University and Cambridge University that Westover receives a real education. At 17, Westover begins to attend BYU after getting a 28 on the ACT. Upon receiving a good scholarship to attend the college, Westover’s “professors came into focus, suddenly and sharply … My textbooks began to make sense, and I found myself doing more than the required reading.”

Later, due to outstanding work, a professor recommends her to a program at Cambridge. There, she “began to read — Hume, Rousseau, Smith, Godwin, Wollstonecraft and Mill.” It is only outside of Idaho that Westover really starts learning.

Idaho’s colleges have promise. In 2019, Boise State reclassified as a [“](https://www.boisestate.edu/strategicplan/goal-posts/) Doctoral Research University” for its “High Research Activity”; in 2025, the University of Idaho did this yet with [“](https://www.uidaho.edu/newsroom/carnegie-r1) very high research activity.” But Little’s decision to cut funds threatens to hurt this growth, reverting our state’s intuitions of higher learning back to the backwater places that “The Blacker the Berry” and “Educated” frame them as.
