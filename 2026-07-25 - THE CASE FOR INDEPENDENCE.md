As a fourth-generation Idahoan, I come by my independence honestly. I was raised with a healthy skepticism of government and a deep belief that how we treat our neighbors matters just as much as how our leaders treat us. I have spent my career as a physician assistant, meeting people in emergency rooms on some of the hardest days of their lives. In those moments, party labels do not matter. What matters is trust, accountability, compassion, and whether someone is willing to show up and help. Those experiences have only strengthened my belief that public service should be about people, not political tribes.

I also spent time overseas in Iraq, and that experience profoundly deepened my appreciation for democracy and individual liberty. I lived in a society where one religion dominated public life, where men held nearly all positions of power, where women were often treated as second-class citizens, and where people who were different could face ostracism, abuse, or worse. Seeing those realities firsthand reminded me that the freedoms we often take for granted in America are neither universal nor guaranteed. It strengthened my conviction that self-government, equal rights, and the freedom to disagree are worth protecting.

America was not born from blind loyalty to a party.

It was born from the belief that free people have both the right and the responsibility to question power. Our founding generation did not risk their lives, fortunes, and sacred honor so future citizens could be told they must choose between two political teams and stay quiet once the jersey was handed to them.

They built a republic, not a clubhouse.

Today, too many Americans are told that voting for an independent candidate is risky, unrealistic, or even dangerous. We are warned that candidates outside the two major parties are spoilers, distractions, or people without a real home. But that fear says more about the weakness of our current political system than it does about independent candidates.

Independence is not the absence of values. It is the refusal to outsource your values to a party machine.

The founders understood the danger of faction. They knew that political groups, once they became powerful enough, could begin serving themselves instead of the people. George Washington warned that party loyalty could become a weapon — dividing citizens, inflaming resentment, and turning public service into a contest for power rather than a duty to the country.

That warning feels less like history today and more like a headline.

Across the country, many voters feel politically homeless. They are tired of being told they must accept the whole package from one party or the other. They may be fiscally conservative but socially tolerant. They may support responsible gun ownership and also believe public schools deserve support. They may believe in strong borders and also believe in basic human dignity. They may support veterans, working families, small businesses, rural communities, and constitutional rights without needing permission from a party platform.

That is not confusion. That is citizenship.

The fear around independent candidates often comes from people who benefit from keeping the system exactly as it is. Party insiders want voters to believe there are only two choices because two choices are easier to control. Two choices allow politicians to avoid accountability. If one party fails, it simply points to the other. If both parties ignore the people, they still expect your vote because they have convinced you there is nowhere else to go.

But there is somewhere else to go.

Back to the idea that elected officials should answer first to their constituents — not party bosses, not national donors, not political action committees, not ideological purity tests, and not out-of-state interests.

An independent candidate is not automatically better than a Republican or a Democrat. That would be too easy, and frankly, it would be dishonest. Every candidate should have to earn trust. Every candidate should have to explain what they believe, how they will vote, who they listen to, and who they are accountable to.

But that is exactly the point.

Independent candidates should not be dismissed because they lack a party label. They should be judged by their principles, their record, their competence, and their willingness to represent the people who actually live in the district.

In many ways, independence demands more from a candidate. There is no party brand to hide behind. No national script to recite. No built-in excuse when the vote hurts real people back home. An independent has to stand on their own two feet and tell voters plainly: this is what I believe, this is why I believe it, and this is who I answer to.

That should not scare us. That should be the standard.

The American experiment depends on citizens who are willing to think for themselves. It depends on voters who are not bullied into obedience by fear. It depends on leaders who understand that public office is not a reward for loyalty to a party, but a responsibility to the people.

Independence is not a rejection of America’s political tradition. It is a return to one of its oldest and most important ideas: that government belongs to the people.

Not to parties.

Not to insiders.

Not to the loudest voices on the far edges.

To the people.

So when voters look at an independent candidate, they should not ask, “Why don’t you belong to a party?”

They should ask a better question:

“Who do you belong to?”

For me, the answer is simple.

I belong to the people I hope to represent.

Sincerely,

**Heather S. Lewis**
