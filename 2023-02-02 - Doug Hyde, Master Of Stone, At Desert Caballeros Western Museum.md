Doug Hyde remembers the early days of his career sculpting stone with a screwdriver for lack of better tools. This was shortly after returning from service in the Vietnam War. Hyde received his discharge following the receipt of near fatal wounds in a grenade blast, the result of a scuffle with an enemy soldier who had that grenade down the shirt of a buddy. He remembers that in detail. And happier times. The grandfather who taught him how to hunt and fish, who taught him so much about his Indigenous heritage.

Hyde (b. 1946; Hermiston, Oregon; Nez Perce, Assiniboine, Chippewa) takes center stage through March 5, 2023, at the Desert Caballeros Western Museum in Wickenburg, AZ which presents the exhibition, [“Doug Hyde: Master of Stone and Bronze.”](https://westernmuseum.org/exhibition/doug-hyde-master-of-stone-and-bronze/ "https://westernmuseum.org/exhibition/doug-hyde-master-of-stone-and-bronze/")

[Working in bronze would come later in Hyde’s career](https://broadmoorgalleries.com/doug-hyde/ "https://broadmoorgalleries.com/doug-hyde/"). His first love, a passion which has never subsided, is for stone. Eschewing the use of preparatory sketches or models, Hyde lets the stone tell him how to proceed.

“The shape of the stone would suggest stuff, putting water over it you can see the colors in it, turning it around–I told my wife I’ve got all these stones and they’re all competing for me to work on them, they want me to work on them,” Hyde told Forbes.com of his artistic process. “I’m changing this rock form and now he wants to be a bird, he wants to be able to fly. Changing his shape and now he’s a bird and he can fly or he can swim underwater instead of just going to the bottom. You have imagination and you let things grow. By not doing a drawing or model in advance for most of my things, it allows you to adapt, adapt to the shape of the stone, adapt to what you find inside the material.”

Hyde has been adapting from an early age. When he was about seven, his mother came down with tuberculosis. Unable to care for her eight children, they were spread out to various children’s homes. Hyde went to a small home in [[Idaho]] which doubled as a working farm.

Hyde’s Nez Perce grandfather Frank Henry and Henry’s brothers would come visit the youngster on weekends. Hyde was allowed off school grounds to stay with his grandfather. Sometimes they would camp. Here he would [learn the stories and songs and practices of his ancestors](https://www.medicinemangallery.com/doug-hyde-biography "https://www.medicinemangallery.com/doug-hyde-biography").

“He taught me about shooting and hunting and one of the main things I always remember is he said, ‘you can’t just shoot things to be shooting them; whatever you shoot you have to eat,’” Hyde recalls. “There was no plinking little birds–I did it once and he was going to make me eat this little bird.”

Hyde’s mother was out of the picture due to her illness for some seven years of his childhood. Her absence forced him to become independent at a young age. He left Idaho when he turned 17, finishing high school and completing two years of schooling focused on sculpture at the Institute of American Indian Art in Santa Fe, NM.

Following his time at IAIA, he received a small scholarship from his tribe to attend the prestigious [San Francisco Art Institute](https://www.forbes.com/sites/chaddscott/2023/01/31/mike-hendersons-american-odyssey-on-view-at-university-of-california-davis/?sh=4b8e7aaa4cb6 "https://www.forbes.com/sites/chaddscott/2023/01/31/mike-hendersons-american-odyssey-on-view-at-university-of-california-davis/?sh=4b8e7aaa4cb6"), but it wasn’t a great fit.

“I had nothing in common, absolutely nothing, (with the students there),” Hyde said. “I had already did like 100 sculptures and I got there and people were only working in plaster. I thought I was wasting my time and wasting the tribe’s money by being there. I didn’t think I was doing them justice by being in a school where I wasn’t learning anything.”

## Diversity of Native People

When Hyde first attended IAIA, he studied painting. That didn’t last long. As someone who was always hands-on, tactile, making things, building things, even as a kid–“I used to find little pieces of wood and turn them into little animals,” he recalls–the allure of sculpture captured him quickly. He could visualize what he wanted to create in three dimensions more naturally than in two.

His work continues to consist of animals, and Native people. Native people from all nations, a consequence of his time as a student at IAIA.

“When we went there, we had, I don’t know how many different tribes, but kids from 26 states. We used to have this thing called Culture Day where a group from the southwest would prepare food from the southwest and then do their dances so you could learn their cultures. We’d do it all the time, all throughout the school year,” Hyde said. “Tribes in those days, they're still in the in the ‘fight the other tribe’ kind of mentality. (Culture Day) allowed you to learn something about them and their culture, and that's when we started sharing designs and stuff in the arts.”

This cross-cultural exchange among Indigenous people against a backdrop of contemporary art making and traditional heritage fostered by IAIA when it opened in 1962 proved revolutionary. IAIA remains [the only fine arts college in the world](https://iaia.edu/making-history-celebrating-60-years-of-iaia-and-50-years-of-mocna/ "https://iaia.edu/making-history-celebrating-60-years-of-iaia-and-50-years-of-mocna/") dedicated to the study of contemporary Native American and Alaska Native arts.

“Combining of different tribes–you could be an Alaskan and doing stuff from the Miccosukee in Florida,” Hyde remembers as one example, a summer school project creating artwork for display inside Miccosukee tribal buildings. “I really enjoyed that, the fact that we could mix cultures.”

The creative atmosphere fostered at IAIA [from the early years](https://www.medicinemangallery.com/essential-west-action-abstraction-august-2022 "https://www.medicinemangallery.com/essential-west-action-abstraction-august-2022") through today [has changed Western art](https://www.medicinemangallery.com/essential-west-pottery-steamboat-art-museum-november-2022 "https://www.medicinemangallery.com/essential-west-pottery-steamboat-art-museum-november-2022"), contemporary art and launched [dozens of successful careers](https://iaia.edu/making-history-in-a-photograph/ "https://iaia.edu/making-history-in-a-photograph/"), taking Native American art where it had never gone previously. [Earl Biss](https://www.seegreatart.art/mateo-romero-discusses-artwork-and-influence-of-earl-biss/ "https://www.seegreatart.art/mateo-romero-discusses-artwork-and-influence-of-earl-biss/"), [Dan Namingha](https://www.forbes.com/sites/chaddscott/2022/07/14/a-first-time-visitors-guide-to-indigenous-art-in-santa-fe/ "https://www.forbes.com/sites/chaddscott/2022/07/14/a-first-time-visitors-guide-to-indigenous-art-in-santa-fe/"), [David Bradley](https://www.seegreatart.art/david-bradley-icon-of-contemporary-native-american-painting/ "https://www.seegreatart.art/david-bradley-icon-of-contemporary-native-american-painting/"), Roxanne Swentzell, Anita Fields, [Tony Abeyta](https://www.medicinemangallery.com/abeyta-family-wheelwright-march-2022 "https://www.medicinemangallery.com/abeyta-family-wheelwright-march-2022"), Joy Harjo, Patricia Michaels, [Rose Simpson](https://www.forbes.com/sites/chaddscott/2022/08/25/send-off-summer-with-an-arts-binge-across-massachusetts/ "https://www.forbes.com/sites/chaddscott/2022/08/25/send-off-summer-with-an-arts-binge-across-massachusetts/"), Cara Romero, [Cannupa Hanska Luger](https://www.medicinemangallery.com/essential-west-amarillo-museum-of-art-november-2022 "https://www.medicinemangallery.com/essential-west-amarillo-museum-of-art-november-2022").

Doug Hyde was one of the first with lessons learned there continuing to feature in his sculptures today.
