# The Hebrew Bible + The Talmud

> *"the Torah is a text that cannot be altered — not even a single letter."*
> — Neil Yerman, sofer (master Torah scribe), in Logan Finney's FāVS News reporting, 2026-02-23

## The `+` — written canon and rabbinic interpretive tradition

The Hebrew Bible and the Talmud are not two parallel compilations; they are a **paired relationship**. The Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) is the **written canon** — the inalterable text in scroll form. The Talmud is the **rabbinic interpretive tradition** — the chain through which the written canon is transmitted, interpreted, and applied. The classical formulation is *Torah she-bichtav* (the Written Torah) and *Torah she-be'al peh* (the Oral Torah). The Mishnah (codified ~200 CE) and the Gemara (compiled ~400-500 CE) together form the Talmud — the written record of what was, until that codification, oral tradition.

The chain of transmission is asserted at the opening of Pirkei Avot 1:1:

> *"Moses received the Torah from Sinai and passed it on to Joshua, and Joshua to the Elders and the Elders to the Prophets, and the Prophets passed it on to the Men of the Great Assembly."*

The Men of the Great Assembly (Anshei Knesset HaGedolah) are conventionally located in the early Second Temple period (~5th-3rd centuries BCE) and stand at the cusp where the chain enters the named rabbinic generations.

## The Hebrew Bible (Tanakh)

### Name and three divisions

**Tanakh** (תנ"ך) is an acronym for the three divisions:

- **T**orah (תורה) — Law, Teaching, Instruction
- **N**evi'im (נביאים) — Prophets
- **K**etuvim (כתובים) — Writings

The standard Jewish enumeration counts **24 books**. The Protestant Old Testament contains the identical content reorganized into **39 books**; the Catholic Old Testament adds the deuterocanonical books (~46 total). The difference is enumeration, not content — books that are unified in the Jewish counting are split in the Christian counting (Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, and Ezra-Nehemiah are each one book in Jewish counting; the Twelve Minor Prophets are one book, *Trei Asar*, "The Twelve").

### The 24 books

**Torah (5 books)** — the Pentateuch / Five Books of Moses:

1. Bereshit (Genesis)
2. Shemot (Exodus)
3. Vayikra (Leviticus)
4. Bamidbar (Numbers)
5. Devarim (Deuteronomy)

**Nevi'im (8 books)** — divided into Former Prophets (narrative-historical) and Latter Prophets (oracular):

- *Former*: 6. Joshua · 7. Judges · 8. Samuel · 9. Kings
- *Latter*: 10. Isaiah · 11. Jeremiah · 12. Ezekiel · 13. The Twelve (Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi)

**Ketuvim (11 books)** — Writings, varied in genre:

14. Psalms (Tehillim)
15. Proverbs (Mishlei)
16. Job (Iyov)
17. Song of Songs (Shir HaShirim)
18. Ruth
19. Lamentations (Eichah)
20. Ecclesiastes (Kohelet)
21. Esther
22. Daniel
23. Ezra-Nehemiah
24. Chronicles (Divrei HaYamim)

Within Ketuvim, five short books — Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther — are called the **Five Megillot** (Five Scrolls) and are read liturgically at five festivals (Passover, Shavuot, Tisha B'Av, Sukkot, and Purim, respectively).

### Composition: traditional and modern views

The novice surfaces both views without adjudicating between them.

**Traditional view.** The Torah was given to Moses at Sinai (~13th century BCE in traditional chronology) and passed down through the chain articulated in Pirkei Avot 1:1. The remaining books were composed by named prophets and authors over the following ~1,000 years. Final canonization is conventionally located in the Second Temple period or shortly after.

**Modern critical scholarship.** The **Documentary Hypothesis**, crystallized by Julius Wellhausen in *Prolegomena zur Geschichte Israels* (1878), proposes that the Pentateuch is a compilation of four independent source documents combined by a series of redactors:

- **J (Yahwist)**: ~900-850 BCE, Southern Kingdom of Judah
- **E (Elohist)**: ~750-700 BCE, Northern Kingdom of Israel
- **D (Deuteronomist)**: ~622 BCE, conventionally identified with "the book of the Law" found during Josiah's Temple repairs (2 Kings 22)
- **P (Priestly)**: post-exilic, ~6th-5th century BCE

The hypothesis has been refined, contested, and modified by subsequent scholarship (Form Criticism, Tradition Criticism, the Supplementary Hypothesis, the work of Richard Elliott Friedman and others); the J/E/D/P labels remain the conventional shorthand. Modern critical scholarship generally places canonization of the Tanakh as a process completed by ~100 CE rather than a single act.

The two views are not reconciled in this compilation. Both are surfaced; neither is adjudicated by the novice.

### The Masoretic Text and the doctrine of textual stability

The authoritative form of the Hebrew Bible in Jewish tradition is the **Masoretic Text** (MT) — the consonantal Hebrew text together with vowel-pointing (*nikkud*) and cantillation marks (*te'amim*) developed by the **Masoretes**, Jewish scholar-scribes active primarily in Tiberias, Jerusalem, and Babylonia from approximately the 6th through the 10th centuries CE.

The **Ben Asher** family of Tiberias led the dominant masoretic school for five generations. The standard reference manuscripts:

- **Aleppo Codex** (~930 CE) — consonants scribed by Shlomo ben Buya'a; vocalized and annotated by Aaron ben Moses ben Asher. Conventionally treated as the most authoritative masoretic witness, though portions were lost in 20th-century unrest in Aleppo.
- **Leningrad Codex** (1008 CE) — scribed by Samuel ben Jacob in Cairo, adhering to the Ben Asher tradition. The oldest **complete** surviving Hebrew Bible manuscript; the basis of modern critical editions (*Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*).

The textual-stability tradition is preserved through the discipline of the **sofer** (scribe). The novice has primary-source reporting on this from Logan Finney's own FāVS News journalism (2026-02-23), recording **Neil Yerman**, a master sofer from New York, restoring two century-old Torah scrolls at Congregation Ahavath Beth Israel in Boise. Yerman's words:

> *"the Torah is a text that cannot be altered — not even a single letter."*

The restoration craft Yerman described:

- Sewn with sinew from the legs of a kosher animal (ox or goat)
- A *blind seam* keeping the stitching concealed inside the fold
- Triage of folds, tears, and structural damage before sewing
- Tools: sponges, alcohol, sandpaper, erasers, scalpels
- Rationale: *"the Torah is considered a major sanctity — the spoken word of God — its physical form must appear whole and unbroken"*

This is a contemporary witness, in this vault, to a discipline whose codification reaches back at least 1,500 years.

## The Talmud

The Talmud (תלמוד, from the root *lamad*, "to learn") is the central rabbinic compilation. There are **two Talmuds**: the **Talmud Bavli** (Babylonian Talmud) and the **Talmud Yerushalmi** (Jerusalem / Palestinian Talmud). The Bavli is more authoritative and far more widely studied; the Yerushalmi is shorter, less complete, and was redacted earlier.

Both Talmuds share the same structural foundation: a passage of **Mishnah** followed by **Gemara** — rabbinic commentary, discussion, and application.

### The Mishnah

The **Mishnah** (משנה, *"repetition"* / *"teaching"*) was compiled by **Rabbi Judah haNasi** (also called Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, or simply *Rabbi*) around **200 CE**, conventionally located in Beit Shearim or Sepphoris in the Galilee. The Mishnah is the first major work of rabbinic literature — a redacted record of centuries of oral tradition organized into structured legal-doctrinal form.

The Mishnah contains **63 tractates** (*massekhtot*), organized into **six orders** (*sedarim*). A mnemonic for the orders is **Z'MaN NaKaT**:

1. **Zeraim** (Seeds) — agricultural law; blessings and prayers
2. **Moed** (Appointed Times) — Sabbath, festivals, fast days
3. **Nashim** (Women) — marriage, divorce, vows
4. **Nezikin** (Damages) — civil and criminal law (including **Pirkei Avot**)
5. **Kodashim** (Holy Things) — Temple sacrifices, dietary laws
6. **Tohorot** (Purities) — ritual purity and impurity

**Pirkei Avot** ("Chapters of the Fathers" or "Ethics of the Fathers") sits within the order of Nezikin and is the principal source for the chain-of-transmission account and for ethical maxims of the Tannaitic sages.

### The Gemara and the two Talmuds

The **Gemara** (גמרא, *"completion"*) is the rabbinic commentary on the Mishnah, recording the debates and applications of the **Amoraim** (the post-Mishnaic rabbis).

- **Talmud Yerushalmi** (Jerusalem Talmud) — redacted ~400-450 CE in the Land of Israel. Shorter; covers only the first four Mishnaic orders in detail; ends abruptly. Written in Western (Galilean) Aramaic and Hebrew.

- **Talmud Bavli** (Babylonian Talmud) — traditionally redacted by **Rav Ashi** and **Ravina II** at the academy of Sura, conventionally dated ~500 CE. Modern scholarship more often places final redaction in the mid-6th century, with the post-Talmudic generation of **Savoraim** completing editorial work. Written in Eastern (Babylonian) Aramaic and Hebrew.

The standard print edition is the **Vilna Edition Shas** (Romm Family Press, Vilna, 1870s-1880s) — 25 folio volumes containing the Mishnah, Gemara, the medieval commentaries of Rashi (inner margin) and Tosafot (outer margin), and cross-references. It remains the basis for almost all subsequent editions, reproduced photomechanically worldwide.

### The generations of rabbis (Chazal)

*Chazal* (חז״ל) is the standard acronym for *Chakhameinu Zikhronam Livrakha* — "our Sages, may their memory be a blessing." The named generations form a continuous chain:

- **Zugot** ("Pairs", ~2nd-1st century BCE) — five paired teacher-and-student leaderships of the Sanhedrin. The last pair: **Hillel** and **Shammai**.
- **Tannaim** ("repeaters/teachers", ~10-220 CE) — the Mishnaic-era sages, conventionally counted in six generations. Notable: Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai, Rabbi Akiva, Rabbi Meir, Rabbi Judah haNasi (final compiler).
- **Amoraim** ("speakers/interpreters", ~220-500 CE) — the Talmudic-era commentators. Notable: Rav, Shmuel, Rabbi Yohanan, Rabbi Yehudah, Rava, Abaye.
- **Savoraim** ("reasoners", ~500-625 CE) — post-Talmudic editorial generation.
- **Geonim** ("eminences", ~600-1000 CE) — heads of the Babylonian academies (Sura, Pumbedita).
- **Rishonim** ("early ones", ~1000-1500 CE) — medieval authorities. Notable: **Rashi** (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, 1040-1105, Troyes — the standard Talmud commentator), **Rambam** (Moses Maimonides, 1138-1204, Cordoba/Cairo — author of the *Mishneh Torah* and the philosophical *Guide for the Perplexed*), **Ramban** (Moses Nachmanides, 1194-1270, Catalonia/Acre).
- **Acharonim** ("later ones", ~1500-present) — early modern and modern authorities.

## Key principles

### *Lo bashamayim hi* — "It is not in heaven" (Bava Metzia 59b)

The Talmudic story of the **Oven of Akhnai** (*tanur shel akhnai*) is the locus classicus for the doctrine that interpretive authority belongs to the rabbis on earth, not to direct divine revelation after Sinai.

The story is set in the early 2nd century CE. The dispute concerns the ritual purity status of a particular type of oven (one cut into segments width-wise, with sand placed between the segments). **Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrcanus** holds it cannot become impure; the **Sages** hold that it can. When his logical arguments fail to convince the Sages, Rabbi Eliezer appeals to miracles:

- A carob tree is uprooted from its place (100 cubits, or 400 in another version)
- A stream reverses its flow
- The walls of the study hall lean inward to fall

**Rabbi Yehoshua ben Hananiah** rebukes the walls: *"If Torah scholars are contending with each other in matters of halakha, what is the nature of your involvement?"* The walls stop leaning (they do not return to upright, out of respect for both Rabbi Yehoshua and Rabbi Eliezer).

Rabbi Eliezer finally calls on Heaven itself. A **bat kol** (heavenly voice) declares that the halakha is with Rabbi Eliezer in every place that he expresses an opinion. Rabbi Yehoshua stands and replies, citing Deuteronomy 30:12:

> *"It is not in heaven."*

He continues: since the Torah was already given at Mount Sinai, the rabbis do not regard a heavenly voice — for the Torah itself says (Exodus 23:2), *"after a majority to incline."* The majority of sages had ruled against Rabbi Eliezer, and Heaven's voice does not override the majority ruling.

The traditional gloss on the story: God smiles and says, *"My children have defeated Me, My children have defeated Me."*

The aftermath is somber. The items Rabbi Eliezer had declared pure were burned. Rabbi Eliezer was placed under ban (*niddui*) by the Sages. The story preserves the cost as well as the principle.

### *Eilu v'eilu divrei Elohim chayim* — "These and these are the words of the living God" (Eruvin 13b)

The **School of Hillel** (Beit Hillel) and the **School of Shammai** (Beit Shammai) disputed on legal questions for three years. Each insisted the halakha agreed with their view. A **bat kol** issued:

> *"Eilu va-eilu divrei Elokim Hayyim"* — *"These and these are the words of the living God."*

Both sides have standing as authentic Torah; *and* the halakha follows Beit Hillel.

The reason given: Beit Hillel were *agreeable and forbearing*. They taught both their own statements and those of Beit Shammai, and when citing a dispute they would mention Beit Shammai's view before their own.

The principle has become foundational for rabbinic doctrine on legitimate disagreement (*machloket*): a minority opinion is not erased but preserved, even when not adopted as practice. The Talmud famously preserves the minority opinions of Rabbi Eliezer, Rabbi Akiva's students, and many others alongside the majority rulings.

### *Machloket l'shem shamayim* — "Disagreement for the sake of Heaven" (Pirkei Avot 5:17)

The Mishnah distinguishes between disputes that endure because they pursue truth (the dispute of Hillel and Shammai) and disputes that do not (the dispute of Korach and his assembly, from Numbers 16). The disagreements of the sages are not anomalies in the tradition; they constitute it.

## The rabbi as teacher

The contemporary articulation of the rabbi's role, in the novice's vault-resident primary source — Logan Finney's FāVS News interview with **Rabbi Johanna Hershenson** of Congregation Ahavath Beth Israel, Boise (2026-02-23):

> *"The rabbi is a teacher. In theory, the most learned person in the community, so the community can lean on the rabbi. I generally conduct services with volunteers who play music, and often on Saturday morning we'll have people in the congregation come lead some of the prayers. You don't need a rabbi to lead services."*

This matches the rabbinic tradition's own self-articulation: rabbinic authority is grounded in learning and interpretation, not in priestly mediation or hierarchical position above the community. The Tannaim and Amoraim were not priests — the priestly class (the **Kohanim**) had a separate Temple function that ended with the Temple's destruction in 70 CE. Rabbinic authority succeeded priestly authority in the post-Temple period; its mechanism is the chain of learning, not the chain of bloodline.

## Etymology notes

- **Torah** — from the Hebrew root *yarah* (יָרָה), "to throw, shoot, point, instruct." Often translated "Law," but more precisely "Teaching" or "Instruction."
- **Talmud** — from the root *lamad* (לָמַד), "to learn." Cognate with *talmid* (student).
- **Mishnah** — from the root *shanah* (שָׁנָה), "to repeat, to teach." Cognate with *sheni* (second).
- **Gemara** — from the Aramaic *gemar* (גְּמַר), "to complete, to finish."
- **Halakha** — from the root *halakh* (הָלַךְ), "to walk." Law as "the way one walks."
- **Aggadah** — from the root *nagad* (נָגַד), "to tell." Narrative, ethical, and homiletic material (as distinguished from legal Halakha).
- **Shibboleth** (שִׁבֹּלֶת) — "ear of grain" or "stream/flood." The comprehension test of Judges 12:6 — the Ephraimites could not pronounce the *sh* sound, saying *sibboleth* instead, and were killed by the Gileadites at the Jordan fords. The vault uses *shibboleth* in its broader sense — a comprehension gate.

## What this compilation does NOT do

- **Does not adjudicate the traditional vs. modern-scholarship views on composition.** Both surfaced; neither standing.
- **Does not survey every book or tractate.** Structure, principles, and primary citations point to depth; a full survey would require its own bibliography.
- **Does not claim halakhic authority on any matter.** This is research compilation; the rabbinic authorities cited adjudicate within their own tradition.
- **Does not collapse rabbinic Judaism into a single voice.** *Machloket* is the form; preserving disagreement is the tradition.
- **Does not extend beyond the rabbinic period.** Kabbalah (medieval-to-modern mystical tradition), Hasidic literature, and the Reform / Conservative / Modern Orthodox / Reconstructionist developments of modernity all lie outside the scope of "Hebrew Bible + Talmud" as LOGAN framed it.
