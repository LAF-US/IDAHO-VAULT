If you proudly describe yourself as either “pro-choice” or “pro-life,” you’ve already made up your mind on the Reproductive Freedom and Privacy Act.

But lots and lots of people have moderate views on abortion. They don’t think it should be legal in all cases, but they think, for example, that if a woman’s health is at risk, she should be able to obtain one. Or they think they should be available in the very early term but not in the later terms of pregnancy.

Idaho is more conservative on the issue than the country as a whole, but not as conservative as you might think. [Idahoans are about evenly split](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/06/12/americans-views-on-abortion-differ-by-state/) on the question of whether abortion should be legal in most or all cases.

With the recent news that the Reproductive Freedom and Privacy Act will be on the November ballot, such voters find themselves in a quandary. Proposition 1 likely entails abortion policy that is more liberal than they would like, while the status quo — where a woman can’t get an abortion even if there’s no chance the fetus will live and her health or future fertility is at risk — is far too extreme for them.

What to do?

If you’re one of these voters, just ask yourself this question: Is it more likely for the Idaho Legislature to loosen or tighten abortion laws in the future? If the answer to this question is obvious, your vote should be, too.

It’s naive to think that passing the proposition would decide Idaho’s state policy going forward. It amends state law once, and the Legislature can immediately amend it again.

This is exactly what’s happened with Idaho’s most recent successful proposition: Medicaid expansion, which was passed nearly a decade ago, has been repeatedly modified. And as you would expect, the modifications have been to add restrictions, additional qualifications or to cut benefits.

So the policy we have today is something between what the proponents of Medicaid expansion wanted and what the Legislature wanted. But if it had not passed, we would simply have the Medicaid restrictions the Legislature passed without expansion.

The smart money is that something quite similar would happen if Proposition 1 were to pass. The language of the proposition would become the baseline, and Republican lawmakers would over time pass laws that would introduce greater restrictions on abortion.

The overwhelming likelihood is that it will be significantly amended over time by the Republican-dominated Legislature. They will go after the lowest hanging fruit — things like later term abortions, abortions without parental consent, etc.

But a clear message from voters would place countervailing political constraints on them. As Medicaid expansion demonstrates clearly, it’s politically difficult to outright reverse the will of the voters. We still have Medicaid expansion, though in a modified form.

There are lots of Republican lawmakers who know there’s a problem with current law. They knew there were problems when they passed them. But the incentives against any kind of vote that could be seen as protecting women’s choice in the matter are overwhelming.

So if you’re somewhere in the middle on abortion, voting yes on Proposition 1 is probably how you get state policy closer to your preferences. If it passes, it is unlikely that Proposition 1 will be the law in 10 years. But if it fails, it’s likely abortion policy will get even more punitive and extreme than it is now.

On the current track, it is entirely realistic to imagine a day soon where women who obtain abortions could be charged with murder — this is the avowed position of many far-right lawmakers, and some have previously introduced legislation to this effect. The official platform of the Idaho Republican Party backs not only this view but supports [outlawing abortion in the cases of rape or incest.](https://idgop.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2026/06/2026-Convention-Platform-Report.pdf)

So it is not hard to see a day in the future where if a teenager is raped by her father, and she travels out of state to have an abortion, she could face life imprisonment. That is the endgame for the political party with supermajority control in Idaho, though it is miles from the views of the typical Idaho voter.

The only realistic prospect of arresting the continuous slide set off by the overturn of Roe v. Wade in Idaho is a yes vote on the Reproductive Freedom and Privacy Act.

*Bryan Clark is an opinion writer for the Idaho Statesman.*
