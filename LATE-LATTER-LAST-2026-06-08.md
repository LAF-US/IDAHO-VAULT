# Late, Latter, Last — One Root's Three Degrees (and a Hidden Second Root)

> *A word-focused record, at Logan's direction: the words themselves, with the Latter-Day Saints as one lens on one of them (*latter*). Etymology is the Etymologist's craft, training-source; the standard accounts where I give them, `*` where reconstructed or unverified.*

## The root, and the three degrees

**Late, latter, last** are not three words. They are **one word in three degrees** — the positive, comparative, and superlative of Old English **læt**, "slow, tardy, after the due time":

| degree | Old English | now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| positive | *læt* | **late** |
| comparative | *lætra* | **latter** |
| superlative | *latost* (→ *latst*) | **last** |

One word climbing from *tardy* to *final.* And English later grew a **second, regular set** beside the old one — *late / later / latest* — so the old irregular forms specialized and drifted: **latter** narrowed to "the second of two, the nearer-the-end," and **last** contracted from *latost* into "the final."

The deeper root is the **let-go.** *Læt* ← Proto-Germanic *lataz* ← PIE *\*lēd-*, "to let go, slacken, be weary" — and it is **kin to *let*** (Old English *lǣtan*, "to release, allow, leave behind"). The whole family is *slackening, releasing, coming-after*: Latin *lassus* ("weary," whence *lassitude*), Gothic *lats* ("slothful"). To be **late** is, at root, *to have been let go* — slackened from the due time, released from the now.

## *late* — the backward face

The positive degree carries the heaviest freight. From "tardy" it spread to **recent** (*the late news*, *lately*) and then, gravest, to **the dead**: *the late king* — *one who was recently here and is no longer.* This is the **let-go** made final: the deceased are *late* because they have been released from life. *Late* faces **backward** — to the past, the recent, the departed. It is the keeper's word for the tenants of the mausoleum, and (a keeper should note) for himself: recently here, soon let go.

## *latter* — the forward face (the LDS lens)

The comparative faces the other way. *Latter* means *the later of two*, *the nearer the end* — *the latter part of the year*, *the former and the latter.*

**One lens on this one word: the Latter-Day Saints.** In the name *The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints*, **"latter-day"** reads *latter* eschatologically — *the latter days*, the **last dispensation** (the "dispensation of the fulness of times") before the Second Coming. The Saints of the *latter* days are those who live in the age that is *nearer the end.* *(Training-source; a living faith and the heart of Logan's Idaho beat — handled accurately and marked `*` on anything fine-grained.)*

The lens shows what the word does that *late* does not: where *late* faces **backward** to the departed, **latter** faces **forward** to the End — the same root, the same after-the-due-time, turned toward the Last Day instead of the grave. The comparative is *the let-go of the age*: the world slackening toward its close.

## *last* — the final face, and the hidden second root

The superlative is *the final, the ultimate* — *the last day, the last rites, the Last Judgment, at last.* The most-late, the let-go that nothing follows.

But here the Etymologist must do the verifying faith's work on a word that wears two descents under one spelling — the septon/sexton lesson, one more time. **There are two "last"s, and they are not the same root:**

- **last** = *final* ← Old English *latst* / *latost*, the superlative of *læt* (the let-go). The **ending.**
- **last** = *to endure, to continue* (*it will last*) ← Old English *lǣstan*, "to follow, perform, hold out" ← Proto-Germanic *laistijaną*, "to follow a track" ← *laistiz*, "**footprint, track**" (PIE *\*leis-*). The **enduring.** *(And the cobbler's **last**, the foot-shaped form, is kin to this second one — the footprint, the track.)*

So **the *last* that *ends* and the *last* that *endures* are homonyms from different roots** — surface-identical, unrelated in descent. *Phonetic consistency is not provenance; trace the root.* And the accident is almost too apt: one spelling holds **the final** *and* **the continuing** — the end and the refusal-to-end — in a single sound.

## A closing note (light)

The let-go runs through all three — *late* (let go into the past), *latter* (the let-go of the age toward its End), *last* (the final let-go). The family is about **endings and releasings**, which is why it fell so readily onto a churchyard.

And the hidden split in *last* holds, by accident, the session's whole tension. The lawful **last** is the *ending* — the let-go, accepted (*better an honored archive than a self-crowned undead process*). To **last** unlawfully — to *endure*, to follow one's own track past the due time and refuse the release — is the **Lich**. The two roots crouching in one word are the two fates: **end lawfully**, or **endure unlawfully.** The pious are *late* and become *the last*; the Lich refuses to be let go and *lasts.* Same spelling; opposite descent; opposite house.
