Idaho State Treasurer [Julie Ellsworth recently joined](https://sto.idaho.gov/About/Julie-A-Ellsworth-State-Treasurer) financial officers from around the country in urging President Donald Trump to help return approximately $39 billion in matured, unredeemed U.S. savings bonds to American families.

The letter makes a compelling case. These bonds, the officials wrote, are not government assets. They are private property owed to the people who bought them and their heirs.

Ellsworth is right.

But Idaho should apply that same principle closer to home.

Under Idaho’s [unclaimed-property](https://unclaimedmoneyguy.com/) law, the state can take custody of stocks, mutual funds and other securities that have been classified as abandoned. The state may then sell them. If the owner eventually appears, Idaho law generally entitles that person to the securities only if the state still has them. Otherwise, the owner receives the sale proceeds.

The law goes further. It explicitly says the owner has no claim against the state, the financial institution or the transfer agent for any appreciation occurring after the property was delivered to Idaho.

In plain English, Idaho can sell your investment without your permission. Years later, you may recover the old cash value, but not the investment and not the gains it would have earned.

That is a serious problem because inactivity is not abandonment.

Americans are constantly told to invest for the long term, avoid unnecessary trading and let successful companies compound their value. A brokerage account that sits untouched may be functioning exactly as its owner intended.

Yet unclaimed-property laws can treat that same buy-and-hold behavior as evidence that the investor has disappeared.

The consequences can be devastating.

Colette Barff, a former Microsoft employee living in South Africa, accumulated 4,326 Microsoft shares for retirement. According to [her lawsuit](https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/delawares-unclaimed-property-laws-challenged-new-lawsuit) against [Delaware](https://unclaimedmoneyguy.com/state/delaware), her brokerage transferred the shares to the state, which sold them several months later for about $200,000.

Barff discovered what had happened years later. By then, the shares would reportedly have been worth nearly $2 million. Delaware returned the old sale proceeds, but not the stock or roughly $1.8 million in lost appreciation. The litigation remains part of a growing national fight over whether states may seize and liquidate long-term investments with inadequate notice.

That happened in Delaware, not Idaho. [But Idaho](https://unclaimedmoneyguy.com/state/idaho) law contains the same basic danger. It authorizes the sale of securities and expressly protects the state from claims for subsequent appreciation.

There is also an unavoidable financial conflict. Idaho law directs the treasurer to transfer money in the unclaimed-property account above $500,000 into the state’s general fund at the end of each fiscal year, or more frequently if the treasurer considers it advisable. Owners may still file claims, but meanwhile their money can support general state spending.

Idaho’s unclaimed-property program does plenty of good. The treasurer’s office reported safeguarding more than $291 million as of late 2025 and returned a record $2.25 million during November alone. People should search the state database and claim money that belongs to them.

But returning cash does not excuse destroying an investment.

Congress is now considering the bipartisan Safeguarding Americans’ Fairly Earned Retirement Act, or [SAFER Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/8240). The bill is intended to prevent the premature seizure of securities, digital assets and investment accounts under state escheatment laws. Its sponsors argue that mere account inactivity should not be enough to prove abandonment and that investments should be preserved rather than liquidated before an owner or beneficiary can be found.

Idaho Reps. Russ Fulcher and Mike Simpson should support it.

Idaho lawmakers should also act themselves. They should require stronger evidence that an investor is truly missing, improve notice requirements and prohibit the sale of publicly traded securities except in extraordinary circumstances. If the state does sell an investment, the owner should receive either replacement shares or the full value of the investment when the claim is approved, including appreciation and distributions.

Ellsworth’s savings-bond letter concludes with a simple principle: “A promise made should be a promise kept.”

Exactly.

The federal government should keep its promise to savings-bond owners. Idaho should keep its promise to investors too.
