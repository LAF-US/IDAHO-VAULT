Upgrading stormwater drainage outlets that discharge to Payette Lake could keep more pollutants from road runoff out of the lake, according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency.

A study commissioned by the federal agency at no cost to the city estimated about $1.8 million would be needed to complete the upgrades to three drainage outlets in Art Roberts Park and Legacy Park.

Existing filtration systems in the city meet state standards, according to the study, but do not capture all pollutants before the stormwater pours into the lake, which is the city’s source of drinking water.

“The city is concerned that its current stormwater treatment best management practices may not treat stormwater runoff thoroughly enough to protect the water quality of Payette Lake,” the study said.

McCall currently relies mostly on underground [“Vortechs” treatment systems](https://www.conteches.com/stormwater-management/hydrodynamic-separation/vortechs/), which swirl stormwater to separate sediment and other pollutants before discharging the water to the lake. During large storms, however, those systems can be overwhelmed, flushing previously captured pollutants downstream into the lake.

“They do a good job, as long as you maintain them,” McCall Public Works Director Nathan Stewart said. “But those are typically used as pre-treatment devices for filters.”

The filtration systems recommended by the EPA would increase removal rates by an estimated 22% to 45%, depending on the pollutant.

They would also be designed to capture a recently discovered contaminant known as 6PPD, a chemical used in tire rubber to increase resistance to cracking and wear.

As tires wear down, the chemical is deposited on roadways and broken down further by traffic until it is washed into stormwater drainage systems.

![St. Luke's Health Plan - 2026 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/3cffa70b-bd30-43bc-8a9e-62008bbc7dff.png)

Research has [linked 6PPD to harm to cold-water fish species in the Pacific Northwest](https://wdfw.wa.gov/species-habitats/science/marine-toxics/6ppd). In Payette Lake, that could mean trouble for several types of trout, including bull trout[, a federally listed threatened species](https://www.fws.gov/species/bull-trout-salvelinus-confluentus). It may also have human health impacts, according to the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife.

The chemical has not been confirmed in the lake, EPA consultants said, but it could be detected by testing fish in the lake.

“We haven’t done monitoring in Payette Lake to know for sure that it’s present, but there’s research-backed ways to point to that connection,” said Maureen Harris of AECOM, the environmental engineering firm hired to complete the study.

The EPA study did not analyze all of the city’s stormwater drainage outlets into Payette Lake, but instead focused on the three main outlets for downtown McCall.

“The majority of the stormwater runoff is coming from transportation (sources) between the city and the Idaho Transportation Department, so it seemed like a fairly direct connection to make,” Harris said.

## Phosphorus and suspended solids

Beyond 6PPD, the study found that improved filtration in the city’s stormwater outlets could reduce the amount of total suspended solids and phosphorus entering the lake.

![St. Luke's Health Plan - 2026 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/3cffa70b-bd30-43bc-8a9e-62008bbc7dff.png)

St. Luke's Health Plan - 2026 2:1

Phosphorus can fuel toxic algae blooms, while suspended solids can make the water murky, lower oxygen levels in the water, harm fish habitat, and increase strain on filters in the city’s drinking water treatment plant.

The Idaho Department of Environmental Quality has not established a phosphorus limit for Payette Lake to maintain water quality standards. However, a 2024 report by the DEQ found water samples from the lake exceeded targets established in a 1998 water quality management plan for the lake.

The DEQ report recommended increased nutrient reduction efforts not only to improve phosphorus levels in Payette Lake, but also to reduce downstream impacts to Lake Cascade, which has been plagued [by phosphorus-fueled algae blooms for decades.](https://valleylookout.com/2025/08/19/health-advisory-issued-for-lake-cascade-boat-ramp/)

## Funding, impact unclear

While the McCall City Council was receptive to the recommended improvements, paying for them presents a challenge.

Little grant funding is currently available for the work, Stewart said. Low-interest loans are available through DEQ, but those would still require the city to find money to repay the debt.

“That’s the only thing preventing us from doing what I would say is the right thing,” Council Member Bob Giles said. “I mean, this is a very sensitive area.”

The EPA study, however, will help position the city well for future grant funding should it become available, Stewart said.

“It’s extremely valuable and every little piece of information helps,” McCall Mayor Colby Nielsen said of the EPA study.

It also remains unclear how much the $1.8 million investment would improve water quality because scientists have limited data on the pollutants in Payette Lake, and regulatory targets have not been established for many of them.

![City of McCall - Visiting? 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/57592d17-0c69-4d28-a6e9-c5d629733da1.png)

City of McCall - Visiting? 2:1

Council Member Julie Thrower suggested testing for 6PPD could be added to upcoming efforts to increase near-shore water quality monitoring in Payette Lake. Stewart agreed, adding that more data would be key in deciding whether the investment is worth the expense.

“I think getting some more technical information on the lake in general, and what is really the driving force behind any eutrophication, or if any eutrophication is actually happening, will help guide us on where we should put our investment,” he said.
