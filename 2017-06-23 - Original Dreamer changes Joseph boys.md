Smohalla, Chief Joseph elder and younger

When you Google Smohalla, a spiritual leader of the Wanapum behind the Dreamer religion of 19th century — known today as the Seven Drums, or Washat — the first image to show up, for some reason, is one of Young Joseph, of the Nez Perce tribe.

Smohalla is a far less photographed Indian of the day, of the generation closer to the latter’s father, Old Joseph, or Tuekakas.

Old Joseph was part of the Nez Perce who had adopted the Christian religion — something that Smohalla criticizes, as is presented at the Wanapum Heritage Center near Priest Rapids. Smohalla, born about 1815, tells Captain E.L. Higgins in a text bubble mounted on the museum wall that Old Joseph’s move to adopt Christianity was pathetic:

“Joseph’s people had good fields and gardens,” Smohalla says, “but they were driven away. I have no pity for them. They had no business to plant fields like the white men. Many indians are trying to live like white men but it will do them no good. They cut off their hair and wear white man’s clothes and some of them learn to sing out of a book.”

Smohalla believed the Christian beliefs many Indians were adopting and spreading would lead to the destruction of their people and way of life. Joseph’s son, the more famous Chief Joseph, was about 7 years old when his father made the switch. *(It’s worthy to note that most photos of young Joseph include him sporting a Dreamer religion hairstyle, the open forehead with hair or braids coming down the shoulders.)*

Watching his father bounce from traditional Nez Perce beliefs to Christianity and to the Dreamer religion probably influenced young Joseph and his followers.

“My father was the first to see through the schemes of the white man,” he said.

After the famous journey where he and hundreds more eluded the U.S. Army, then suffered imprisonment and were subsequently released, young Joseph was caught between a rock and a hard place. An interpretor told his loyal friend, Yellow Wolf: “Lapwai and be Christian, or Colville and just be yourself?”

Joseph probably would have preferred an alternative: “Wallowa and be myself,” but, instead, in 1884 he and 149 others were shipped on a train to the Colville reservation, where they were approved for relocation by the government.

Now in a place where he had the peace he famously spoke of desiring, it is said he dove further into the Dreamer religion. This statement reaffirmed Joseph’s stance: “We do not want churches because they will teach us to quarrel with God, as the Catholic and Protestants do. We do not want to learn that. We may quarrel with men sometimes about things on earth. But we never quarrel about God. We do not want to learn that.”

It’s recorded many of the estimated 2,000-member following left the Dreamer religion near the end of Smohalla’s life near the turn of the century (he didn’t quite come through on the prophecy that all dead Indians would be resurrected). But the influence started by this Wanapum leader was carried on and can still be seen here on Colville today.

Just in 2017, the Colvilles agreed to have the Ancient One, widely known as Kennewick Man, buried according to Seven Drums tradition, maybe due to the fact that they were found relatively close to tribes who have a denser number of members devoted to the ways.

Also, a new Nez Perce longhouse is planned for the future after a fire took down the old one.

And the Tribe renovated Skolaskin’s church, a Washat building that was originally near Whitestone but now in Nespelem.

Also of note, acting IHS director Chris Buchanan received a Seven Drums song during a luncheon at the Lucy Covington Government Center.

But 100 years after the time of Smohalla in 1895, animosity remained.

In 1995, the late Horace Axtell, of the Nez Perce Tribe of [[Idaho]], gave a Seven Drums funeral service for a cousin. “As soon as I got up there with the bell and began singing, people left. And they didn’t come back, my own relatives,” he told the Spokesman-Review.

Something tells me Smohalla and the Josephs — who made what must have been a difficult break from many of their Nez Perce relatives — would be proud to see their ways carried on in the face of doubt.

Think about that next time you hear a Washat bell ringing and feel compelled to join in.
