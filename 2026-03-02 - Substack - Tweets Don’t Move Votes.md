For the past couple of years, a handful of social media influencers have tried to convince Idahoans that political power can be measured in followers.

Build a large audience on X, they said, and you can shape legislation. Tweets move votes. Tweets move the needle.

We now know that simply isn’t true.

Over the last 48 hours, establishment politicians have wreaked havoc on several conservative immigration bills.

Yesterday, Sen. Kelly Anthon quietly sent two of the measures to a committee where they will almost certainly never see the light of day again.

Today, a committee handpicked by Speaker Mike Moyle killed another proposal — one that would have prevented Idahoans from providing certain forms of aid and shelter to illegal immigrants.

None of this surprised veteran political observers.

Anthon has long maintained close relationships with industries that benefit from cheap labor. Campaign records show he has received multiple donations from the Idaho Dairy PAC, a political group that opposes E-Verify and favors immigration amnesty policies.

Moyle’s ties to that sector are less pronounced, but he also has a history of advancing legislation that would create pathways to legal status for undocumented workers.

Both men also have their eyes on higher office.

Anthon is widely believed to be positioning himself for a congressional run should Rep. Mike Simpson ever step aside. Moyle, meanwhile, has made little secret of his ambitions for the governor’s office.

To reach those goals, maintaining good relationships with the Idaho Association of Commerce and Industry — as well as major agricultural interests — is essential.

And that is exactly what they are doing.

The timing is almost comical. These moves come just days after candidate filing closed. With no challengers on the ballot, both men can comfortably ignore conservative voters and govern exactly as they please.

Yet these realities seem lost on the chronically online activists who mistake X for real life.

Many have convinced themselves that tweets, retweets, and quote tweets can meaningfully change legislative outcomes.

That belief has always been nonsense.

None of this means X has zero value. The platform can be extremely powerful when it comes to shaping narratives, informing activists, and igniting political controversies. I’ve seen it trigger political firestorms more than once — occasionally even here in Idaho.

But its reach is limited.

At most, there are perhaps 7,000 to 9,000 highly engaged Idahoans who regularly participate in political discussions on the platform. That’s fine if you want to debate policy with a small circle of insiders.

It’s not enough to pass legislation.

Idaho has hundreds of thousands of registered voters, and only a tiny fraction of them spend their time arguing politics on X.

In fact, I originally planned to make this point later this year, after Old State Saloon owner Mark Fitzpatrick loses the Republican primary to Gov. Brad Little.

Fitzpatrick may be the most “online” candidate Idaho has ever seen. His provocative posts generate plenty of impressions on X.

But impressions are not a constituency.

My guess is he receives somewhere around 15 percent of the vote in the primary. A particularly strong showing might push that number closer to 25 percent.

Either way, the result will be the same: defeat.

Despite the tweets.

The lesson here is simple, and it has always been simple.

Tweets don’t move votes.

Social media can be a useful tool in a political operative’s toolbox. But it cannot replace the hard work of campaigning — fundraising, organizing, building coalitions, persuading voters, and communicating with people who don’t live their lives online.

To pretend otherwise isn’t just naive.

It’s political malpractice.
