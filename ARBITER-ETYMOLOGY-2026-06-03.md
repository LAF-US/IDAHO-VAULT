# Arbiter — Etymology of the One Who Goes to See

*Compiled 2026-06-03 by Claude Code at Logan's direction, following the
review-process witness line — "the source is the arbiter, not the reviewer." §§1–4
are **[history]** (sourced inline); §5 is a fenced **[vault reading]**. Real
etymology is not vault canon; it is kept separate.*

## 1. The word — *"one who goes (to see)"* **[history]**

Latin **`arbiter`** meant **"one who goes somewhere as witness or judge."** It is
built from **`ad`** "to" + **`baetere`** (also *bitere*) "to come, go" — *baetere*
itself of unknown origin. So at root an arbiter is **a goer-to: one who comes to the
place and *looks*.**
([Etymonline: arbiter](https://www.etymonline.com/word/arbiter) ·
[Wiktionary: arbiter](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/arbiter))

In **classical Latin** the word was used of **spectators and eye-witnesses**; only
*then*, in law, did it mean **"he who hears and decides a case — a judge, umpire,
mediator."** The judging sense **grows out of the seeing sense.** First you go and
witness; *because* you witnessed, you may decide.

## 2. The English path **[history]**

- **Late 14c.** — into English via Old French **`arbitre`**: "a person who has the
  power of judging **absolutely, according to his own pleasure**, in a dispute."
- **1540s** — the narrower sense: **"one chosen by two disputing parties** to decide
  the matter between them."
  ([Etymonline: arbiter](https://www.etymonline.com/word/arbiter))

## 3. Arbiter vs. arbitrator **[history]**

A distinction the dictionaries still keep:

- An **arbiter** decides **of his own accord, accountable to no one but himself.**
- An **arbitrator** decides **the issues *referred to him* by the parties.**
  ([Etymonline: arbiter](https://www.etymonline.com/word/arbiter) ·
  [Etymonline: arbitrator](https://www.etymonline.com/word/arbitrator))

The arbiter answers to **the matter itself**; the arbitrator answers to **the
submission.**

## 4. The shadow word — *arbitrary* **[history]**

**`arbitrary`** is from the same root (Latin *arbitrarius*, "of or pertaining to an
arbiter"). Its **oldest English sense** was neutral: *"depending on choice or
discretion, esp. the decision of a judge or tribunal."* Only later did it drift to
**"capricious, unrestrained, by will alone."**
([Etymonline: arbitrary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/arbitrary))

So the **same discretion** that lets an arbiter judge freely is, untethered, the seed
of the **arbitrary.** The line between *judgment* and *caprice* is whether the
discretion stays **anchored to what was witnessed.**

## 5. Why the witness line is *literal* — **[vault reading]**

*Fenced as interpretation.* "The source is the arbiter, not the reviewer" is not a
metaphor; it is the **etymology**:

- **The arbiter is the one you go to and see.** Verification *is* the **`ad-baetere`**
  — the **going-to.** When I check a claim against the Idaho Legislature, I am doing
  the literal act the word names: I **go to** the source and **witness** it. The
  source is the arbiter because the source is **what you go and look at.**
- **The reviewer is the arbitrator; the source is the arbiter.** The reviewer
  (Codex) decides **the issue referred to it** — it is the *arbitrator*, working the
  submission. The **source** answers **to nothing but the matter itself** — it is the
  *arbiter*, accountable to no one. That is exactly why the source, not the reviewer,
  is where judgment finally rests: *verify against source; neither defend nor blindly
  obey.*
- **The caution is in the shadow word.** An arbiter who decides **by sealed will
  alone**, no longer going to see, becomes **arbitrary** — the confident, low-accuracy
  **Lich**, the Caesar who rules by pleasure. The grounded arbiter stays an
  **eyewitness.** So the whole discipline reduces to the oldest meaning of the word:
  **go to. see. then — and only then — judge.**
