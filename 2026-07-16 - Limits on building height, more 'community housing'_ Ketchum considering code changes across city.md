Ketchum is considering a series of changes to its code that could shape the appearance of its downtown and, leaders hope, encourage the development of deed-restricted housing.

Based on community feedback gathered over the past 18 months, city staff [drafted proposed updates](https://projectketchum.org/code-updates/) that would revise building height limits in downtown and residential areas, establish new design standards for buildings taller than two stories, and update the city’s Community Housing Incentive Program and the permitted uses within its tourist zone districts.

The city’s planning and zoning commission will review the changes on August 6 at 4:30 p.m. Depending on the commission’s recommendation, the proposed changes will then be scheduled for discussion during a public hearing with the city council.

Amanda Anderson, communications manager for the city, said that the proposed amendments to the city’s code follow 18 months of community discussions and planning, including gathering input from residents and developers through focus groups.

## Building height changes

Anderson said the city’s comprehensive plan calls for limiting building heights in residential areas to two stories rather than three.

Accordingly, a draft ordinance calls for a reduction in the maximum allowable building height from 35 ft. to 27 ft. in residential areas, including the city’s Mountain Overlay District. The city approved the creation of the district in [1974 to regulate development in the city’s hillside areas](https://www.ketchumidaho.org/sites/default/files/fileattachments/ordinance/35017/996_-_amending_title_17_chapter.104_mountain_overlay_zoning_district_sec._17.104.030_b._17.104.060_f._17.104.070_0.pdf).

For downtown buildings, Anderson said that city code currently allows up to 42 feet for market-rate developments, 52 feet for projects providing 100% community housing, and 68 feet for hotels. The city’s tallest building downtown is the Limelight Hotel Ketchum, which is five stories, she said.

Over time, thinking has shifted on what heights should be permitted downtown. One code update being considered would prohibit fifth floors and above for all buildings downtown.

“Through the comprehensive plan process, the community identified that fifth floors are generally not contextually appropriate for downtown Ketchum,” Anderson said. “The goal of prohibiting fifth floors is to preserve the downtown’s established scale, character, and relationship to surrounding neighborhoods while ensuring future development remains consistent with the community’s vision.”

Another potential change would limit fourth floors to downtown buildings on lots that slope away from the street and those that will be 100% community housing. The city defines community housing as residential housing that is deed-restricted [to being a rental or for-sale home based on income and residency requirements](https://www.projectketchum.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/KetchumHousingMatters_DRAFTActionPlan_Public.pdf).

Anderson said that the visual impact of a four-story building on a sloped lot is mitigated by the lot’s topography.

“When a building steps down with the natural grade, the upper level is less prominent from surrounding streets and viewpoints, allowing the additional floor to better fit within the existing skyline and downtown context,” she said.

Allowing fourth floors for community housing developments is intended to provide developers more flexibility for what they’re allowed to build in exchange for supporting the city’s housing goals: building more deed-restricted workforce housing, Anderson said.

Another proposed change would require downtown buildings taller than two stories to have a minimum 10-ft step back for any floor above the second floor. Anderson said that type of design feature “helps create a more pedestrian-oriented streetscape, preserves downtown character, and ensures that new development better aligns with the city’s goals for context-sensitive design and appropriate building scale.”

## Community Housing Incentive changes

Lastly, Anderson said that the city will consider revised language for its Community Housing Incentive to increase the amount of housing produced through the program while providing clearer standards for developers and the community.

Anderson said the updates would include “updates to the permitted and maximum floor area ratio (FAR) bonuses for each zoning district, increased community housing requirements for lots greater than 10,000 square feet, and clarification of the review and approval process.”

“These changes are intended to make the incentive program more effective at generating deed-restricted community housing while ensuring that the benefits and expectations of the program are clear,” she said.
