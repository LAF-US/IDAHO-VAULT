Louise Shadduck was a force in [[Idaho]] Republican politics and an Idaho Gem. Though she never held elected office, she influenced generations of leaders and helped shape the direction of our state for decades. She served in senior leadership roles for Idaho governors, a U.S. senator, and a congressman, demonstrating exceptional intuition, administrative skill, and judgment. Her influence came from trust, not titles.

If Senator William Borah was known as the “Lion of Idaho,” Louise earned the title “Lioness of Idaho” for her strength, tenacity, and lasting impact on our state. The nickname was not about bluster or combativeness. Quite the opposite. Louise influenced with manners, not manipulation. She listened carefully, spoke thoughtfully, and treated everyone she met as if they mattered, because to her, they did.

I met Louise early in the final decade of her life. She had a particular interest in our youth and took them seriously long before they had resumes to offer. She always had time for young people, and she even included our youngest daughter in her annual Christmas teas. This is just one example of how intentionally she invested in the next generation.

Louise’s dedication to Idaho was fearless. She championed the state’s interests with determination and grace, always believing Idaho deserved to be confident on the national stage.

She was also a trailblazer. Louise became the first woman in the United States to serve in a governor’s cabinet when she was appointed Idaho’s Secretary of Commerce and Development. In a male-dominated political world, she broke barriers not by demanding attention, but by earning respect. Under her leadership, Idaho’s economy grew and the state gained national visibility. Wherever she traveled, Louise promoted Idaho unapologetically.

True to her roots as a farm girl who grew up at the foot of Canfield Mountain on what is now Shadduck Lane, she famously hosted visiting business leaders on horseback. Her theme as Commerce Secretary was simple and confident: “Idaho is the place to go.” She would not be surprised to see today’s growth, because she believed deeply in Idaho’s people, land, and potential.

Her list of remarkable accomplishments include founding of North Idaho Young Republicans in 1944 and speaking at the 1956 Republican National Convention. She also served as Ronald Reagan’s Idaho campaign director, was president of the National Federation of Women Journalists, lectured in Tel Aviv on dehumanization and its effects on families, and interacted with several U.S. presidents.

Louise later became executive director of the Idaho Forestry Industries Council. From there, she continued to shape Idaho Republican politics for more than 30 years from her home overlooking Lake Coeur d’Alene. For decades, if you were thinking of running for Republican office, the best place to start was a visit with Louise.

Her politics were conservative but not rigid. As Mike Bullard wrote in "The Lioness of Idaho," Louise believed in a broad tent that welcomed diverse views while holding firm to core values, especially individual responsibility. She believed individual initiative and the willingness to assume responsibility were more effective than government programs or heavy-handed regulation.

Louise valued civic responsibility over factional loyalty. She was wary of ideological rigidity and was skeptical of bloated bureaucracy and grievance politics. She believed communities mattered more than distant elites and that stewardship, integrity, and restraint were virtues, not weaknesses. We at North Idaho Republicans could not agree more.

In today’s divisive political climate, I can almost hear Louise urging Idaho Republicans to return to those principles, not outrage and absolutism, but responsibility and care for the institutions entrusted to us. We would be stronger for it. It is the Idaho way.
