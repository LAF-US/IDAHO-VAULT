The total value of properties in Blaine County continues to march upward, nearly doubling in the last five years.

That’s according to the Blaine County Assessor’s Office, which has assessed property values across the county and mailed out assessment notices for 2026.

The assessed value of all properties in Blaine County on Jan. 1, 2026—the date on which the Assessor’s Office sets preliminary values for 2026 tax assessments, based on market values in 2025—was approximately $26.2 billion, a gain of approximately 2.6% over the previous year. The assessed value for 2025—based on market values from 2024—was approximately $25.6 billion.

“After several years of rapid growth, the market began to balance out in 2025,” Blaine County Assessor Jim Williams said in a newsletter sent to property owners in late May with their 2026 assessments. “Prices were generally stable, and sales activity remained steady.”

Property values have been increasing steadily in Blaine County. Assessor’s Office data shows that in 2019 the total assessed value in the county was approximately $10.7 billion. After increasing again the following year, the valuation surged in 2021, jumping to approximately $13.9 billion, and then to about $19 billion in 2022.

All told, the county recorded 642 sales of residences, commercial properties and land in Blaine County in 2025, the Assessor’s Office reported. The median sales price of those properties—in all categories—was approximately $870,000. The median assessed value of residential properties in Blaine County was slightly over $918,000 on Jan. 1, up from approximately $875,000 the year before, the Assessor’s Office reported.

Total property values increased in all areas of the county, with Hailey and Sun Valley leading the way, Assessor’s Office data shows. The 2026 preliminary values by location are:

- For unincorporated Blaine County, the total value is approximately $8.8 billion, up from about $8.76 billion the previous year, an increase of.54%.
- Ketchum’s total value rose from about $6.95 billion to $7.14 billion, up 2.65%.
- Sun Valley values climbed from about $6.55 billion to just over $6.85 billion, up approximately 4.5%.
- Hailey values climbed from about $2.65 billion last year to about $2.76 billion this year, up approximately 4%.
- Bellevue values rose about 3.4% year-over-year, from about $550 million to just almost $569 million.
- In Carey, the total value increased from just over $97 million to almost $100 million, rising approximately 2.5%.

The figures do not represent how individual property values might have changed, Williams said in an interview this week. Some properties in an area might have larger or smaller increases, depending on various factors, such as market activity, location, property type, property condition and age.

The Assessor’s Office is required by law to mail out preliminary tax assessments by the first Monday in June. The notices are largely intended to give property owners an opportunity to review the preliminary assessment figures and consider whether they want to appeal for an adjustment. The assessment notices are not tax bills, which are sent out by the county treasurer in late November.

Property owners can call the Assessor’s Office if they have questions about their preliminary assessments, Williams said. If a property owner thinks the 2026 preliminary value does not accurately reflect 2025 market conditions, county appraisers can assist them in an appeal for a change, he said. The appeal deadline is Monday, June 22.

“It’s every property owner’s right to appeal their property values,” Williams said, noting that the number of appeals filed during the June window is generally quite low.

After all adjustments are made, the values will be certified and finalized by the Idaho State Tax Commission in August, Williams said. While the assessed values will ultimately be factored into property-tax bills later in the year, the amount of those bills is currently far from being determined, he noted.

### How property taxes work

Property taxes are levied to support a variety of entities and services. The final tax charged to property owners is the sum of amounts owed to taxing districts that apply to their property, such as counties, cities, school districts, recreation districts and ambulance districts. Which districts property owners fund depends on property location.

When those districts complete their annual budgets, the county determines a levy rate for each district to be applied to a property’s taxable value. Because some districts have funding sources apart from property taxes, the amount taxpayers contribute might be for a portion of a district’s budget, not all of it.

Though higher property values can equate to higher property taxes, it is not an automatic assumption, Blaine County Treasurer John David Davidson said. Several factors come into play that can raise or lower one’s tax bill, he said.

“The change in value doesn’t mean that you are going to be paying more in taxes,” Davidson said. “And it doesn’t mean you’ll be paying less in taxes.”

While there’s not a legal limit to how much a property’s tax bill can increase in Idaho, the myriad taxing districts can only raise the property-tax portion of their budgets by 3% each year. However, more taxes can be taken if voters approve them, such as through bonds or overrides.

Often, when property values increase, the applied tax rate decreases—though the amount owed can still increase. According to Davidson, factors that can affect a property owner’s tax bill include:

- How values in various sectors of the property market—such as commercial or raw land—rise and fall. A surge in commercial values, for example, could bring more money from that sector and reduce the obligation of another sector, such as residential.
- The Idaho homeowner’s exemption—which provides a value deduction to people who own and occupy a property as their primary residence—and other state contributions.
- Whether taxing district initiatives—such as bonds or temporary levies—expire, or new ones are applied, such as the Blaine County Ambulance District override approved by voters in May, which will factor into the 2026 bills issued later in the year.

“\[As of now\], it’s hard to know what each individual home is going to do,” Davidson said, later adding, “We’ll know more in the fall.”

Blaine County tax bills will be issued in late November. The bills are due by Dec. 20.

If taxpayers choose to take advantage of a six-month leniency period, they can pay half of their bill in December and the second half by June 20 of the next year, unless that date falls on a weekend. Blaine County property owners who chose that option must pay the second half of their 2025 taxes this year by June 22, Davidson said, or face paying interest charges for the entire six-month period after Jan. 1. ￼
