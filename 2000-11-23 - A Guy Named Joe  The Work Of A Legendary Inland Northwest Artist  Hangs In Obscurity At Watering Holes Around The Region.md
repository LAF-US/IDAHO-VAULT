Patrick Haight Special To The South Voice

He was a high-speed itinerant mural painter who long ago went through the Inland Northwest like a human Lake Missoula, leaving evidence of his passage on nearly every vertical surface that would stand still long enough for a brush stroke.

He cut a swath from the West Plains to the Idaho Panhandle. It once seemed as though every tavern and restaurant in the Spokane Valley had a Breckenridge wall.

OK, I might be exaggerating a little. But even after 30 years, I can’t smell stale beer or eat a pickled egg without envisioning a Breckenridge.

Joseph R. Breckenridge, was born in 1894, just as barbed wire and abundant railheads were bringing the cowboy era to a close. He served with the Marine Corps in World War I and traveled throughout Canada, Mexico and the U.S. as a rodeo performer.

At the age of 34, he settled in Kootenai County to spend his remaining years painting Western landscapes.

My search for a Breckenridge started when a friend told me he’d seen a neat old mural on the wall of the Sage Tavern on East Sprague Avenue. “I think it was by that old Breckenridge guy that painted pictures out at Wild Bill’s years ago,” my friend stated with the assurance of a man overly familiar with Budweiser signs.

Convulsed with nostalgia and hankering to recapture my youth, I headed immediately to the Sage for a slug of fine art.

Well, the stale beer smell was right, but even as the owner assured me that the Sage’s murals were indeed Breckenridges, I couldn’t be certain. The two squiggles in the sky over the lake might be Breckenridge birds, but his “hell for leather” brush strokes and distinctive signature seemed to be missing.

While I pondered the mural’s probable authenticity, I began to wonder how many old Breckenridges might still be left out there.

My friend had one thing right: For a period during the late 1960s, Joe was the artist in residence at Wild Bill’s Frontier Saloon on the old highway between State Line, Idaho, and Post Falls.

Wild Bill’s was a fire marshal’s nightmare of sawdust floors and barn-wood walls covered with cowboy tools and old shootin’ irons. It thrived in the years when Washington bars still closed at midnight on Saturdays.

In a far back corner, surrounded by rectangular pine boards primed for painting, sat a skinny old cowpoke with paintbrush in hand and hat thrown back from a face the color and texture of an old saddle drowned in the Mississippi.

It was not the face of an effete salon painter, but a mug perfectly comfortable in tough hangouts. It took more than one set of knuckles to send that nose zigzagging chinward like an alpine road race.

I’m not sure how he came to painting in saloons or how his landscapes compared with others, but what “Cowboy Joe” lacked in style, he made up for in speed. Using four-inch-wide brushes and paint from Sears Roebuck, he could finish a pastoral landscape in the time it took to down a schooner of beer. If you liked, he would use the same tools to duplicate the scene on a matchbook cover.

He once boasted of setting a record on the old television program “You Asked For It” by finishing a painting in 42 seconds.

His muted landscapes sold for $1 - or $2 if framed in scorched wood - and they displayed a predictable format. A patron might choose between a placid lake reflecting distant mountains, or a rickety bridge spanning a quiet stream. A broken wagon wheel or dilapidated fence rendered an Old West feel to the scene. For animation, he often added a horse or an elk. His horses resembled hammer-headed mules, and his elk were mules with antlers.

A Breckenridge sky was greenish blue; lakes were bluish green. His trees were slender evergreens done in two brush strokes; one gray, one green, and clumps of buckbrush were toned whatever green was currently on sale.

Distraction seemed the chief value of his murals. Whether sawing through a gristly chicken fried steak or wincing down a Lucky Lager, the sight of a Breckenridge on the wall comforted like Grandma’s lullaby.

His paintings would never hang in the Tate, but I knew people who proudly claimed to have five or six Breckenridges hanging on their walls.

With such a prolific output, why was I having such a difficult time finding a surviving Breckenridge?

The one I thought occupied a wall of the Bronco tavern in Dishman was long ago painted over, and the one at Falk’s Lodge up Mount Spokane turned out to be another artist altogether.

A visit to Joe’s hometown sounded like a good bet.

While searching old newspapers in the Post Falls Library, I came across a group photo of a Greenacres Irrigation District meeting in Phil’s Cafe. Could that be a Breckenridge mural in the background?

I guess I’ll never know. The fire that destroyed Phil’s in the late 1970s must have been a colorful blaze indeed.

Likewise, the dingy old Breckenridges that once darkened the walls of Don’s Pool Hall on Spokane Street were destroyed when the joint was demolished in a fit of civic beautification.

While Breckenridges might have become scarce in Post Falls, stories of his legendary tippling were not. Lifelong Post Falls resident Geraldine Engan, a retired waitress who once purchased a Breckenridge for her son Darrell, recalls occasionally finding the old fellow asleep on the family’s front lawn. Joe was often known to paint while holding a brush in one hand and a beer in the other.

A faint memory brought me back to the Valley and a vacant restaurant on East Trent Avenue that was once Joe’s Pizza and most recently was known as the Lame Duck. During my high school years, Joe’s served the first pizzas in Spokane.

Even before my eyes adjusted to the darkened interior, I glimpsed an elk-mule beside a rickety bridge. It would require confirmation, but it looked as if at least one Breckenridge had survived the decades.

Joe Rizzuto, the retired former owner, confirmed the truth:

Breckenridge spent a day in 1958 painting the scene for $200 and a case of beer. Rizzuto’s sister, Mary Hill, remembers it as more like two hours and $50, but then old Joe always had a way of polarizing opinions.

“A real character,” Rizzuto chuckled. On that, his sister agreed.

Funny thing about breaking a dry spell - about a week after the Joe’s Pizza find, my daughter called from Medical Lake, where she had tracked down a Breckenridge mural in the Wagon Wheel tavern. Sure enough, on the wall beside the big-screen TV, hammer-headed horses and broken wagon wheels roamed the scenery.

On the way home, a hunch took me to the Saddle Inn at Four Lakes. Who would have guessed Joe would travel this far for a beer?

The Breckenridge winter scene stretched from the back wall all the way to the jukebox.

“Have you seen the Breckenridges at Miller’s in Cheney?” the bartender asked.

I couldn’t find Miller’s, but as I stood in Bill’s Tavern admiring the three Breckenridges near the front window, an older fellow detached himself from the bar where he was awaiting a chicken dinner.

“I used to know old Joe,” offered Walt Merwin as he began telling me of a time Breckenridge was working on a threshing gang in Rudyard, Mont.

“It was back when Joe had a lot of waterfalls in his paintings, and this rich gal came up in the bar and offered him a sum to paint a wall in her parlor. But she didn’t want him puttin’ any waterfalls in it.

“Well, Joe worked all day on the job, and when he went to get paid, she said she didn’t care for the way he did the painting and offered him half wages, take it or leave it. Everybody thought Joe was crazy for givin’ in, but he just shrugged it off.

“That night, when the old gal was gone, Joe sneaked in a window and put waterfalls all over that painting! Got a big kick out of it.”

As we were laughing at the thought, Walt remembered another fact.

“You know all those paintings in the dining room at the old Longhorn Barbecue? Joe did those, too.”

With few family members remaining, there is little to define Breckenridge’s life from 1928 to 1960, aside from the occasional old news article (he was a shameless self-promoter who treated the truth as a malleable virtue), or the random anecdote.

By his own account, he was born in Miles City, Mont., or Ione, Mich. He was either “a natural artist with nary a lesson in brush stroking” or a graduate of the Art Institute of Chicago.

He claimed to have appeared on “You Asked For It” - twice! - and to have been featured in a Life magazine article. Who knows?

Joe Breckenridge, a colorful local character who said he once sold paintings to Jack Dempsey and Franklin D. Roosevelt and loved telling the story of how he awoke one morning, broke and thirsty, to complete a painting in 40 seconds using his brother’s sock for a paintbrush, died in Coeur d’Alene in 1972.
