# Language of flowers

The **language of flowers**, or **floriography**, is a means of cryptological
communication through the use or arrangement of flowers. Particular flowers —
and the way they were presented — carried agreed meanings, so that a bouquet,
a single bloom, or a small bound posy could be "read" as a message. The
practice reached its height in the 19th century, especially Victorian England
and the United States, where it offered a coded vocabulary for sentiments that
social convention made awkward to speak aloud.

This note is a vault reference summary, grounded in the Wikipedia article cited
in the frontmatter and the additional sources listed below. It is a synthesis,
not a verbatim copy.

## Origins

Assigning symbolic meaning to plants and flowers is ancient and cross-cultural,
appearing in classical mythology, religious iconography, and folk custom long
before any codified "dictionary." The systematized Western practice, however,
crystallized in the early 19th century out of an Ottoman influence transmitted
through European travelers.

- **Ottoman *sélam* (or *selam*).** In Ottoman Turkish culture there was a
  custom — sometimes associated with the harem — of conveying messages through
  objects, including flowers, in which the *sound* of the object's name rhymed
  with the words of the intended message. Meaning attached to the rhyme and the
  object rather than to a fixed flower-symbol lexicon, but it supplied the model
  of "objects as encoded speech."
- **Lady Mary Wortley Montagu.** As the wife of the British ambassador to the
  Ottoman Empire, Montagu described this Turkish practice in letters written
  from Constantinople around 1717, later published as the *Turkish Embassy
  Letters*. Her account is widely credited with introducing the idea of a
  flower "language" to English readers.
- **Aubry de La Mottraye** and other travel writers likewise carried
  descriptions of the Ottoman custom into European print, feeding the
  fascination that the French and English would later formalize.

## Codification in France

Interest blossomed into published reference works in early-19th-century France:

- **Charlotte de Latour**, *Le Langage des Fleurs* (1819), is the touchstone
  text — a flower dictionary that paired blooms with definitions and helped fix
  the practice as a recognizable system. It was widely imitated, translated,
  and adapted.
- A flood of subsequent flower dictionaries followed in French, English, and
  American editions through the 1800s. Because each compiler made choices, the
  assigned meanings were **never perfectly consistent** from book to book — a
  given flower could mean different things in different dictionaries.

## The Victorian peak

The Victorians (roughly 1837–1901) elaborated the language of flowers more
fully than anyone before them. In a culture where overt declarations of emotion
could be considered improper, a carefully chosen flower or arrangement let
people send love, reproach, refusal, or remembrance under cover of a gift.

- **Tussie-mussies / nosegays.** Small bound bouquets ("tussie-mussies") became
  a vehicle for encoded sentiment, sometimes carried as accessories.
- **Grammar of presentation.** Meaning could be modified by *how* a flower was
  given, not just *which* flower:
  - Presenting a flower **upside down** could invert or negate its meaning.
  - Handing with the **right vs. left hand**, or the recipient's response, could
    signal affirmation or denial.
  - Color variants shifted meaning (see the rose, below).
- **Kate Greenaway**, *The Language of Flowers* (1884), the illustrated English
  writer's edition, helped standardize and popularize many meanings still
  repeated today; it has been reprinted continually since.

## Example meanings

Because dictionaries disagreed, these are representative rather than canonical:

| Flower | Common assigned meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Red rose | Love |
| Yellow rose | Jealousy, infidelity (sometimes friendship) |
| White lily | Purity; white lilies also linked to sympathy and funerals |
| Orange lily | Passion, confidence |
| Purple violet | Thoughts "occupied with love" |
| White violet | Innocence |
| Rosemary | Remembrance |
| Forget-me-not | True love, remembrance |
| Pansy | Thoughts |

## In literature

Flower symbolism predates and outlives the formal dictionaries, and writers
have long exploited it:

- **Shakespeare** — Ophelia's mad scene in *Hamlet* distributes flowers by
  meaning ("rosemary, that's for remembrance … and there is pansies, that's for
  thoughts"), a passage often read as floriography avant la lettre.
- The motif recurs across 19th-century fiction and poetry, where a flower's
  "meaning" lets an author signal a character's intent or fate obliquely. The
  practice has also seen renewed popular and literary interest in modern times.

## Why this is in the vault

Floriography is a system in which **the object is the message** and meaning
depends on selection, arrangement, and gesture rather than on plain statement —
a coded register layered beneath an ordinary surface. That makes it a natural
companion to the vault's symbolic and interpretive concerns (see the SOUL layer
and notes such as [[The Garden of Proserpine]]). It is filed here as cultural
and historical reference, on the record per [[VAULT-CONVENTIONS]].
