A punishing mix of drought, frost and surging pest and disease pressure has battered grain fields across southern and eastern [[Idaho]] this growing season, forcing some farmers to mow down fall wheat and barley rather than take them to harvest, a University of Idaho researcher said.

The 2026 growing season has brought devastating losses for many grain farmers across the region, but overall damage could have been much worse, according to Juliet Marshall, a University of Idaho College of Agricultural and Life Sciences professor, associate director of the Idaho Agricultural Experiment Station and an Extension cereals specialist. Other cereal fields have recovered better than expected.

“In some cases, it’s been as bad as you can imagine, with farmers having to mow, while in other cases some of the worst outcomes have been avoided depending on when and where the crop was planted,” Marshall said. “Overall, the crops recovered quite a bit more than I ever expected.”

Early-planted fall grains suffered the greatest damage, Marshall said. Winter temperatures never stayed cold long enough for crops to enter full dormancy, allowing plants to keep growing slowly and draw down soil moisture from November through April. With little moisture and limited snow cover, crops were stressed, stunting plant shoots known as tillers and pushing seedheads to develop several weeks early.

Fields that matured too soon were especially vulnerable when freezing temperatures arrived in April, causing widespread frost damage, Marshall said.

In southeast Idaho’s Rockland Valley, a dryland production area, frost damage stunted plant growth and triggered new tillers to emerge and produce secondary wheat heads. As of July 10, some primary heads were already overly dry, while grain in secondary heads still needed more time to mature, delaying harvest.

Frozen tillers were also more likely to die, leaving a layer of dead leaves and stems on the soil surface that can harbor disease.

Losses have been especially acute in the Magic Valley, where much of the fall grain was planted early. In eastern Idaho, the region’s short growing season likely helped many grain farmers avoid more serious losses because planting is often delayed by later harvests of sugar beets or potatoes. Late-planted fall grain had less time to grow over the winter and was less susceptible to April frost damage because it had not yet passed the boot growth stage, when the developing seedhead swells inside the sheath of the last leaf to emerge, known as the flag leaf.

Beyond frost damage, the warm, dry winter allowed disease-carrying pests such as aphids and mites to survive in large numbers. Barley yellow dwarf virus, spread by aphids, and wheat streak mosaic virus, transmitted by mites, have been rampant across southern and eastern Idaho and have reduced yields. Pest populations were also high during the spring planting season, contributing to unusually high rates of the two viruses in spring grains.

Marshall said she remains optimistic about the spring grain outlook compared with what she predicted in early spring.

“The spring crop looks really good,” Marshall said. “There was some frost damage but not nearly enough to cause yield loss.”
