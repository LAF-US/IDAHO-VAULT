# ISP facing recruitment shortage in panhandle

## State police are afraid problem will continue to worsen in the future

[

![Royce McCandless avatar](https://public-assets-prod.pubgen.ai/brand_b31a1432-2c12-4a67-8456-0baf383b7aac/asset_39c4a4e0-4b29-41c0-b52f-b522a08bdb39.jpg?w=3840)

Royce McCandless

](https://www.dnews.com/writers/royce-mccandless)

![Mooney](https://public-assets-prod.pubgen.ai/brand_4fc3eb37-4c8a-4951-9324-365f6e4b53fd/asset_69adcc01-602d-579f-af25-112113117dc2.jpg?w=3840)

Mooney

BOISE — The Idaho State Police announced the agency is facing an ongoing shortage of troopers in north Idaho that could worsen due to a limited pool of applicants.

A release issued this past weekend detailed just a month remained before applications close for the Idaho State Police trooper academy in January. At present, only three in the Lewiston region, and seven applicants in the Coeur d’Alene region, are meeting current minimum requirements, numbers that are expected to decline further as the hiring process advances.

Now the agency is issuing a call to potential applicants who will have until Sept. 18 to apply for the academy hosted in Meridian.

ISP Capt. Mike Mooney, who will have served for 25 years this October, described the decline in troopers as being a “slow burn” rather than a “precipice.” Though some external economic and job market factors may have been at play, Mooney said the consistent through line has been compensation at the agency falling behind and seasoned officers feeling they had a “brighter future” with other agencies.

“Then you combine that with the difficulties of recruiting … we end up with this perfect storm of just having a lot of vacancies,” Mooney said.

Another factor in recruiting difficulty stems from the rural character of the state, with several regions drawing from smaller pools compared to what would be seen in areas with a major metropolitan center. Wanting to be able to attract candidates from across the nation, Mooney said part of the task is communicating what the state has to offer.

“Our troopers are just like everybody else,” Mooney said. “They live in these communities, they hunt, fish, hike — we enjoy everything that Idaho has to offer, and that’s the beauty of of living here. We just need to get people to understand that, even if you’re not from this area, moving here would be a fantastic move for you and your family.”

As for the agency’s needs, Mooney said in just his Central Idaho District — covering Idaho, Latah, Lewis and Nez Perce counties — there are about a dozen openings, a shortage that has been particularly pronounced in Moscow. At times, the district has had as many as four troopers assigned to the city to allow one officer to be on every shift across days, nights, weekdays and weekends. At the lowest point, officers assigned to Moscow had fallen to zero.

When these circumstances arise in Moscow and other areas of the state, ISP brings in troopers from outside the district and houses them in a hotel in what is termed a “temporary duty assignment.” Mooney said at least one trooper per weekend has been assigned to help cover shifts in the Lewiston region, reducing the number of troopers in other communities.

It also puts incoming officers in difficult circumstances as they are tasked with quickly adjusting to different communities with different roads, prosecuting attorneys, mileposts and law enforcement partners, in addition to being separated from families for six days at a time, between four 10-hour shifts and travel days, Mooney said, difficulties that don’t arise when positions are filled and troopers can be on long-term assignment.

He noted there was “light at the end of the tunnel for the area” with two new recruits out of ISP’s academy now being in training and on track to fill the gap. With shortages still remaining elsewhere, however, the practice of temporary assignment will continue.

The difficulties stemming from recruitment shortages can be further evidenced by Riggins. Though one trooper is assigned to and lives in the community, most calls came from Nez Perce County, which has the largest population centers for the district.

“That trooper in Riggins, when they come on duty, frequently has to commute two hours north to cover calls for the shift because we only have one or two troopers on at any given time,” Mooney said.

This circumstance also bore out in Fleming with a trooper assigned to U.S. Highway 12. In June, the trooper was tasked with assisting in traffic control due to a fire in Lewiston at the same time three motorcyclists were killed in a crash on the highway.

“Unfortunately, our low staffing pulls people from the areas where we would like to have them,” Mooney said. “… That’s one of the biggest reasons we need to build back as quickly as possible, so we can cover all of our communities and our responsibilities.”

The agency’s effort to build back will be contingent on its ability to remain competitive amongst both municipal police departments and neighboring states.

For city departments, the difference, at least when looking at starting wages alone, is smaller. The Boise Police Department, among the highest paid in the state, has a starting wage of $32.42 that is due to improve by $1 per hour over the next three years. In the case of the Moscow Police Department, entry level hourly pay starts at $30.55. These compare to a starting hourly wage of $30.07 for ISP.

The difference is more pronounced when comparing highway patrol wages for neighboring states.

The Spokesman-Review previously reported on Washington State Patrol being a significant draw for ISP troopers as a result of a more lucrative pay scale. When comparing starting wages, ISP troopers with under three years experience can expect about $62,545, assuming 40-hour workweeks and without additional incentives.

At the same time, Washington State Patrol salaries start at $70,008 for cadets before improving to $91,332 for fully commissioned troopers while in Oregon, recruit troopers start at $6,422 per month, or $77,064 annually.

Prior to the start of this year’s legislative session, of the 61 commissioned officers who have left ISP since January 2024, “18 received a pay increase from another law enforcement agency or reported pay as a primary reason for separation,” according to state budget documents.

An effort to increase the PROJECT CHOICE program fee — that charges $3 per vehicle registration for the Idaho Law Enforcement Fund — was brought forward this year to help ensure ISP salaries remained competitive, as was previously reported by KTVB. The bill, which died in committee after passing out of the Senate, would have brought an estimated $10 million per year in funding for the program.

Rep. Brandon Mitchell, R-Moscow, said he would be open to a similar measure being brought forward next session. He remarked the increase would have cost his household just $18, but said it was a matter of whether the votes are there to get such a bill through.

“I do know that one of the biggest things that we’re supposed to do as a state, that’s part of the proper role of government, is to make sure that we get our law enforcement take care of,” Mitchell said.

Budgets were tight across the board this past legislative session, but some positive steps were still made to improve ISP’s trooper pay.

State budget documents showed ISP received $6.5 million in pay improvements this session. Though down from an initial request of $12.6 million, Mooney was supportive of the funding increase that he noted arrived when most agencies concluded the session with some form of spending cut.

Much of these funds came from House Bill 967, redirecting $4 million from the state Liquor Account to the Idaho Law Enforcement Fund.

Mitchell said there was concern from cities about the transfer as some of the Liquor Account’s funds go to municipal governments. Speaking to his own district’s experience, he recalled ISP assisting Moscow during the investigation into murders at the University of Idaho.

“ISP came in and helped out dramatically and never sent a bill,” Mitchell said.

After signing the bill authorizing the transfer, Gov. Brad Little issued a letter of support for the pay increases, but underscored there was more to be done next session.

“I support this legislation as a long-overdue step to deliver the pay increases our brave Idaho State Police (ISP) officers have earned and deserve,” Little wrote. “However, backing the Blue means we must deliver a sustained commitment to our men and women in law enforcement. The Legislature must return next year prepared to fully address ISP’s needs.”
