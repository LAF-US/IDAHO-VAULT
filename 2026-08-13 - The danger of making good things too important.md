### St. Augustine’s wisdom on rightly ordered loves.

*The views expressed in this opinion column are those of the author. They do not necessarily reflect the* [*views*](https://favs.news/about-favs-news/) *of* [*FāVS News.*](https://favs.news/)

I learned something about myself this morning that’s pretty extraordinary. Apparently, according to a man I met in passing years ago, the fate of the world rests on my shoulders. This man is a political consultant, and he’s currently “rallying the troops” to doorbell homes in a congressional district his candidate doesn’t have a chance of winning.

He sent me a message rather out of the blue telling me of his plans and asking if I’d help. I told him I had left politics behind long ago. (Never mind that I live 400 miles away and in a different state.)

His response? If I didn’t get involved, a nationwide catastrophe would ensue.

This elevating things beyond what St. Augustine called their “ordo amoris,” our rightly ordered loves — putting things in their proper place, God before all else, family before work, and so on — is something we all do all the time.

In fact, politics used to be a disordered love of mine and even when I was in the thick of it, managing candidates’ campaigns, running myself, serving as a delegate to state and national conventions or simply reading too much political news, I knew on some level it was toxic.

## Hold on to what’s ‘rightly ordered’

That’s not to say all political involvement is inherently bad. Elections do have consequences and my deep involvement in politics for 16 years brought about some good.

I still have many friends from that time. If it hadn’t been for my political involvement, my life would probably look much different, and I likely would not be married to the wonderful woman I am married to today.

That’s what “ordo amoris” is all about. Since God is the author of all creation, everything he created is good. It is only when it is used in an inappropriate way or elevated beyond its proper place that it becomes evil.

Changing water into wine was Christ’s first miracle, but too much wine leads to drunkenness. Money can be good when we master it and direct it toward good works, but the love of money, allowing it to master us, is evil.

Taking pride in the gifts that God has bestowed on us and sharing those gifts with the world to make it better is good. Believing that those traits originate with us is the beginning of sinful pride.

The man’s comment about political involvement reflects a temptation shared by many of us to believe we are indispensable. Even things far more benign than political involvement can be a form of pride.

“If I don’t organize this event it will fail. If I don’t manage my child’s life, he will fail. If I don’t say something or do something, something will fail.”

## Beware of apathy — the other extreme

All these things might be good things. We also shouldn’t become apathetic and do nothing, for God has invited us to be part of his plan and our showing up when called to certain things may truly be the difference between life and death in some cases.

As the saying goes, “All that’s required for evil to prevail is for good men to do nothing.”

But we need to remember that it doesn’t all rest on our shoulders. God is always in control. Had any number of prophets and saints from Noah to St. Carlo Acutis rejected his call, he still would have accomplished his plans.

So, as a friend of mine says, “You have permission to resign as master of the universe.” It doesn’t all rest on your shoulders, and what you think is important for the future of humanity will likely be nothing more than a footnote, if that, sooner than we think.

As St. Mother Teresa of Calcutta said, “Not all of us can do great things. But we can do small things with love.”

And ultimately, it’s many of those small things, added together, that end up changing the world.
