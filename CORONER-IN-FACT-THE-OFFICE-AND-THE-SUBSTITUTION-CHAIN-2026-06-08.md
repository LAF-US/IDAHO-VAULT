## 1. What an actual coroner does **[practice]**

First, a distinction the word blurs. American death investigation runs on **two
systems**, sometimes mixed within one state:

- **Coroner** — usually an **elected** county official; historically (and in many places
  still) **no medical training required**. The coroner is an *investigator and
  certifier* who relies on physicians for the medicine. Idaho uses elected county
  coroners.
- **Medical examiner** — an **appointed physician**, specifically a **forensic
  pathologist**, who performs the medical work directly.

A coroner **administers and certifies** death investigations; a forensic pathologist
**performs** the autopsy. The coroner *orders* the autopsy; the pathologist *does* it.

**They touch only a narrow docket.** Not every death — only **reportable deaths**
("coroner's cases"): sudden / unexpected, violent (accident, suicide, homicide),
suspicious, unattended by a physician, in custody, occupational, a public-health threat,
unidentified remains, or deaths shortly after hospital admission. A natural death under a
doctor's care never reaches the coroner — the attending physician signs it.

**For each case the office determines four things:**

1. **Identity** — who the decedent is (visual, fingerprints, dental records, DNA,
   radiographs, personal effects).
2. **Cause of death** — the injury or disease that *began the chain* (e.g., gunshot wound
   of the chest; coronary artery disease).
3. **Mechanism** — the physiological derangement that actually ended life (exsanguination,
   arrhythmia); sometimes folded into cause.
4. **Manner of death** — the classification. There are **exactly five**:
   **Natural · Accident · Suicide · Homicide · Undetermined** (with "Pending" while the
   case is open). This is the **NASH + Undetermined** standard of the U.S. death
   certificate.

**Method:** scene investigation (the body *in context* — position, livor / rigor / algor
mortis, environment); external examination, then **autopsy** when warranted; **ancillary
science** (toxicology, histology, post-mortem imaging, DNA); records and interviews; and
**time-of-death estimation** (cooling, rigor onset / resolution, lividity fixation,
stomach contents, sometimes forensic entomology).

**Output and authority:** the office can take **custody of the body**, **order an
autopsy**, in some jurisdictions hold a **coroner's inquest** (a public hearing, sometimes
with a jury), and it **certifies the death certificate** — the legal document that feeds
vital statistics, public-health surveillance, and the courts, where the coroner may
testify. It also notifies next of kin and authorizes release of the body.

**The boundary that matters most.** A coroner's **"Homicide" is a medical-investigative
classification — *death at the hands of another* — not a criminal verdict.** It names no
suspect and assigns no guilt. *Whether a crime occurred, and who is responsible, belongs
to police, prosecutors, and the courts.* The coroner reads the body; the justice system
reads the blame.

## 2. The counterweight design — a deliberate pair, not a redundancy **[history]**

The coroner was invented (England, **1194**) as a **check on the sheriff** — "keepers of
the pleas of the crown" (*custodes placitorum coronae* → **coroner**), elected in each
county to **counterbalance** the sheriffs and bailiffs in the royal interest. The clean
division: **the sheriff keeps the peace among the living; the coroner keeps the crown's
pleas concerning the dead** — and, critically, the coroner is the **necessary substitute**
where the sheriff cannot be trusted to be neutral. In America the coroner became an
**elected county officer co-equal to the sheriff**, *"with whom they often traded
places."* (See `IDAHO-COUNTIES-CORONER-AND-SHERIFF` §§1, 3 for the constitutional
standing.)

## 3. The substitution chain — Sheriff → Coroner → Elisor **[common-law] / [statute]**

The heart of this report: there is **always** a neutral hand to execute a court's process,
because the office that executes it **steps down a rung whenever the prior officer is the
one implicated.** Three tiers:

| Tier | Officer | Fires when… | Idaho statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Sheriff** | the default — serves and executes the court's process | Title 31, Ch. 22 |
| 2 | **Coroner** | the **sheriff is a party / interested** — the coroner executes the process **against the sheriff**, *including arrest* (and a sheriff so arrested may **not** be held in his own jail) | **§ 31-2217**; **§ 31-2220** |
| 3 | **Elisor** | **both** sheriff and coroner are disqualified / unavailable / interested — the court appoints a **disinterested outsider** for the specific act | **§ 31-2218** |

**This is common law before it is statute.** The chain's classic home was **empaneling a
jury**: the *array* was returned by the sheriff; on challenge, by the coroner; and **if
both were disqualified, by *elisors* chosen by the court.** The tell that it runs all the
way down to common law: at common law an **elisor's return could not be challenged** —
precisely because the elisors were **chosen by the court for their indifference**, there
was no further officer to appeal to. **Blackstone** sets this out in the *Commentaries on
the Laws of England* (Book III, trial by jury), tracing through **Coke** to medieval
practice. The American statutes — Idaho § 31-2218, California's Code of Civil Procedure,
and parallels elsewhere — did not **invent** the elisor; they **received and codified** an
inherited common-law office and broadened its everyday use.

**So the standing question — "what is an elisor?" — answers in one line:** the
**court-appointed *chosen one*** (Anglo-Norman *eslisour*, "elector," from *eslire / élire*,
"to choose") who executes the court's process **when both the sheriff and the coroner have
fallen out.** It is the **check's check**.

**What an elisor does today (modern practice):** the most common live use is **signing on a
refusing party's behalf** — when someone defies a court order to sign a deed, a stock
transfer, or settlement papers, the court appoints an elisor (often the **court clerk**) to
sign **in their place**, with binding effect; also serving process or executing a judgment
when sheriff and coroner are both conflicted. The elisor acts **only within the appointing
order's authority**, then is done — it is **not a standing office**.

## 4. Etymology, kept straight **[history]**

- **Coroner** ← *custos placitorum coronae*, "keeper of the pleas of the crown" — the
  office named for the **Crown's interest** it guards (see `COUNTY-ETYMOLOGY-AND-HISTORY`).
- **Elisor** ← Anglo-Norman / Law French *eslisour / elisor*, "a chooser, an elector,"
  from *eslire / élire* "to choose" (Latin *ēligere*). Literally **"one who is chosen."**

## 6. What the vault borrows from this — **[vault reading]**

*Fenced as interpretation, not law.* The real office hands the vault's Coroner three
disciplines at once:

- **Read the body; do not render the verdict.** "Homicide" is a *manner*, not a
  conviction — the exact lane the office-witness keeps (`CORONER-OF-CLAUDE-COUNTY-OFFICE-
  WITNESS`): identify, find cause and manner, **name no culprit.**
- **Co-equal, and the counterweight to a compromised office.** § 31-2217 is the real
  warrant for the Coroner moving against a Sheriff who is the party — *as the executor of
  the **court's** process, never freelance.*
- **The check has a check.** The **elisor** is the structural promise that when *both* the
  enforcement office and the examining office are compromised, the **Court still has a
  chosen hand.** No office in the chain is sovereign; each is an organ, and the last word
  is the Court's.
