### What are fire regimes, and how can differences in fire regimes impact how fires burn and affect ecosystems over time?

---

###### \[00:03:32\]

**CHRISTIAN MONTERROSA:** Awesome. Thank you all. Before we begin taking audience questions, I’ll ask each of our panelists a few questions myself. Journalists, you can submit your questions at any time during the briefing. Just click the Q&A icon at the bottom of your Zoom screen and please let us know if you’d like your question directed to a specific panelist. We’ll be posting a recording of this briefing on our website later today and a transcript will be added in the next couple of days. With that, let’s begin. I’ll start with you, Andi. What does the term fire regime mean? And how can differences in fire regimes impact how fires burn and affect ecosystems over time?

###### \[00:04:10\]

**DR. ANDI THODE:** Great. Yeah. So a fire regime is a term that we use basically to describe the general way that fire works in an ecosystem, and those ecosystems vary from mountain, forested ecosystems of different types down to the deserts. So we really think of this as seven sort of different terms that make up a fire regime. That have to do with how frequently the fire burns in an ecosystem. Is it every year? Is it every 50 years? Is it every 100 years plus? What time of year? So the seasonality of fire, the size of the fires and the pattern of those fires, how they’re burning on the landscape, severity wise, which I’ll talk about in a minute.

And then also the intensity of the fire, which we describe as the heat released by the fire. And when you see really long flame links versus very short flame links, that’s directly relating to that intensity of the fire, how much heat is coming off the fire. And then severity is a broad term that we look at for fire regimes, which is talking about the effects of the fire on vegetation and wildlife, are probably the two most direct ways that those are used, soils as well. And severity on soils versus wildlife can be different and vegetation can be different. And then the last is back to that pattern of fire, which is the pattern of that severity. So do you have really big, high severity patches, typically, or is it a very patterned fire where it’s a mix of different kinds of fire on the landscape?

So those fire regimes are really important for how they used to work and then how they’re currently working because with past management, from logging to grazing to fire suppression, and then changes in our climate that are leading to the large droughts, like we’re seeing affecting the Western U.S. much this year, those can really change the way that fire regimes are working and how frequently or how severely fire comes through a system. I also kind of want to bring up relative to that, that not all wildfires are bad. We really have a range of what the word bad means. If you talk about bad or good ecologically with wildfire.

That really depends on that fire regime and how fire is supposed to work in that system. So if you think of, like, a Ponderosa pine, lower elevation forested system, fire is actually really, really critical for that system to function and have the proper kind of fire, which originally was short flame lengths, low intensities, low severities and very frequent every ten to twentyish years kind of thing. You could compare that to ecologically a fire system like Southern California chaparral, which we hear a lot about in the more fall time frames, those systems are supposed to burn at high severity with long flame lengths, but they’re not supposed to burn as frequently as they are because of invasive species and human ignitions.

So that’s why understanding these fire regimes is really critical to understanding really how good or bad ecologically the effects are. Now if we’re talking about social effects. Even though Southern California chaparral fire is ecologically supposed to burn at high severities and intensities, socially, that’s not great when you have homes right in the middle of that. So those are kind of different ways that we have to think about how fire works in these systems. And when talking about wildfires being good or bad, there’s multiple perspectives to that.

### What conditions make a wildfire difficult to control?

---

###### \[00:08:02\]

**CHRISTIAN MONTERROSA:** Great. Thank you. I’d love to take a step back even before a fire, what conditions have to come together for a wildfire to become particularly difficult to control?

###### \[00:08:13\]

**DR. ANDI THODE:** So, the world of fire loves triangles, and we have a couple of different triangles. The one that relates directly to fire behavior or how quickly fires moving, how intense it is. Excuse me, we call the fire behavior triangle, very hard concept to get. And so that includes fuel, weather, and topography. Weather is the most unpredictable piece of that triangle. And so weather really is a big driver of when we get these large fire events that builds on other things going on, but big wind events, lack of precipitation, really dry weather and low relative humidities. So a lot less moisture in the system in general. Things that add to this fire behavior triangle, though, is the longer term climate.

So drought, if we’re coming in on a high wind event with low relative humidities on top of a long term drought or even just a couple of years of drought, that allows our larger fuels to become more available, and that allows our ignitions or something that people call PIG, our probability of ignitions to go up so that basically, whenever an ember lands, there’s more of a chance that it actually will ignite the fuels around it.

So all of those pieces coming together will cause it to be particularly difficult to control. The other piece that I want to bring into this, though, is the wildland urban interface or basically where homes and communities meet our wildlands and areas that burn. And if those wildland urban interfaces are not prepared themselves and don’t have the right kinds of roads that fire trucks can turn around in and all of these other social infrastructure things, that also can make it very difficult to control wildfires because our firefighters are then focused on trying to save houses instead of trying to put out a fire.

And I’d like to also just put quotes sort of around the word control because we really, in many cases, don’t control wildfire. We herd it around. We do things to try and take the heat out of it, to remove oxygen and remove fuel from the system. But I think this idea of control, you know, this is a natural event, and we don’t really control completely nature. And so I think to think that to put the onus on wild and firefighting community as a whole to control these events is not exactly acceptable, especially when we haven’t done our part socially on communities and our homes.

---

**DR. HEIDI HUBER-STEARNS:** Yes, there’s a lot of different elements to this. I’ll give a bit of a high level couple points about it and then have some links for folks to follow up as well. Broadly, incident command is the standardized system that manages all wildfire responses across all agencies in the United States. The incident command system for wildfire is managed by the National Wildfire Coordinating Group and NWCG Incident Command System framework. In practice, what happens is that when a local entity reports a fire, the local agency, fire department, local national forest unit, state, private company, essentially, local resources respond first. So that’s for initial attack. That’s the first initial response.

If it grows beyond that local capacity, then one of the 11 regions of different geographic areas that we have in the U.S., the GACCs, the geographic area coordination centers will respond and support with additional resources. So the resource requests go from there. And then at a certain point it goes to the National Interagency Coordination Center, the NICC, at the National Interagency Fire Center, the NIFC in Boise, Idaho.

Those are all the acronyms. But essentially, what that means is there are different points of mobilization and where those resources are coming from. A couple of things about this is these resources are not distributed evenly across the country, particularly when we think about they are positioned in different ways, from a federal resources perspective, but at the state level, local and then also private businesses that provide contracting for a lot of this work as well and provide a lot of the surge capacity across the system. Those resources, some of them are preposition, and many of them are actually really heavily based in the Pacific Northwest. Not that that’s where they end up getting deployed, but it does mean that they have to travel to get to incident. So just some perspective on a little bit around that. Who’s making the decisions. Many different levels. The decisions are being made. The system, we have the system in place.

It’s worth flagging that we are at different preparedness levels across the nation, PL levels, preparedness level, and some of that impacts where some of these resources go. From a very pragmatic standpoint, if you’re looking for information about an incident InciWeb is the official incident information location. You can search the fire name, you can look up incident updates. You can see who the incident commander is, who the management team is, and the agency administrators. That’s going to be the best place to go to understand who’s in charge of a fire. That can change over time as the fire grows in complexity or as on the back end as things calm down. And so it’s worth checking there. You can also look up the PIO, the public information officer for the fire. You can look at daily incident reports, and that’s going to tell you more about what’s going on. For larger fires, they have ICS 209s, which also give you detailed information.

So those are all good places to look that give you a bunch of that base information without having to try to contact someone who’s actively on the fire, for example, and not being able to be responsive. Another local example in case it’s helpful, at least here in Oregon, we have regular smoke and heat or smoke and fire calls where there are calls during smoky times like we’ve been having, that happen fairly regularly and those are open to a lot of different folks. That’s another great place to find out what resources exist. Like Andi said, the consortia can also be a helpful resource for finding some of this information as well.
