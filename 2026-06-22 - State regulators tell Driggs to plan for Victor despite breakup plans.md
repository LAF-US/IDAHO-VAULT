A [$35 million civil](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/teton_valley/local/legal-but-atypical-victor-mayor-will-frohlich-sued-driggs-without-council-vote-financial-backing/article_ac7c0895-ed79-4adb-b79f-33721d8dd39c.html) lawsuit, a mayor [facing recall](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/teton_valley/local/amid-wastewater-dispute-victor-idaho-mayor-must-resign-or-face-november-recall-election/article_899b5397-1eb7-43c1-9abf-10b69d62b369.html) and a looming deadline to replace a wastewater plant have prompted Idaho regulators to weigh in on the future of the two cities in Teton Valley’s shared sewer system.

On June 16, the Driggs City Council unanimously approved moving forward with a new wastewater treatment plant sized to serve both Driggs and Victor, citing [state regulatory](https://www2.deq.idaho.gov/admin/LEIA/api/document/download/24841) requirements and the lack of a binding commitment from Victor to build its own facility.

“This is more than a City of Driggs decision,” said Driggs Mayor August Christensen at the June 16 meeting. “This is a decision for a whole community.

“It is disappointing that it has gotten to this place,” Christensen added at the meeting. “But if at this point the city of Driggs \[has\] to step up to be the adults in the room and make the decisions for our whole community.”

[In 2024](https://www.tetonvalleynews.net/community/victor-stays-with-driggs-as-partner-in-wastewater-treatment-plant/article_9277d2f2-34ec-11ef-840e-a3dd9e3d4b9b.html), Victor’s leadership voted to remain connected to Driggs’ wastewater system. At the time, the Driggs facility was operating under a consent decree with the U.S. Department of Justice and the Environmental Protection Agency after [violating permit requirements](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/city-driggs-idaho-pays-400k-penalty-clean-water-act-violations-agrees-wastewater). The agreement required Driggs to replace the treatment plant by 2030. With Victor’s support, Driggs moved ahead with plans for a joint facility.

But by March 2025, Victor had retained outside legal counsel, [Smith Currie Oles](https://library.municode.com/id/victor/munidocs/munidocs?nodeId=7bda82354583f), which argued that breaking from Driggs was prudent for several reasons.

There were billing problems with the City of Driggs and it wasn’t proper to have paid into a system that wasn’t working, the firm said. Driggs leadership pushed back, saying the city had always accepted Victor’s wastewater and did not falter on its commitment to serve the southern end of the valley.

The Georgia law firm argued Driggs had overbilled the southern city and contended that Victor could have pursued its own treatment plant had it been informed earlier that Driggs’ facility was out of compliance.

Victor notified Driggs that it intended to disconnect from the shared system and pursue construction of its own wastewater treatment plant.

Victor’s decision to withdraw forced Driggs back into negotiations with federal regulators, delaying the planning process.

**Diverging paths**

As relations between the neighboring cities unraveled, each community began pursuing its own vision for wastewater treatment.

Driggs continued designing a replacement facility in order to comply with federal mandates, while Victor moved to separate from the regional system and develop an independent plant, despite community [pushback](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/search/?l=25&s=start_time&sd=desc&f=html&t=article,video,youtube,collection&app=editorial&nsa=eedition&q=Victor+waste+water).

By November 2025, Victor was pursuing a [$35 million loan](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/teton_valley/local/as-critics-lawyer-up-victor-idaho-seeks-approval-to-borrow-35-million-for-wastewater-plant/article_022b1136-2b35-4648-a487-633e716135b1.html) through the courts while seeking to [annex an 80-acre](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/teton_valley/local/victor-council-annexes-80-acres-as-wastewater-plant-strategy-shifts/article_c4bc46ec-9af1-4ed6-bfeb-26be830743be.html) site north of town for a new treatment facility.

Those plans have since encountered a series of setbacks. The [proposed land deal](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/teton_valley/local/victor-loses-key-wastewater-site-as-land-deal-collapses/article_0970e91f-dfe5-4087-84a5-ad43f8e192de.html) for the plant collapsed, neighbors sued over the annexation and rezoning, and a recall effort targeted Victor Mayor Will Frohlich over the issue.

Before that, Victor had [sought mediation](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/environmental/local/try-again-victor-pauses-plans-for-wastewater-treatment-plant-trying-to-reconcile-with-driggs/article_e1e957f8-7d06-4262-80e5-7d64a5408666.html) with Driggs in hopes of resolving the dispute. A Jan. 22 settlement offer from Driggs proposed forgiving more than $3.25 million in fees it said Victor owed if the city built its own plant and [disconnected](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/teton_valley/local/mediation-fails-victor-and-driggs-split-on-wastewater/article_4004736e-3bd7-4f86-9fbf-8bf7cf815d43.html) from the regional system by 2030.

Victor [passed on the offer](https://www.jhnewsandguide.com/news/teton_valley/local/mediation-fails-victor-and-driggs-split-on-wastewater/article_4004736e-3bd7-4f86-9fbf-8bf7cf815d43.html) and maintained their course for independence.

Before the Driggs vote at the June 16 meeting, Driggs Councilwoman Allison Michalski said councilmembers had little choice but to proceed with a plant designed for both communities, arguing that without a formal commitment from Victor outlining its plans, delaying could jeopardize Driggs’ discharge permit and put the city at odds with state regulators.
