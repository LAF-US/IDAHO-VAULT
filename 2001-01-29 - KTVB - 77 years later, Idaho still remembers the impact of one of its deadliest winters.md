BOISE, Idaho — The winter of 1948–49 remains one of the most devastating in Idaho's history, and much of the western U.S.

It was a season so severe it shut down transportation, crippled agriculture and ultimately reshaped how the state builds and prepares for extreme weather.

Seventy-seven years later, the impact of that winter still stands in sharp contrast to the largely snowless start to the 2025–26 season in Idaho’s valleys.

Weather forecasting in the late 1940s was limited in comparison to 21st century forecasting. Snow removal equipment was basic or nonexistent, and Idaho lacked the modern infrastructure it relies on today. When relentless storms began arriving in November 1948, Idahoans weren’t prepared for the months to come.

“Reports indicated that the snow was so compacted because of just the constant dumping of precipitation that they had to use explosives to clear it,” said Idaho State Historian HannaLore Hein.

At the time, Idaho depended on railroads and rural roads. There was no dedicated state transportation department, and many communities were quickly cut off.

“It was actually a devastating winter for the entire region,” Hein said. “Idaho was not alone in experiencing extremely high snowfall, frigid temperatures and high winds.”

Snow fell consistently for roughly 13 weeks across southern Idaho and the broader region.

“The worst storm, however, happened in February of 1949, and that's really where we start to see snowfall accumulation that was untenable,” Hein told KTVB.

Snow piled up in feet, winds gusted up to 40 miles per hour, and temperatures plunged to 20 to 30 degrees below zero. With little break between storms, recovery was nearly impossible.

Roads disappeared beneath snowdrifts, trains stopped runnin,g and fuel supplies dwindled. In some communities, the situation became dire.

“Moscow was three days away from being completely out of coal, and so if that city had lost all of its coal, they wouldn't have been able to heat homes,” Hein said.

The consequences were deadly in several states.

“Across the entire western region, there were upwards of 150 deaths, and that came from maybe home fires where they were trying to burn items to keep their homes heated,” Hein said.

Others froze to death.

Agriculture was also hit hard as livestock struggled to reach food buried beneath deep snow and ice.

“Hundreds of thousands of animals perished because of the lack of food that they could forage for,” Hein said.

With roads impassable, help sometimes came from planes. The National Guard dropped in supplies, not only for people, but for animals as well.

For children, the experience felt different.

“Even though it was hard, I had an overall feeling of enjoying playing in the snow just like any kid would I think,” said Lanny Ames, who was 13 years old at the time.

Ames lived on a small, family-owned dairy farm near Heyburn. When roads became impassable, Ames’ father got creative.

“He contrived a small sled that would carry four 10-gallon milk cans on it, which is pretty heavy,” that he, his father and his brother pulled for miles across snow-covered fields.

“The snow was so drifted so high, we didn't even have to go through gates. We just went over the fence lines with the sled,” he said.

While Ames remembers the work and hardship, he also remembers childhood moments of play, including building snow caves and being out of school for weeks.

Those experiences were shared by families across southern Idaho. Recently, [the City of Rupert posted](https://www.facebook.com/share/p/1Dh7usZDkR/) historic photos of the winter on Facebook, and residents flooded the comments with personal memories.

“My dad hooked an old scraper backwards to a team of horses to get to the canal bank to get a truck to take my Mom into town so I could be born. Thanks,” one commenter wrote.

Another recalled tragedy read, “We had a family member that died trying to go out and find the cattle. He never came back. Went to try to find him. Had froze out there.”

Throughout this winter, larger issues came to light. Idaho lacked the infrastructure to handle a season of that magnitude.

At the time, the Division of Public Roads operated within the Department of Public Works.

“The Department of Public Works made repairs to more than 1300 miles of roads in Idaho,” Hein said.

The damage forced government action. “Immediate emergency relief, nearly $200,000, which in today's money is about 2.7 million,” Hein said.

The widespread destruction also prompted the state to study whether Idaho needed a dedicated highway agency.

“So in 1950, this study resulted in legislation that created the Idaho highway department, which is the precursor to today's Idaho Transportation Department,” Hein said.

At the time, a delegate to Congress, John C. Sanborn, a native of southern Idaho, advocated for recovery aid for the West at the federal level. As a legislator originally from Hagerman, he was plugged in to the agriculture scene and had his own experience with this destructive winter.

Sanborn’s direct district was impacted, and he fought for a sufficient federal response to help the Gem State.

For Idaho, the legacy of this storm still impacts how the state prepares for winter today.
