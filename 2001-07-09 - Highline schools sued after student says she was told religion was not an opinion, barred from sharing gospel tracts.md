BURIEN, Wash. — A father has sued Highline Public Schools, alleging the district violated his daughter’s civil rights by restricting her religious speech at school and forcing her to remain at an assembly that conflicted with her Christian beliefs.

The federal lawsuit was filed Wednesday in U.S. District Court in Seattle by Jonathan H., on behalf of himself and his minor daughter, identified in the complaint as J.H.

The defendants are Highline Public Schools and Lori McEwen, the vice principal at Sylvester Middle School.

KING 5 News has reached out to the Highline school district for a comment regarding this lawsuit.

According to the complaint, J.H. is a Sylvester Middle School student who was distributing Christian gospel tracts to classmates during noninstructional time, such as breaks and lunch. The lawsuit says she asks students whether they want a tract and does not continue if they said no.

The complaint alleges McEwen pulled J.H. from math class on or about Feb. 18 and told her she could not distribute religious gospel tracts at school.

When J.H. asked why students could share other viewpoints, the lawsuit says McEwen told her students may share opinions but not religious beliefs.

J.H. also asked why students were allowed to leave campus during school hours for an anti-ICE protest, according to the complaint. The lawsuit says McEwen responded that the protest was allowed because it was an opinion.

Highline Public Schools later responded in writing that J.H. had been distributing religious tracts before school in the cafeteria, “which is permitted,” according to the complaint.

The district said McEwen had gone to the classroom to confirm that J.H. knew distribution during class was not allowed.

The district also acknowledged in that response that “J.H. may certainly distribute her materials during non-instructional times such as before and after school and during lunch,” the complaint says.

The lawsuit also centers on a May 29 schoolwide “Inclusion Assembly” at Sylvester Middle School.

According to the complaint, students were required to attend the assembly, and J.H.’s parents were not notified beforehand about the content or given a chance to opt her out.

The lawsuit says the assembly covered inclusion topics including racial diversity, disability, women’s rights and LGBTQIA+ content. The complaint says part of the presentation discussed gender identities, sexual orientation, pride flags and student “coming out” stories. It also says a slide titled “How to be an Ally” told students, “When you are standing up against oppression, you are creating a moral standard in your community. You are putting people on notice that targeting any group will not be allowed.”

J.H. became upset during the assembly and began crying, the lawsuit says. According to the complaint, she told school staff she was uncomfortable with the LGBTQIA+ content because of her Christian beliefs.

One teacher responded, “they aren’t forcing their belief on you, but they are informing you about this,” according to the complaint.

The lawsuit says J.H. repeated that she did not feel comfortable going back inside.

It alleges another teacher then told her, “You have no choice because me and Ms. Holmes told the people in charge that you had to use the bathroom.”

J.H. returned to the assembly and remained there in tears, according to the complaint.

After a demand letter from the family’s attorneys, Highline Public Schools denied that J.H.’s constitutional rights were violated, according to the lawsuit.

The complaint says the district asserted that assemblies at Sylvester Middle School are not mandatory, that students may excuse themselves from assemblies and that students who do not attend may go to an alternative location.

The district also claimed J.H. “chose to re-enter the assembly and remained there for the duration of the event,” according to the complaint.

The lawsuit disputes that account, saying J.H. believed school staff were directing her to return.

The complaint says the district’s student handbook does not tell students that assemblies are voluntary or that they may leave for any reason.

The lawsuit also cites a prior incident from January 2022, when J.H. was a second-grade student at North Hill Elementary School. The complaint alleges school officials at that time barred her from distributing religious tracts and crosses, searched her backpack for religious materials and sent her to the principal’s office at least 10 times during recess.

According to the complaint, the district later agreed in writing in 2022 that J.H. could distribute materials at school, including religious materials.

The lawsuit alleges Highline Public Schools has shown a pattern of treating J.H.’s religious expression differently from other student speech.

It brings civil rights claims under the First and Fourteenth Amendments and Washington law.

Jonathan H. is asking the court for declaratory and injunctive relief, compensatory damages, attorney fees and an order allowing J.H. to distribute religious tracts and discuss her faith during noninstructional time.

The lawsuit also seeks to prevent the district from requiring J.H. to attend future programs that conflict with the family’s religious beliefs unless her parents receive advance notice and a meaningful chance to opt out.
