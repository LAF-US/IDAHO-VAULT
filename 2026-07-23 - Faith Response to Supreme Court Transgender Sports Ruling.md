## The Constitution leaves the decision to states. Faith leaves it to us.

### The Law Sets a Minimum. Faith Asks for Something More.

**Guest Column by Pam Kohlmeier**

*The views expressed in this opinion column are those of the author. They do not necessarily reflect the* [*views*](https://favs.news/about-favs-news/) *of* [*FāVS News.*](https://favs.news/)

![cropped drpam](https://favs.news/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/cropped-drpam.jpeg)

Dr. Pam Kohlmeier

The U.S. Supreme Court recently ruled that states may exclude transgender girls and women from participating on girls’ and women’s athletic teams. In upholding laws enacted in [[Idaho]] and West Virginia, the Court concluded that these policies do not violate the Equal Protection Clause of the United States Constitution or Title IX.

The decision concerns school athletics, but its significance extends well beyond the playing field. It reflects a legal landscape in which the rights and daily lives of transgender Americans — and their opportunities to participate fully in their communities — may increasingly depend on the states in which they live and compete.

As both an attorney and a physician, I understand why this decision matters. I also fear its impact.

## ‘What love requires’

Courts define the constitutional boundaries of government power. Federal and state legislatures make policy choices within those boundaries. Ultimately, laws and policies shape the lives of real people, many of whom are already struggling simply to survive. As we debate difficult questions of public policy, we should never lose sight of human beings whose lives are affected.

But for people of faith, the most important question has never been simply what our Constitution permits.

It is what love requires.

While our Constitution establishes the minimum legal obligations of government, faith calls us to something higher.

Jesus never instructed us to love only those whose rights were protected under the law, whose lives made sense to us or with whom we agreed. He simply said, “Love your neighbor as yourself.”

Nothing more.

Nothing less.

His command contains no exceptions.

## What does faith ask of us?

As an emergency physician, I have cared for people on the worst days of their lives. In the emergency department, illness and injury have a way of stripping away labels. At the bedside, political debates fade away. There is simply another human being in need.

Medicine taught me that healing begins by recognizing another person’s humanity. We listen before we judge. We seek to understand before we diagnose. We relieve suffering whenever we can. We should — and must — bear witness to the inherent dignity of every person.

Faith asks the same of us.

As an attorney, I also recognize that legality and morality are not always the same. A law may be constitutional and still leave people feeling excluded, unseen or deeply alone. Legal authority does not determine how people of faith should treat one another.

That distinction is especially important when discussing transgender youth.

Too often they are spoken about as political issues rather than as children.

Children who are vulnerable.

Children who are wondering whether they belong.

Their lives have become legislative debates, court opinions and social media arguments. Meanwhile, they are simply trying to attend school, participate in activities they love, be accepted, know they are loved — and survive.

## The cost of not loving our neighbor

The emotional toll of this environment is profound. According to The Trevor Project’s most recent national survey, approximately 40% of transgender and nonbinary young people seriously considered attempting suicide during the past year, and more than 90% reported that recent anti-LGBTQ+ laws, policies and public debates negatively affected their well-being by causing stress or anxiety.

Research has also found that following the enactment of certain state anti-transgender laws, suicide attempts among transgender and nonbinary youth increased up to 72%, depending on age and the time elapsed after those laws took effect.

Whatever one’s views on public policy, these findings remind us that our words, our debates and the climate we create have real consequences for young lives.

Imagine wondering whether every election, legislative session or court decision will determine whether your community recognizes your humanity.

Whether we agree or disagree about specific public policies, no child should have to question whether they are worthy of love.

## Disagreeing without cruelty

People of faith may sincerely differ on difficult questions involving athletics, privacy, fairness, medical care, education or parental authority. These are complex issues deserving thoughtful and respectful discussion. Good people may arrive at different conclusions.

But disagreement never excuses contempt.

It never justifies cruelty.

It never permits us to forget the humanity of the person standing before us.

Scripture repeatedly reminds us that every human being is created in the image of God. That image does not appear or disappear because of a court ruling, a legislative vote or a cultural debate. It does not depend on whether someone fits comfortably within our own understanding of the world.

Every person remains worthy of dignity.

Jesus consistently moved toward those whom others pushed aside. He saw individuals where society saw categories. He crossed social boundaries to meet people where they were. Again and again, he demonstrated that compassion was never contingent upon agreement.

Our calling has not changed.

The Supreme Court has clarified what states may do.

It has not answered what people of faith *should* do.

That responsibility belongs to us.

## Finding balance in the differences

Here in Washington, we now face important conversations about how best to balance fairness, safety, inclusion and opportunity. Those conversations deserve honesty, humility and careful listening — to students, parents, educators, coaches, physicians and those whose lives are directly affected.

They also deserve compassion.

Whatever policies we ultimately adopt, they should never result in the unnecessary marginalization of a small and vulnerable group of young people. We should reject language that dehumanizes and, instead, choose words that reflect both conviction and grace.

Our churches, perhaps more than anyone, now have an opportunity to decide what kind of witness they will offer.

Will transgender children and their families find communities that welcome them, pray with them, and walk beside them through life’s challenges?

Will parents searching for guidance encounter compassion instead of condemnation?

Will our congregations be known first for their opinions—or for the love of Christ?

The Apostle Paul wrote that the fruit of the Spirit is “love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control.”

Those are the qualities our neighbors should encounter when they meet us.

Not fear.

Not contempt.

Not hostility.

Love.

The Constitution may leave many decisions to the states.

But our faith does not leave love up to chance.

Our faith demands that we love unconditionally, even when we do not fully understand.

Whether history remembers this recent U.S. Supreme Court decision as wise or misguided, I hope it remembers something even more powerful: that when our neighbors felt uncertain, frightened or alone, people of faith chose compassion over condemnation, relationship over rhetoric, and love over fear.

Long after courts have issued their opinions and legislatures have cast their votes, our vulnerable youth will remember how we treated them.

May they find in us not merely an opinion, but compassion. Not merely tolerance, but welcome. Not merely words about love, but the courage to practice it.

*Pam Kohlmeier is running for election to the Washington House of Representatives to represent District 3-Position 2 as a Democrat. She is on the ballot in the primary on Aug. 4. Her career experience includes working as a physician, lecturer and attorney at law.*
