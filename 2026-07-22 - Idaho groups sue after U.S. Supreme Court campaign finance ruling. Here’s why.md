Idaho groups and individuals have filed a lawsuit over state law restricting the amount of money political parties can spend in coordination with their candidates.

The lawsuit alleged that Idaho law on coordinated campaign expenditures between parties and candidates violates the First Amendment. It comes just weeks after the U.S. Supreme Court [issued a ruling](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-621_h315.pdf) that said federal limits on coordinated campaign expenditures were unconstitutional.

“We believe that the limits on party coordinated expenditures impair the party’s traditional form of communication,” Russell Spencer, an attorney representing the plaintiffs, told the Idaho Statesman. “They don’t allow the party to amplify the voice of the members of the party. They end up imposing artificial burdens on the political parties, and that just stifles what a party is able to do, and kind of prevents it from doing what it exists to do.”

The lawsuit said plaintiffs are threatened with “immediate injury” as absentee voting for the upcoming November general election will begin in September.

“Plaintiffs each wish to exercise their First Amendment rights to coordinate, without limitation, campaign expenditures between official party organizations and their candidates,” the lawsuit said. “To that end, Plaintiffs seek declaratory and injunctive relief protecting them from enforcement of this unconstitutional statute.”

The Libertarian Party of Idaho and the Idaho Majority Club filed the lawsuit, along with Desi Burbank, a Republican candidate for the Idaho Senate, and Travis Clyde of the Idaho Majority Club. The suit names Attorney General Raúl Labrador and Secretary of State Phil McGrane. The attorney general did not respond to requests for comment.

McGrane said in a statement that his office has received questions from parties and some candidates about how the Supreme Court decision will impact Idaho’s campaign finance laws. He said his office is working with the attorney general to review Idaho laws, and “a court order may be needed to enact the changes.”

“We’re overdue to update Idaho’s campaign finance laws to better align with modern elections,” McGrane said in a statement to the Statesman. “The most recent example is earlier this month, the U.S. Supreme Court held NRSC v. FEC, which held that the First Amendment allows recognized political parties to coordinate with their party’s nominees related to campaign activity leading up to the general election. … The decision does not immediately apply to states, but it likely will allow some degree of coordination between Idaho’s political parties and their nominees.”

Under Idaho law, there are limits on coordinated campaign expenditures that range per election from $1,000 for local offices to $10,000 for statewide offices.

“These limits are unusually low and severely infringe on the free speech activities of Idaho political parties,” the lawsuit said.

Most other states across the country have no limits, or "reasonably healthy limits” on coordinated campaign expenditures, according to the lawsuit. The lawsuit said the “relative power” of political parties in Idaho has declined in recent years compared to outside groups and that the limits impose a “stifling effect.”

Political parties may spend money on political advertising and “coordinate these activities with their endorsed candidates,” the lawsuit said. These expenditures can include organizing statewide joint fundraising operations or “something as simple as a party chairman asking a candidate to provide a photo and bio so the Party can promote that candidate,” the lawsuit said.

Spencer said political parties exist to get their candidates elected. They want the court to recognize that state political parties have the same First Amendment rights as do national political parties to coordinate expenditures with their nominees.

“Idaho’s current law allows the parties to make unlimited independent expenditures for candidates, but then limits the way those expenditures can be coordinated with their own candidates,” he said, “and that doesn’t make sense.”

In the dissenting opinion for the U.S. Supreme Court, Justice Elana Kagan wrote that the opinion “rewrites the rules, to allow circumvention of the contribution limits” and “ushers back in the same opportunities for quid pro quo corruption that the contribution limits were meant to check.”

Spencer said there are laws to prevent that type of corruption. He added this shouldn’t be a partisan issue.

“This isn’t something … that benefits the Republican Party over the Democrat Party or anything,” he said. “What it’s going to do is it’s going to empower political parties.”
