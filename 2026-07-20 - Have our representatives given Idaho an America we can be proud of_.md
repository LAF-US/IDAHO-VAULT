Since Socrates first asked, some 2,500 years ago, the searching question — “How shall I live?” — philosophers and others exploring the great ethical choices we humans must make through our lifetime, those advocating self-awareness have encouraged consideration of our individual values and beliefs to determine the essential, defining answers to profound questions, including “Who am I?” and, more broadly, “Where am I?” that keep college coffee shops open into the wee hours of the morning as students engage in deep dive conversations that begin to shape their lives.

These absorbing questions are contemplated, perhaps initially, by over-caffeinated, agile young minds but are certainly worthy of life-long exploration, are also ones that the citizenry should be asking of America.

Because the premise and promise of republican self-governance — “We have the power to begin the world over again, ” as beautifully stated in Thomas Paine’s history-making pamphlet, “Common Sense” — is one of citizen empowerment, we the people should ask, similarly, “what is America,” “where is America,” and “how shall America live?”

The power of citizenship — the right of citizenship — is an invitation, indeed, to state it more strongly, the duty, to raise these defining questions about our nation, knowing that the right answers about the nature, state and future of America, must involve a democratically engaged citizenry eager to preserve that which is worth preserving about America, taking stock of where America stands at this juncture in our nation’s history — its strengths and weaknesses, merits and demerits — and asking the most nation-defining question, how shall America live?

These are questions for all seasons, and they are central to Benjamin Franklin’s declaration that delegates to the Constitutional Convention had given us “a republic” if we the people “can keep it.”

Let us briefly consider these categories of questions, with full admission that this effort is but a sketch in an evolving sketchbook.

To ask, “What is America?” is to examine the core beliefs and values of our nation, beginning with our foundational documents — the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution. The Declaration of Independence set forth the majestic principles of life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness, and trumpeted the doctrines of natural rights and popular sovereignty.

These values defined America at the beginning. They stated to the world who we are, what we stand for and, as Lincoln explained, what we aspire to become. In important ways, the Constitution, over time, has fulfilled some of the premises and aspirations of the Declaration and, through various mechanisms such as voting rights and inclusion of women, Black Americans and Native Americans, embodied the values, beliefs and principles that have made America more democratic than it was at the beginning.

Where is America, now?

This solemn question invites reflection on the state of our union, compliance with constitutional commands, the health of the doctrines of separation of powers and checks and balances, the rule of law and judicial independence. A candid appraisal requires consideration of the gulf between constitutional principles and governmental practices, and the relative success and failure of policies, laws and programs. Looking into the mirror and asking if we are proud of ourselves or whether we can live with ourselves will reveal the face we wear to the world, as well as the content of America’s character.

Critical examination should encompass evaluation of our representatives — their motivations, ambitions, virtues and integrity — as well as their devotion to their constituents and, perhaps, their self-serving behavior. This honest, to-the-bone assessment of the nation includes policies, foreign and domestic.

Citizens can create an extensive scorecard and examine and grade such policies as tariffs and war-making, and others that affect affordability, energy and food prices, as well as health care. Is America committed to the grand principles of the Declaration of Independence? Are we a nation that lives up to the principle of equal justice for all or do we have a two-tiered justice system, one for ordinary citizens and another for the rich and powerful?

In the end, the question of how America should live is to ask what values, beliefs and principles should govern the life of our nation. The 250th anniversary of the founding of our country represents a timely opportunity to commence an ongoing conversation of what America stands for, at home and abroad. For starters, let’s ask if we continue to believe in government of, by and for the people?

*David Adler, Ph.D., is president of The Alturas Institute, created to defend American democracy by advancing the Constitution, civic education, equal protection and gender equality.*
