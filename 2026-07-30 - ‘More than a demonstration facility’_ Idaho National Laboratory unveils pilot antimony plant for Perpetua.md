A facility that could process antimony from the Stibnite mine in eastern Valley County for use by the U.S. military is complete.

The pilot processing plant was built by the Idaho National Laboratory in eastern Idaho in partnership with Perpetua Resources, the Boise mining company that owns the Stibnite Gold Project near Yellow Pine.

The Stibnite mine, located about 40 air miles east of McCall, is expected to produce nearly 115 million pounds of antimony over the 12 to 15-year life of mining operations. [Construction on the mine is currently underway](https://valleylookout.com/2026/06/11/perpetua-starts-construction-on-stibnite-mine-amid-ongoing-legal-battles/) and mining could begin as soon as 2029.

“As construction advances at the Stibnite Gold Project, this pilot plant extends that momentum downstream by helping establish the capabilities needed to produce military-grade antimony materials here in America,” Perpetua CEO Jon Cherry said.

Cherry attended a ribbon-cutting ceremony for the new plant on Wednesday at the INL campus near Arco. Gov. Brad Little, Lt. Gov. Scott Bedke and U.S. Rep Mike Simpson were also in attendance.

The pilot plant was funded by the U.S. Department of War, which is expected to receive about 10% of the antimony produced by the Stibnite mine. [The department previously awarded $20 million to Perpetua](https://valleylookout.com/2025/05/28/ground-to-round-perpetua-gets-another-6-9m-from-u-s-military/) to develop a domestic supply chain for antimony, which is deemed a critical mineral by the federal government.

The pilot plant is expected to enable INL to train a military-grade antimony processing workforce while also further developing technical capabilities. The plant will operate for about five months to assess whether antimony at Stibnite can be refined to military grades.

Testing of the plant will begin immediately using rock samples collected at Stibnite, said Jann Marie Saindon, a Perpetua spokesperson.

“We are currently focused on the immediate demonstration of the pilot plant,” Saindon said. “Ultimately, the U.S. Army will own the plant and will determine the best next steps for the technology.”

## ‘Vital’ military munitions

If the pilot plant is successful, the antimony would be used by the Department of War for munitions, mortars, artillery, mines, flares, grenades and missiles.

Currently, nearly all of the global antimony supply is [controlled by China, Russia and Tajikistan](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-antimony.pdf). In 2024, China banned exports of antimony to the United States, though the ban is currently suspended through Nov. 27 under a temporary trade deal.

Col. Steve Adcock, who oversees ammunition at the Picatinny Arsenal in New Jersey, said it is “vital” to national security to establish a domestic supply chain for antimony.

“This pilot plant represents an important step toward reducing America’s dependence on foreign sources of antimony while strengthening the industrial capabilities needed to support our warfighters,” Adcock said. “Partnerships like this help ensure the United States can reliably access the materials essential to military readiness.”

Since 2022, the federal government and U.S. Army have awarded more than $87 million to Perpetua to help the company develop a domestic supply chain for antimony.

The pilot plant is built inside of a shipping container and can be moved to different locations as needed, according to Pearl Loyola of [Westpro Machinery](https://www.westpromachinery.com/), a Canadian company that helped develop the facility.

The plant can process about one ton of antimony ore per day, Loyola said, and could be scaled up to process more antimony and potentially other critical minerals.

“This pilot plant is more than a demonstration facility,” INL Laboratory Director John Wagner said. “It’s an investment in the nation’s critical mineral future.”

[Idaho National Laboratory](https://inl.gov/), located west of Idaho Falls, is one of 17 federally funded research and development centers in the U.S. Department of Energy’s national laboratory program, which was first established after World War II and the Manhattan Project.

The lab has historically been linked to nuclear reactors and first opened in the late 1940s. A reactor at the site was the first to power an entire town with nuclear energy when it turned the lights on in Arco in 1955.

Today, the facility employs more than 6,000 people and continues to conduct research on nuclear energy and cybersecurity to protect power and water infrastructure, as well as other national security initiatives, such as the pilot antimony processing plant.

## Perpetua still seeking commercial antimony processing

Perpetua is still seeking proposals for a commercial processing plant that could process the other 90% of antimony the company expects to extract from three open-pit mines.

“We are still in discussion and due diligence with interested partners,” Saindon said.

In commercial applications, antimony is commonly used for electronics, batteries, green energy, and flame retardants, among other products.

Stibnite is expected to provide up to 35% of the country’s annual demand for antimony in the first six years of operations, according to Perpetua.

Despite the political interest in antimony, most of the Stibnite mine’s value is tied to [4.2 million ounces of gold the mine is expected to produce](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1526243/000110465926037403/ppta-20251231xex96d1.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com). The gold alone would be worth more than $17 billion, based on current prices.

Plans for the Stibnite mine were approved last year by the Payette National Forest, the lead permitting agency for the proposal. An eight-year environmental review of the mine cost Perpetua more than $400 million.

The approval issued by the Forest Service is currently subject to [separate lawsuits filed by conservation groups](https://valleylookout.com/2025/02/19/forest-service-sued-over-stibnite-mine-approval/) and the Nez Perce Tribe. A three-year construction phase, however, is underway.

[Opponents of the mine](https://savethesouthforksalmon.com/) fear it could pollute the East Fork South Fork of the Salmon River, which flows through the project site, and cause other environmental damage.

The Stibnite project zone totals 1,740 acres, on which three open-pit mines spanning 473 acres would be excavated. The project zone is about three miles from the Frank Church – River of No Return Wilderness and is the site of historic mining operations dating to 1899.
