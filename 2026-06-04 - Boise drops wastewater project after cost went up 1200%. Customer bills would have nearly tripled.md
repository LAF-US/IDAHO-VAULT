City officials have put plans to recycle industrial wastewater in Boise’s southeast on the shelf, for now.

Following a work session, the Boise City Council opted to pause plans to develop a recycled water facility on a parcel purchased by the city in 2024. The project would have taken wastewater from businesses and industry and purified it so that it met high standards and could be used again, according to a news release from the city. Boise Public Works Director Steve Burgos told the council that the project would have allowed for “industrial reuse as well as groundwater recharge in an area where we know that the aquifer is starting to fall.”

However, Burgos suggested that the council agree to pivot from the project due to ballooning construction and labor costs. Burgos said that when the project was initially conceived about ten years ago, the city estimated its price tag at $56 million. Two years ago, Burgos said that figure had risen to about $350 million, and today, it would cost $750 million to complete.

One of the city’s main goals is to keep wastewater rates low, Burgos said. Infrastructure projects typically have “off-ramps” at which city staff can evaluate whether to recommend proceeding or not, he said. Recently, with the project design about 30% complete, Burgos’s team received updated cost figures and decided to recommend a change of course.

“The consensus was, we’ve got to talk to council,” Burgos said. “This is not feeling right.”

## Rising costs

The average cost for water renewal services for a Boise resident is $60 per month, Burgos said. Had the city moved forward with the project, it would have brought that cost to $175 per month, he said.

“That’s almost a tripling of our current rates. Again, it just did not feel like we could make that recommendation to council,” he said.

Abby Haydin, a spokesperson for the city’s public works department, said that the project was initially conceived as a means to expand capacity in the city’s water renewal service system — the city’s term for wastewater treatment — as the population grows. In the early stages of planning, city staff envisioned a facility that would have taken industrial wastewater, treated it, and put it in a pond where it could slowly filter down through the soil and into the groundwater, Haydin said.

However, due to the makeup of the soil and rock on site, the project shifted from the pond filtration system to injection, or piping the water directly into the ground, Haydin said. The Idaho Department of Environmental Quality requires that injection projects use recycled water that is treated to drinking water standards, which also contributed to an increase in the cost of the project, she said.

Burgos said the city planned to use some of the funding from a $570 million bond approved by voters in 2021 to pay for the facility’s construction. The city spent about $17 million on this particular recycled water facility.

The facility was also in the works prior to the announcement of Micron’s two new fabrication plants. Burgos clarified that following conversations with the city, the company opted to fund its own system that allows it to recycle almost all of its water. That means that the city will not play a large role in the company’s water renewal services for its expansion, he said.

## New water recycling opportunities

Burgos said that while it is possible the project could move forward with a new funding source, some new opportunities for water recycling exist today that didn’t exist ten years ago.

![Zamzows - Class - 4:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/299f1d40-a259-40e1-a36e-e3c98ae2cb8d.png)

Zamzows - Class - 4:1

Haydin said that while old regulations limited water recycling projects to recycling industrial wastewater, cities can now receive permits to recycle residential wastewater.

Before that change, the city was contemplating having to build a separate system to collect and process industrial wastewater, rather than simply taking wastewater from its existing wastewater streams. Haydin said that the city could construct recycling projects at either its Lander or West Boise water renewal plants, or elsewhere in the city, and connect them to its existing infrastructure.

## Funding for water-related capital improvements

Burgos said that to keep providing a high level of service that prevents accidents like sinkholes and sewage overflows, the city has to keep up with rising costs.

The city currently treats about 30 million gallons of wastewater per day and is upgrading its Lander St. facility, which will add an additional 5 million gallons of processing capacity, Burgos said. The city is also investing in its West Boise facility and in phosphorus removal needed to meet newer treatment regulations.

The city is also investing in replacing piping. While it has done so at a rate of 2-3 miles per year, it needs to replace 10 miles per year to keep up with pipes that are reaching the end of their lifespan, he said.

Altogether, Burgos estimated that the city needs to be spending $1.3 billion over the next ten years, or about $130 million per year on these needs. The city has recently reached a point where it may surpass $100 million per year in that area of spending, he said.

To address increased funding needs, the city will likely need to consider some combination of bonds and rate increases, and staff need time to study potential options, Burgos said.
