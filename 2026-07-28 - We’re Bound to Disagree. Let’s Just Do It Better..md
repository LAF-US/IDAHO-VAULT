State leaders shared how and why to disagree better in the opening session of NCSL’s Legislative Summit in Chicago.

Panelists began with a lightning round of tips: “Build personal relationships,” suggests Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker. Or, as Oklahoma Gov. Kevin Stitt recommends, “focus on policy, not personality. That’s a great way to talk with someone that might not see the world the way you do.”

Ohio House Speaker Matt Huffman says, “You have to acknowledge that you’re going to disagree and sometimes vociferously. And if you cede things to the other side, this is what Abraham Lincoln said: ‘It doesn’t do any damage to you; it facilitates agreement.’” Pennsylvania House Speaker Joanna McClinton suggests trying to understand how your opponent sees things and asking yourself, “Do you want to solve the problem or just debate?”

> “It’s worth remembering that disagreement is hardly new. From the start, Americans have argued about the structure of government, the balance of power, and how to weigh individual versus collective rights.”
> 
> —Susan Urahn, president and CEO, The Pew Charitable Trusts

The group [Disagree Better](https://disagreebetter.us/) joined NCSL to sponsor the discussion. The independent nonprofit was launched in 2023 by Utah Gov. Spencer Cox, who believed leaders from both parties should demonstrate a different way forward. Research shows that’s what the public wants—9 in 10 Americans support working across the aisle to solve differences, according to Disagree Better. And a survey of 40 years of congressional data shows lawmakers are more effective if they build bipartisan support.

Each panelist has come up against tough opposition but says that is when it’s crucial to remain even-keeled and focus the conversation on shared values.

McClinton put that to the test last year when a debate over a gun control bill nearly erupted into a brawl as lawmakers circled one another on the House floor. After gaveling repeatedly and calling on the deputies to restore order—“It was ugly,” she says—McClinton steered everyone back to their shared purpose.

“I said, ‘Listen, why are you here? Why did you ask your neighbors for the privilege of serving in this body? Why did you sign up for the hard work? Did you come here so you could fight a colleague from the other party? Did you come here so you could engage in physical violence? Did you come here to ruin your reputation? Did you come here to get arrested?’” McClinton says. The reminder seemed to calm things down.

“It does start with just recognizing that the people that you’re talking to on the other side of the aisle have families, are decent people who genuinely want to do good things for the state and for the country,” Pritzker says. “I like to say even Republicans have good ideas every once in a while,” he adds with a chuckle, noting Republicans could say that about Democrats, too.

The leaders say the point isn’t to avoid conflict when working through issues.

“You can’t take your ball and go home just because there’s a conflict. That’s a loser strategy,” Stitt says. “Our Founding Fathers had massive disagreements. But when you stay in there and you are focused on the next generation, not just the next election, I think that just changes the dynamic of how we can solve some of these major issues in our country.”

Stitt, who heads the National Governors Association, took a high-profile bipartisan stance this year when he told President Donald Trump the NGA would not meet at the White House if the president followed through on his decision to ban some Democratic governors. Trump relented.

“Teddy Roosevelt started this organization back when he became president because he was a governor of New York and knew the importance of federalism and having all the governors at the table bringing the states’ concerns,” Stitt says. “It’s important that we bring those issues to the White House, whether it’s a Biden White House or a Trump White House. There’s a long history of us coming together and actually dealing with problems that affect our states.”

Huffman says some people seem to relish conflict with a “bring it on” attitude.

“But those who can avoid unnecessary conflict usually succeed because they’re not building an entire aisle of refuse between you and the other person,” Huffman says.

He says social media often dangerously amplifies conflict and promotes misinformation, and he urges leaders to resist the temptation to join the fray.

Panel moderator Susan Urahn, president and CEO of The Pew Charitable Trusts, notes that conflict has a long history in the U.S.

“As we mark America’s 250th anniversary,” she says, “it’s worth remembering that disagreement is hardly new. From the start, Americans have argued about the structure of government, the balance of power, and how to weigh individual versus collective rights. But our nation’s founders framed their arguments around what they thought was best for the country as a whole, guided by a shared vision of a nation established on the principles of individual freedom, equality and opportunity.”

With that in mind, McClinton urges legislators to be leaders. “Don’t be a follower in this age when there’s misinformation, disinformation, name calling. You stand up and you be the leader that our time is demanding.

“It’s up to us to show what this democracy will be all about for the next 250 years,” she says.
