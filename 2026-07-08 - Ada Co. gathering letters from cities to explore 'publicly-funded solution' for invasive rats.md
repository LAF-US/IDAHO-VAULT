As invasive rats expand their reach across the Treasure Valley, some cities are debating what action they and local counties should take to stem the spread.

On Tuesday, the Kuna City Council heard from Adam Schroeder, director of Ada County’s Weed, Pest, and Mosquito Abatement agency, about stopping the rapid spread of invasive Norwegian and roof rats in the county. Kuna Mayor Joe Stear said that the city is in the process of drafting a letter asking the county to take action on the issue. After Schroeder’s presentation, Stear said that the city would continue its drafting prior to bringing the letter back for the council’s consideration at its next meeting.

Schroeder told the council that he is getting two to five calls per week from residents concerned about rats on their property. The rats pose a concern because they can spread diseases to people like leptospirosis and salmonellosis. In addition to residential properties, Schroeder said that the rats are getting into feedlots and dairies in agricultural areas, as well as commercial kitchens and restaurants.

The discussion comes as counties and cities in Southwest Idaho decide how to act against the pests following a year in which the Idaho Legislature attempted and failed to pass legislation to address the problem, as BoiseDev reported. Concerns raised during bill discussions included a desire to specify the creatures as a nuisance [in order to mobilize state resources against them](https://boisedev.com/news/2026/03/27/rats-local-gannon/), while at the same time, finding a solution that avoids growing government and taking away business from [private pest control companies](https://boisedev.com/news/2026/03/17/rats-house-denied/). In June, Rep. John Gannon and Sen. Tammy Nichols sent a letter to local governments in Ada County urging them to take action in ways they can without dedicating additional resources, [as BoiseDev reported](https://boisedev.com/news/2026/06/25/idaho-legislators-urge-local-government-action-on-multiplying-pest/).

Stear said that Ada County Commissioner Ryan Davidson told him that the commissioners would like to see letters from Ada County cities encouraging ongoing discussions to find solutions. So far, Boise, Meridian, and Eagle have submitted letters, Stear said.

## Potential county action

Schroeder said that his purpose in speaking to the council was to raise awareness and to encourage the city to do what it can to address the problem sooner than later.

“It just gets more expensive and more difficult as time goes on,” Schroeder said.

He said he has spoken to Boise and Meridian on the issue as well, and that the City of Eagle is “very engaged in this issue.” The rats, which are believed to have hitched a ride in shipping containers from California, were first observed in Eagle, [as BoiseDev reported](https://boisedev.com/news/2026/03/27/rats-local-gannon/).

The Ada County Board of County Commissioners is exploring a publicly-funded solution to address the issue, but is “reticent to compete with private industry.” To that end, a county program might look like offering free rat traps, offering inspections, and potentially doing trapping in areas that are not actively managed by parcel owners, he said.

Schroeder estimated that the county would need about $1 million per year for a program to “have any kind of effect on rat populations.” That figure could include funding a division coordinator to lead rat-related programs and paying for inspectors. That could include setting aside funds for contracting with private pest control services to control rats in homes or areas where rat trapping and other prevention efforts by landowners are falling short, he said.

## Avoiding additional taxes, levies

Council President Chris Bruce said he would like the City of Kuna’s letter to say that the council is interested in hearing the county’s ideas on potential solutions. He said that if funds will be allocated toward the issue, he would prefer designating some money in the budget to that purpose rather than creating an additional levy or tax.

“That way, we can say, ‘this is what we’re going to spend on it; we discussed it,’” Bruce said. “Sometimes levies can get open-ended, or continue forever.”
