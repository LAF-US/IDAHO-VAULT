A temporary market that would have brought vendors of all kinds to Canyon County’s Sunnyslope area will not be moving forward.

The Canyon County Planning and Zoning Commission voted against allowing a conditional use permit for a “weekend bazaar,” or flea market, at 20051 Lowell Rd. in Caldwell. Plans for the site, which is zoned for agriculture, called for up to 328 vendors, space for 12 food trucks, and about 928 parking spaces across about 35 acres.

The applicant, Chris Liudahl, said that he had enjoyed visiting a large flea market in eastern Washington. He said he wanted to provide a market as a family-friendly option for visitors to Sunnyslope, something he said the wineries in the area don’t currently offer.

Canyon County staff, however, recommended denial of the proposal, saying the land use did not fit with the county’s comprehensive plan’s vision for land use in that area. The commission voted unanimously to deny the application at its May 21 meeting.

Though the applicant had the option of appealing the commission’s vote, Canyon County Constituent Services Director Steve Cushman said that the county had not received a request to do so.

It’s not the first proposal for a non-agricultural use for the site, owned by Symms Fruit Ranch, in recent years. Arbay Mberwa, an associate planner with the county, told the commission that a proposal came before the county in 2022 to create an RV park on the site, which was denied. In 2025, the county did approve a retail nursery and food processing facility for six acres on the northeast corner of the site, she said.

## ‘I want this out of the city’

In application materials, Liudahl referred to the market as a “Norwegian weekend bazaar.” When asked by the commissioners if the market would have any kind of cultural connection to Norway, Liudahl said, “Not really.”

“I just think it flows better than ‘flea market,’ and having Norwegian heritage on my father’s side, it just sounds good,” Liudahl said.

Liudahl said that the market would be open to all kinds of vendors and food trucks, as well as groups such as Girl Scouts and fire departments, or other organizations interested in doing community outreach.

He proposed having vendors sign up and set up to sell at the market on Fridays, with the market open Saturdays and Sundays, and teardown on Mondays.

Liudahl said that he considered having the market at the original D&B location at 3816 Cleveland Blvd., but that it’s now used by Dennis Dillon. And though someone suggested the old Kmart at Karcher Rd. and Nampa Caldwell Blvd., Liudahl said in-city locations did not make sense.

“I want this out of the city because what we have in the city now, it would just be more chaos,” Liudahl said, citing heavy traffic.

![Corwin Ford - One Tank Trips - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/9934db1b-47e1-4fe3-ab77-78261066f89f.jpg)

Corwin Ford - One Tank Trips - 2:1

In a traffic study conducted for the project, Liudahl estimated that a Saturday would see about 450 car trips in and out of the site: 225 for vendors and 160 for customers. Liudahl said those numbers reflect that he anticipated taking time to get the market to full vendor capacity.

Vendors would use 10×10 canopies that would be dismantled between markets, he said.

## Staff, public, commissioners voice concerns

Mberwa said that planning staff recommended denial of the permit. The parcel that the market would be on falls within the county’s agritourism overlay. Though the market could include agricultural vendors, it would not be exclusively for those vendors. Its purpose also wouldn’t be to support agriculture in the area, and therefore would not meet the spirit of the overlay, she said.

![Corwin Ford - One Tank Trips - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/9934db1b-47e1-4fe3-ab77-78261066f89f.jpg)

Corwin Ford - One Tank Trips - 2:1

The staff report said that the market would be “injurious to other property in the immediate vicinity,” including residents of several subdivisions, due to its scale during weekend operating hours. It also said it would change the area’s character, which is agricultural and residential. The staff report also noted that the land proposed for the market is considered prime farmland if irrigated.

Three people testified against the market at the hearing, expressing concerns about changes to the quiet character of the area.

Commissioners agreed, expressing skepticism with the traffic impact study and the number of people that would potentially visit the market.

Commission Chairman Robert Sturgill, who said that he recently traveled out of state to visit a large flea market, could not support the market at its proposed location.

“I feel bad; I love this type of business,” Sturgill said, “but it’s just entirely incompatible with the region.”
