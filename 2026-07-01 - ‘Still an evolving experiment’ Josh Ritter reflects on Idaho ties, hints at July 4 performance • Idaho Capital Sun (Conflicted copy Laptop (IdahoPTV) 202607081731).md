## ‘Still an evolving experiment’: Josh Ritter reflects on Idaho ties, hints at July 4 performance

Acclaimed songwriter and America 250 headliner hopes music can be ‘transcendent experience’ at Boise’s Independence Day celebration

![Songwriter Josh Ritter performs live. Ritter is set to headline Idaho's America 250 free concert on July 4, 2026. (Photo by Moonloop Photography LLC/ Brilliant Corners Artist Management)](https://idahocapitalsun.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/DSC_7748ed-2048x1365.jpg)

At 19, Joshua Ritter, born in Moscow to parents Robert and Sue, left the state to pursue his education and music career in Ohio. More than 30 years, 13 albums and two novels later, Ritter is back in Idaho to headline America’s 250-year celebration.

The folk singer/songwriter, announced as a headliner by the [America250 in Idaho Advisory Council in May](https://idahocapitalsun.com/2026/05/26/songwriter-josh-ritter-to-headline-idaho-america250-celebration-in-boise/), now prepares for an occasion he told the Idaho Capital Sun in an interview will be personal and impactful. Above all, he said the event creates an opportunity to bring people together in “fraught times.”

“The beautiful thing about music is you could have a transcendent experience in your own bedroom, or you could have it in a church or on a mountainside, or in groups of people, it doesn’t matter,” he said of the event. “It’s just magic.”

The America 250 in Idaho concert will begin at 11:30 a.m. Saturday in Boise’s Cecil D. Andrus Park. For more information on the schedule, or other America 250 events, see [https://america250.idaho.gov/capitol-celebration/](https://america250.idaho.gov/capitol-celebration/).

“We are thrilled to have Josh Ritter headline the America 250 Capitol Celebration in Boise,” Idaho Secretary of State Phil McGrane said in an emailed statement to the Idaho Capital Sun. “An accomplished performer, songwriter, and proud Idahoan, Ritter was a natural choice for organizers. We appreciate his commitment to Idaho and know he is excited to perform in his home state. As with other featured artists set for the Capitol Celebration, it is great to highlight an artist who frequently showcases Idaho and his hometown of Moscow in his music.”

## Josh Ritter is Idaho born and raised

There may be few musicians more intimately linked with the Western spirit of Idaho’s America 250 celebration than Ritter.

His 1999 self-titled debut plays on the Americana often seen in Western music to talk about love and time, setting himself apart with classic folk ballads in “ [Hotel Song](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=doHtGp1oP9Y&list=RDdoHtGp1oP9Y&start_radio=1) ” or “ [Leaves and Kings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OLcrJGJfK20&list=RDOLcrJGJfK20&start_radio=1).”

Since then, Ritter’s soundscape has continued to build on folk-heavy instrumentals, resembling the likes of Bob Dylan or Nick Drake, to chart his own path – launching his 2006 record “The Animal Years” into 11th place on [Paste Magazine’s](https://web.archive.org/web/20170708231803/https://www.pastemagazine.com/articles/2017/04/the-best-indie-folk-albums-1.html) 2017 Top 100 folk indie albums of all time.

At every step, Ritter said the landscape of Moscow remained in his mind, prefiguring the visuals behind much of his folk-oriented lyricism.

“When I’m writing or singing songs, it often becomes less about language and more about visuals, what I’m seeing in my mind’s eye,” he said. “Those scenes are always set in Idaho.”

“There’s always that landscape, I’m always seeing the ponderosa and the mountains where I grew up … It was all I’d ever known, it was everything, and it still is very deeply in me,” he added.

Memories of Moscow, in fact, seemed to inspire a large part of his creative endeavors. The city and state blend into his 2021 novel “The Great Glorious Goddamn of It All,” a historical fiction set in a small Idaho timber town. Whether writing songs or novels, Ritter said Idaho is always there.

Looking back at his childhood, Ritter recalled a now-fuzzy scene delivering newspapers for a Friday nightly route. He did not work on the paper routes for long, lacking the “diligence” to “get those farmers the papers,” but he said the memory – the feeling – stuck.

“I listened to the radio on (those) late nights, and I listened to the music coming from all around, whether it was at the university radio, from Moscow or Lewiston,” Ritter reminisced. “Those long night drives through the wheat fields and the lentil fields, that really gave me a moment to become a musician and a songwriter.”

Many songs in his catalogue, such as “ [Wings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-eimVCdZffU&list=RD-eimVCdZffU&start_radio=1) ” and “ [Idaho](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jnI-nQfF46A&list=RDjnI-nQfF46A&start_radio=1),” evoke the distinct feel of country backroads: beckoning cedar trees, gravel roads and meadows reminiscent of Idaho.

Images of the West also accompany a focus on memory and the role it plays on individuals. Ritter said this focus on remembrance helps his songs reach a “sense of sturdiness.”

“Memory is this place between the present and the past, where there’s a boundary, there’s friction, and there’s magic,” he said. “I try not to write songs that have a specific time set to them, I try to keep things as unbound by a present time as possible … so for me, memory is incredibly important.”

For Ritter, the “liminal space” found in memories represents a magical in-between that, when thinking of his hometown, can transform quotidian spaces into “much huger and much more vibrant (things) than perhaps they ever were.”

He gave the example of fishing, where reflecting on childhood experiences can make the smallest catch seem gigantic in retrospect. Like a tall tale, he said the fish “ gets to be enormous by the end of your life, but it may have only been this big when you caught it.”

“When I travel back now, I can mark the first time that I smell the pine trees,” he said. “We eventually don’t smell it, but we know it’s home.”

“We all have those markers in our minds, so when I come home I experience the real joy of being back (in) my hometown,” he added.

## Celebrating America 250 in ‘fraught times’

Ritter said he joined the event at the calling of close friend and confidant Eric Gilbert, co-founder of Treefort Music Fest. Upon receiving Gilbert’s call, Ritter said he thought the opportunity could be a “wild, beautiful experience.”

Music, Ritter said, has the ability to bring people together. If he could give something back to Idaho through this event, he said it would be a “sense of common joy.”

“This is a very fraught time in our country that has marred us, but it hasn’t broken us,” Ritter said of his experiences coming home. “We are still an evolving experiment here in America.”

“There’s all sorts of things that I could talk about, my own beliefs in social justice are out there,” he said. “But there is something to be said for a bunch of people together experiencing music, I’m for that.”

Although Ritter’s music largely avoids clear political identification, some of his songs discuss topics relevant to the political zeitgeist. His 2019 song “All Some Kind of Dream,” for example, touches on the immigrant legacy of America, asking listeners: “I saw my country in the hungry eyes of a million refugees … There was a time when we were them, just as now they all are we. Was there an hour when we took them in? Or was it all some kind of dream?”

Still, Ritter said he would rather avoid an instructional approach to music.

“There’s all sorts of ways to approach being given a microphone at an important moment, but I don’t believe in music as being a form of instructor or educator,” he said. “My belief is that music, like in songs, has the ability to release our own consciousness and to allow us to see things in new ways.”

“In the context of a very significant day in our history, that was important to me, I felt like I had something that I could offer,” he added.

With a few days remaining before his performance, Ritter looks forward to seeing the familiar crowd.

“It really is different when you’re playing for old friends and family, those folks that you grew up with,” he said. “I am very much looking forward to being on stage and playing those first couple of notes.”
