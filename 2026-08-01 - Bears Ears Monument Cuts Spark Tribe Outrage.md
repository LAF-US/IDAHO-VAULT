## Bears Ears cuts strip 90% of protected land tribes call sacred

### Trump’s order shrinking Bears Ears and Grand Staircase-Escalante monuments sparks tribal backlash over sacred lands, mining expansion and federal trust obligations.

**By Becky Tallent | FāVS News Columnist**

*The views expressed in this opinion column are those of the author. They do not necessarily reflect the* [*views*](https://favs.news/about-favs-news/) *of* [*FāVS News.*](https://favs.news/)

It’s happened again: Federal government promises made to Indigenous tribes have been broken.

This time it is erasing more than 90% of the protected area for both Bear’s Ears (from 1.36 million to 121,096 acres) and Grand Staircase Escalante (from 1.9 million to 182,000 acres) in Utah. In a July 13 executive order, President Donald Trump said the areas were too large to be protected and should be opened for mining, grazing and other options.

## Sacred land, not just scenery

However, the areas now eliminated from protection contain religious and sacred areas to the local tribes, including the Utes, Navajo, Hopi, Kaibab Band of Paiute Indians, Paiute Indian Tribe of Utah, Ute Mountain Ute Tribe and the Zuni Tribe. The Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition said the area is sacred land, a living cultural space where the land, its resources and relationship between the land and the tribes should be protected.

Tribal leaders call the move a slap in the face; government officials say it is “right sizing” the monuments.

Perhaps the most egregious part of the whole episode is the tribes were not consulted or even contacted ahead of the signing eliminating their sacred lands and the Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition. In the proclamation, Trump even throws doubts on whether the tribes have lived in the area as long as history indicates.

In his proclamation, Trump said the land, also previously protected by the Antiquities Act, “no longer are, or never were, deserving of the Antiquities Act’s protections.”

Ignoring the sacred aspects of the area for the tribes, the proclamation noted the geographical aspects of the land are “generic features, not landmarks, structures or (of) scientific interest worthy of protection.” Areas of historic or scientific interests are already protected by law, Trump said.

Instead, the proclamation opens the land for mining – especially for silver, copper, lead, uranium, zinc and other minerals – grazing and recreational projects.

But the land is sacred to the area’s tribes. In addition to the burial grounds, rock wall paintings and carvings, there are ceremonial grounds, cliff dwellings, trails and ceremonial routes and landmarks holy to the tribes, who were living in the area long before the United States became a country.

Think of it this way, by turning over the sacred tribal lands to developers and mining operations it is the same as turning local churches over to businesses to replace them with convenience stores or other enterprises.

“The federal government must honor its Trust and Treaty obligations to our tribes — it is not optional,” Autumn Gillard, a member of the Southern Paiute and Grand Staircase Escalante Inter-Tribal Coalition coordinator told the Arizona Republic.

“Today’s action is a direct strike against the federal government’s duty to consult with the tribes,” she added. “It also profoundly disrespects our intergenerational traditional knowledge by destroying the framework for tribal co-stewardship over our ancient lands in which we invested years of effort. Today’s actions cannot stand.”

## A legal and political fight ahead

Trump originally reduced the lands in 2017, but those reductions were restored by President Joe Biden in 2021. Trump’s current action reduces both monuments to less than 10% of their previous size. Trump and members of Utah’s Republican Party said this is necessary to reduce costs, plus, they argue the people of Utah want the land.

However, recent polling shows the citizens of Utah want the monuments’ designations to remain as they were. While opening the land to more mining might be jobs, it also brings destruction of the landscape and pollution issues, especially for water — something in short supply in the arid area.

Like many of Trump’s orders, this once again ignores existing federal law. Under a 1996 law, federal agencies are required to submit rules such as land management plans to Congress for a mandatory review period before they can take effect. The presidential order should fall under this law; but this order required the new plan to take effect without review 60 days from Trump’s signature.

The environmental group Earth Justice is already filing legal action; the tribes are looking at the same. As Davina Smith-Idjesa, a Navajo and member of the Bear’s Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition told the Salt Lake Tribune, “It was just another form of erasure for tribal nations.”
