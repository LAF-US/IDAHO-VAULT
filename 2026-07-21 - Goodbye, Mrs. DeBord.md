I was supposed to be at a memorial Monday (or celebration of life, as we say now). It was held back home in Payette, Idaho, and honoring my favorite teacher, an early mentor, and advisor to the school newspaper: Mrs. Anne DeBord. But instead, I spent the evening before my flight at the ER thanks to a (possibly foodborn) gastrointestinal virus. So, I had to cancel my trip the morning of my flight. I spent the weekend (and so far this week) moving hurriedly between the bathroom and the bedroom with the blood pressure reading of a toddler.

(And before you ask, no, they did not say it was the C word since we haven’t had a big *cyclosporiasis* outbreak here in California. I’ve avoided raw vegetables, fast food, and especially any lettuce for two weeks as well. Docs there gave me stuff to treat the symptoms and today they are beginning to narrow, though what remains is weirdly agonizing pain—as though there is a giant doughnut around my abdominal midsection and lower back, squeezing and cramping repeatedly throughout the day. But enough about my intestinal issues.)

I really wanted to be there in person not because I was her favorite student (I’m sure there were so many students that thought they were her favorites; I’m one of many students). No, I just wanted to stand in front of my community and say how important and how pivotal Mrs. DeBord has been in my life. At the peak of my career, I was CEO of the largest LGBTQ media company and served as the editorial director of several magazines, *The Advocate, Out, Plus*, *Chill*, and *Out Traveler*, plus a half dozen websites and a podcast. I became something I’m proud of, I got dozens of awards, and a lot of industry respect (and named one of *Folio* ’s Top Women in Media, which Mrs. DeBord told me afterwards was “impressive”).

Yes, even decades later, as we’re both adults and we’re both on equal footing, and I’m at the top of my industry, I still could not call her Anne. We would speak via Facebook or Instagram over the years, and I still called her Mrs. DeBord (below) or Mrs D like I was 16.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!pqhY!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc8f1b94e-fdb8-4e98-a6a1-ee75e519a204_1145x1222.jpeg)

Her son Eric reached out after she turned 88, as he was helping clean out some of her stuff (as widowed grandmothers do). He came across a note in a book that I gave her back in the 1980s on Ernest Hemingway. Truthfully, I could not stand Ernest Hemingway back in high school, but Mrs. D loved his work, and so we read many of his books in her classes, and overtime there began to be things that I did appreciate about Hemingway.

He began his career as a journalist before he was a famous novelist. He worked as a reporter for the *Kansas City Star* and later served as a foreign correspondent and war correspondent for the *Toronto Star.* That’s where that author honed his signature style: short, concise, almost minimalist sentences without any filler. That’s what I disliked then (as well as the hyper-masculinity) but admire now. Especially, as I am completely unable to write a sentence that is sort and concise, as my book editor will tell you.

Hemingway returned to journalism repeatedly to cover major historical events, including the Greco-Turkish War, the Spanish Civil War, and World War II (the liberation of Paris). Many of these real-life journalistic experiences provided the inspiration and settings for his most famous novels, *A Farewell to Arms* and *For Whom the Bell Tolls*.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!h2FZ!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F19fbf63f-55a1-42d9-990c-bf5b12067159_2268x4032.jpeg)

His house in Key West, Florida, is now a museum, but it’s worth a visit. A sailing buddy once gave Hemingway a kitten who was polydactyly, meaning she had six toes on each paws instead of the usual five. Those cats are considered good luck charms and they’re kind of adorable. Today there’s about 60 cats living there who carry that same gene (all descendants of that one cat). Hemingway would name them after celebs like Audrey Hepburn and Mark Twain but I can’t remember if museum staff still did that. (If this was just any ol’ meandering essay, I would also go into a tangent about how Hemingway’s family made Sun Valley, Idaho, their second home and how his granddaughter Mariel was fantastic in *Personal Best* and *Sex Monster.*)

But I digress. I do have a virus that adds to my brain fog, so you’ll have to excuse me.

One thing I keep pondering is probably less about her and more about me (kind of like this essay). I’ve been thinking recently about how strange it feels to have all the people I cared about impressing now dead: My parents, my in-laws, my aunt, J’s aunt, my grandmother. For me, Mrs. DeBord topped that list. I always hoped she was proud. I always feared disappointing her.

When I stole a car my first year out of high school (that’s another story), I was mortified wondering what she would think. (For sure, she thought I was being an idiot, but so did everybody else once we got caught.)

Later, when I was coming out queer (or bisexual or lesbian or pan or whatever it was that I was then, which generally depends on who you ask), I cared about the opinions of just a couple of people back home and Mrs. DeBord was on the top of that list. I was so worried about rejection and abandonment that instead of telling people, “Hey I’m in love with another girl—and you know her!” I would send them news clippings about me or articles I had written about myself. (I mean I was only 19.)

It’s safe to say the media knew before my friends and family did, which made coming out to them easier for me at least. Mrs. DeBord did not blink an eye. I was never sure if that meant she didn’t care or that she was just good at keeping her opinion to herself, until I met her granddaughter, who is also queer and an actor. She sent me this amazing photo of Mrs. D pushing a walker with a rainbow flag on top of it. I don’t know if it took work in their family to get to that accepting place or not. But truly seeing that image made me smile, especially knowing that her Gen Z granddaughter shares that legacy.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1-lV!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4350c04b-de62-4941-ac78-1504c1854227_1187x1665.jpeg)

When I left mainstream media for LGBTQ media, I was personally so happy and gung-ho that I sent her a copy of the *Crescent City Star* in New Orleans, a weekly newspaper where I served as the editor in chief when I was 19 or 20. I was incredibly proud of what I was doing and the life I was living (later moving from New Orleans to New York to Los Angeles then back to New Orleans to San Francisco and back to LA, each time for brand new career opportunities). But I was so nervous that Mrs. DeBord would think that I had made a wrong choice in career moves. I had enjoyed working at magazines everyone had heard of because they were on the grocery store stands, but my calling was in magazines far fewer people read but with absolutely critical missions and devoted readerships. (And eventually, those became big name magazines too, at least some of them.)

But if Mrs. D was ever disappointed in my direction, she never told me so, and she gave me nothing but unconditional support and feedback as well.

The thing that I’ve never really told most classmates, is that I met Mrs. DeBord while I was still in junior high school, not high school. In my head it was much like that scene from the original Karate Kid, where Daniel (Ralph Macchio) meets his future mentor, Mr. Miyagi, after his bicycle breaks down and Miyagi (played by Pat Morita) fixes the bike while effortlessly using meditative breathing techniques.

Payette had a small weekly newspaper called the *Independent Enterprise* and Darlene Allison, the newspaper’s editor-in-chief, let me start writing a column about what was happening at school and covering some school board meetings while i was in junior high. (Fun fact: I graduated to working at its sister newspaper in Oregon, the Daily Argus Observer, the summer after high school graduation. After being named lifestyle editor *pro tem*, I was so thrilled thinking I was already at a career pinnacle that I considered staying and skipping college. The only other woman in the newsroom, Ann Crosby—who later became mayor of my hometown and is somehow befuddlingly distantly related to me—asked if I ever wanted to make more than $12,000 a year. I did, so she told me to go to college. So, I did.)

Darlene introduced me to Mrs. DeBord long before I became part of Mrs. D’s class. I think the combo of Anne and Allison (giving me direction) and my grandmother and aunt (offering me a new middle-class life) kept me alive when I wasn’t sure I wanted to be—and saved me from running away and working the streets. That had been a plan just a year before I moved to Payette, a rural town of 5,000 where my family history was five generations deep but where I had only spent summers until both my parents agreed I could stay there. I had been juggled about from household to household for the first 12 years and desperately needed the community, safety, stabilty, and friendships Payette afforded.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ekpz!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe909d1d4-148c-40a0-bfdd-332da6995440_3024x3024.jpeg)

That’s me in ninth grade. I’m the two brunette photos on the bottom. The rest are classmates. And no, he isn’t really choking me.

In junior high, while i was writing school column for the *Enterprise,* one of my middle school English teachers (who I adored, even though I can no longer remember his name) let me start what I called our junior high newspaper (but should more fairly be described as a mimeographed newsletter).

To make it, the newsletter had to be typed out on a wax sheet on a typewriter, which would create a stencil of it. Then that stencil was wrapped around a big rotating drum filled with ink, and as you hand cranked it, this memorable bright blue ink would seep through the wax sheet onto blank paper. The blue sheets looked and smelled fantastic to me. A semester or two later, the office got a newer Ditto machine that was sort of similar but put out soggy purple pages. (Just imagine how excited I was to see an actual IBM printer when I got to New York.)

But the combo of all that: meeting Mrs. DeBord, writing a column, publishing this tiny newsletter (that was probably too much about me and not enough about everybody else, like this essay) created this sort of life-changing vortex. I was hooked. No more dreaming of being an actress, no more boo-hooing that I was too short and too fat to be a model. No more planning to run away and work the streets like the lead character in the movie *Angel*. I was a writer and a journalist.

The next year I began Mrs. DeBord’s class. I was on so excited you would’ve thought I had won a Pulitzer. My first year of journalism class and being on the *Cutlass* staff was terrifying. All the kids, the leaders at least, were all older. One of them was kind of a bully. I was kind of a big fat gossip, so maybe I deserve some of that bullying. Still, I found it terrifying because I had been bullied in early childhood to a physical extent. I was terrified of physical violence. I got bullied in that class. I got threatened in that class. I’d often leave school after that class ended, so I didn’t get beat up. That was what I was always pretty sure was going to happen, but it didn’t. And I think Mrs. DeBord both helped mitigate my fears but also curtailed the bullying when she found out from another classmate who never heard the adage “snitches get stitches.”

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PLfo!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3f84e7ed-afda-4504-9192-d83423662f22_1038x1408.jpeg)

It’s a funny thing but even when I skipped classes in high school, I would always go to the two classes I had with Mrs. DeBord: journalism and English. And then I could skip the rest of the day, sometimes privately sobbing over what someone in class had said (that first year). Then I came back the next day without anyone knowing how vulnerable and terrified I felt.

The next year, she made me editor in chief of the *Cutlass*, when I was only a junior. I was more confident by then but still had seniors I had to work with in my new leadership capacity. It was trial by fire, but Mrs. D showed up for me every day, helping me learn how to manage as a team, to lead with transparency, honesty, and ethical standards that helped me the rest of my life. She also taught me a lesson I keep with me even now: teamwork is necessary for good results. Today, employees are often siloed, working alone or in a cube where everyone sports earbuds and has no idea what the rest of the staff is doing. When I have had successes, I have always reminded people that it was a team project. You’re only as good as your team whether it’s the high school newspaper or a media empire.

And the workplaces I’ve liked the best are ones that were a little bit like Mrs. DeBord’s journalism class: people throwing ideas out, talking back and forth to work out what was best for the newspaper, having a laugh, collaborating, doing the work. I know that was long before we had social media, endless alerts, Google, smartphones, even earbuds (though we did have Walkmans, we just weren’t allowed to use them in class). Some of my best memories in high school are with Jeff Probart, Suzi Lettunich, and Nicole Lettunich, eating nachos at Taco Time and laughing (semi-hysterically) because we were so tired from production and we weren’t even half done. That moment, that feeling, has been recreated at every publication I’ve ever worked at.

The *Cutlass* staff, at least us editors, would go across the Snake river to the *Argus Observer* in Ontario, Oregon, where we printed our own school newspaper (it was a gift to be at a working newspaper). We would mock it up using a VDT (video display terminal), a manual waxer, and an Exacto knife. At the time it was during a cutting-edge transition as the industry moved away from traditional “hot metal” linotype and letterpress, and towards what would eventually be computerized text entry, phototypesetting, and web offset lithography. (This switch was famously made by the *Guardian* in London in the spring of 1987. They made a coffin with copies of the final edition of the *Guardian* printed using the old system and had a mock funeral with the printing union members acting as pall bearers. Hilarious.)

I like to brag that we got there first because I graduated in 1986 and our local newspapers papers were already on the new system.

Mrs. DeBord and I had a lot in common. We both started writing for a newspaper before we even graduated high school (Mrs. DeBord began writing for *Idaho Free Press* in Nampa, Idaho, where she grew up). We both loved the Shakespeare Festival (she took me to my first one in Boise).

We took different paths to mentoring young people, though I think we both learned along the way that being a steady presence in a teenager’s life is more important than being perfect. I have no doubt that she made a lot of young folks feel special, not just me, and yet at the same time she always pushed us to do better. That’s what I tried to do with my employees and the young people I’ve mentored along the way as well.

We both believed a woman could have it all, including success in a career and in a marriage. A widow at her death, she was married 58 years. I have been married 35 years.

She was always a presence in a room, had an opinion and knew when to share it, and had a wickedly easy laugh that I can still recall. She was making funny social media memes nearly until her death. She would crack wise about how sexy Jamie on *Outlander* is, post flirty messages about ice cream cones, or even make AI videos of her have adventures like sky diving.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PmJv!,w_720,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31cd4820-5d44-450a-9dcd-6b09db5b8bad_1320x2477.jpeg)

Mrs D being funny and flirty on social media. Courtesy Instagram.

But make no mistake. Mrs. DeBord was born a decade before my parents, when girls and women couldn’t even wear pants and college was a hard-fought honor for a woman. She graduated from McPherson College in Kansas and never took “no” for a final answer. She published her first book at 89 or 90. She was on social media until her passing. Sometimes in the media I’m called a trailblazer; Mrs. DeBord was the true trailblazer among us.

Today, I’m the executive director of GALECA: The Society of LGBTQ Entertainment Critics and a commissioner on Cathedral City (California)’s Public Arts Commission. I’ve had 6 of my books published so far. I’ve launched two of my own magazines and served as editor in chief (or editor) of 14 different national magazines. I’ve appeared in dozens of publications from *The New York Times* to *Esquire Russia*. I’ve been given 32 different awards.

None of this would have happened without Mrs. Anne (Keim) DeBord. None.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uhp5!,w_1456,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa56e94f4-692c-45ba-895d-19ab2c056311_3024x4032.png)

Mrs. DeBord wasn’t just a good teacher. She was a lifesaver for some of us. She helped expand minds and launch careers. She never looked down on the girls in my class who only wanted to become mothers and wives (in a rural area, that’s a good chunk of any class though that was my nightmare as a teen).

But for quirky, ambitious girls like me, she helped us—she helped me—see a path that could take my life around the world, to professional highs and lows (from living in a VW bus to speaking as an expert at the United Nations), to forge a career in an industry where even today men hold the majority of executive positions, get the best newsroom beats, and have a majority of bylines. I am often a first (the first female CEO of Pride Media; the first female executive director of GALECA, etc), which is “amazing” for me but depressing for “women overall.”

Both of us have been, in our own ways, fighting for decades for greater parity and equality in the media. My hope is that among the hundreds (thousands?) of kids Mrs. D taught, there is a new generation of girls (and boys and nonbinary folks) who are ready to move society forward. I’m sure there are scores of young women, like I was, that are now even better equipped to reach that goalpost. I have faith that Mrs. DeBord has already inspired her.
