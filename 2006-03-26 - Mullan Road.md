**Mullan Road** was the first [wagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covered_wagon "Covered wagon") [road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road "Road") to cross the [Rocky Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountains "Rocky Mountains") to the [Inland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_Empire_\(Pacific_Northwest\) "Inland Empire (Pacific Northwest)") of the [Pacific Northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest "Pacific Northwest").[^2] It was built by [U.S. Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army "United States Army") troops under the command of Lt. [John Mullan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mullan_\(road_builder\) "John Mullan (road builder)"), between the spring of 1859 and summer 1860. It led from [Fort Benton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Benton,_Montana "Fort Benton, Montana"), which at the time was in the [Dakota Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakota_Territory "Dakota Territory"), then [Idaho Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho_Territory "Idaho Territory") from July 1863, and into [Montana Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Territory "Montana Territory") beginning in May 1864. The road eventually stretched all the way from [Fort Walla Walla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Walla_Walla "Fort Walla Walla"), [Washington Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Territory "Washington Territory"), near the [Columbia River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_River "Columbia River") to the navigational head of the [Missouri River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_River "Missouri River"), which at the time was the farthest inland port in the world). The road previewed the route approximately followed by modern-day [Interstate 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_15_in_Montana "Interstate 15 in Montana") and [Interstate 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_90_in_Idaho "Interstate 90 in Idaho") through present-day Montana, Idaho, and Washington.

Parts of the Mullan Road can still be traveled; one such section is near [Washtucna, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washtucna,_Washington "Washtucna, Washington").

A segment of the Mullan Road in the vicinity of [Benton Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benton_Lake_\(Montana\) "Benton Lake (Montana)") was listed on the [National Register of Historic Places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places "National Register of Historic Places") in 1975,[^3] and the [American Society of Civil Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Society_of_Civil_Engineers "American Society of Civil Engineers") designated it a [National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Civil_Engineering_Landmark "National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark") in 1977. Three segments of the road in Idaho were also listed on the National Register in 1990. In 2009, the Point of Rocks segment in Montana was also listed on the National Register as part of the [Point of Rocks Historic Transportation Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_of_Rocks_Historic_Transportation_Corridor "Point of Rocks Historic Transportation Corridor").

## History

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/Mullan_Pass.jpg/250px-Mullan_Pass.jpg)

Mullan Pass near Helena, Montana

As early as 1852 the US government began to think about building an overland route to the [Pacific Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Ocean "Pacific Ocean") to help settle the area with [pioneers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_pioneer "American pioneer") and eliminate any claims that [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France"), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England") or [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia") had on the disputed territories. Despite the [Lewis and Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corps_of_Discovery "Corps of Discovery") expedition, little detail was known about the country. [Isaac Ingalls Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Ingalls_Stevens "Isaac Ingalls Stevens"), a [West Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy "United States Military Academy") graduate appointed the first governor of the [Washington Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Territory "Washington Territory"), was ordered to [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying "Surveying") a route from the [Missouri River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_River "Missouri River") to the [Columbia River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_River "Columbia River") suitable for building a [railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad "Railroad"). Stevens assumed the task, but also kept in mind that the route should be suitable for a wagon road.

In the spring of 1853, Congress authorized a U.S. Army [expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration "Exploration"), led by Stevens. It was made up of [engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineers "Engineers") and [explorers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration "Exploration"); they organized near [St. Paul, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Paul,_Minnesota "St. Paul, Minnesota"). They were to detail the [geographical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical "Geographical") and [topographical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographical "Topographical") character of the country. Among them was a small, dark-haired young man, Lieutenant John Mullan; just a year out of West Point, he was anxious to prove his mettle as an engineer.

Mullan was placed in charge of surveying, and later improving, a wagon route (now commonly called the Mullan Road) between [Fort Benton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Benton,_Montana "Fort Benton, Montana") ([Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana "Montana")) and [Fort Walla Walla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Walla_Walla "Fort Walla Walla") ([Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_\(U.S._state\) "Washington (U.S. state)")). Lieutenant Mullan, a topographical engineer, began gathering information in 1854. Delayed by the [Yakima War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakima_War "Yakima War") which ended in 1858, construction began in 1859 from [Fort Walla Walla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Walla_Walla "Fort Walla Walla") in what was then [Washington Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Territory "Washington Territory"). Lieutenant Mullan commanded a workforce of more than 200, including civilian workers, [surveyors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying "Surveying"), [engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineers "Engineers"), and [soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldiers "Soldiers") who carved a 25-foot-wide (7.6 m) road across the region.[^4] [^5]

After the difficult project was completed in 1860, [floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floods "Floods") wiped out substantial stretches of the road. It was re-routed in 1861. Floods again damaged the road, and ultimately, no provision for maintenance was provided.[^4] [^5]

Although the road was never heavily used by the military, it was an important conduit for civilian passage, which hastened settling of the northwestern United States. In the first year after completion, it was used by an estimated 20,000 people, 6,000 horses and mules, 5,000 cattle and 83 wagons. The discovery of gold in North Central Idaho in 1860 by Capt. E.D. Pierce contributed to this usage, making Lewiston, Washington Territory, the largest town in the Pacific Northwest and the capital of the new Idaho Territory beginning July 4, 1863. The Mullan Road helped [Walla Walla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walla_Walla,_Washington "Walla Walla, Washington") become the largest [town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town "Town") in [Washington Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Territory "Washington Territory") by 1870, with a population of 1,394. The road continued to serve as an important route until the completion of the [Northern Pacific Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Pacific_Railroad "Northern Pacific Railroad") in 1883 provided faster and more convenient access to the region.[^4]

## Route of the Mullan Road

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/34/Mullan_Road_2_-_Fourth_of_July_Pass_Idaho.jpg/250px-Mullan_Road_2_-_Fourth_of_July_Pass_Idaho.jpg)

A portion of the Mullan Road over Fourth of July Pass is listed on the National Register of Historic Places

From the origin at [Fort Benton, Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Benton,_Montana "Fort Benton, Montana"), the Mullan Road proceeded:

- West from Fort Benton, it passed north of [Great Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Falls,_Montana "Great Falls, Montana")
- Dropping south to cross the [continental divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Divide_of_the_Americas "Continental Divide of the Americas") west of [Helena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helena,_Montana "Helena, Montana") (following a path through [Mullan Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullan_Pass "Mullan Pass") at 5,902 feet (1,799 m), immediately north of that now traveled by [US 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_12#Montana "U.S. Route 12"))
- Just west of [Garrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrison,_Montana "Garrison, Montana"), it joined the route used by the future US 10 (present-day [Interstate 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_90_in_Montana "Interstate 90 in Montana")) (similar to the US 12 transition to join Interstate 90 today)
- Along the [Clark Fork River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark_Fork_River "Clark Fork River") near the [ghost town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_town "Ghost town") of [Bearmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearmouth,_Montana "Bearmouth, Montana"), also along the future US 10
- It remained with the future US 10 route as it passed through [Missoula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missoula,_Montana "Missoula, Montana"), proceeding west through Montana. The Mullan Road through the Missoula Valley, slightly south of the former US 10 and still in use today as [S-263](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Secondary_Highway_263 "Montana Secondary Highway 263"), fostered rapid growth for the burgeoning city, and allowed the U.S. Army to establish [Fort Missoula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Missoula "Fort Missoula") in 1877. One stretch from [St. Regis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Regis,_Montana "St. Regis, Montana") to Henderson north of today's Interstate 90 is still in use as Old Mullan Road and Mullan Gulch Road.
- From the [Taft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taft,_Montana "Taft, Montana") area, it crossed the [Bitterroot Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitterroot_Range "Bitterroot Range") into [Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho "Idaho") over St. Regis Pass (named Sohon Pass by John Mullan) ([47°27′00″N 115°43′16″W / 47.450°N 115.721°W / 47.450; -115.721](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Mullan_Road&params=47.45_N_115.721_W_)) at 4,926 feet (1,501 m), about one point three miles (2 km) west-southwest of [Lookout Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookout_Pass "Lookout Pass"), where [I-90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_90_in_Idaho "Interstate 90 in Idaho") crosses the state line.[^6]
- From the middle of the Idaho crossing, the road deviates from present-day major roads. The Mullan Road went southwesterly in Idaho to pass south of [Lake Coeur d'Alene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Coeur_d'Alene "Lake Coeur d'Alene")
![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Mullan_Military_Road_Monument_Spokane_WA_20230921.jpg/250px-Mullan_Military_Road_Monument_Spokane_WA_20230921.jpg)

Mullan Military Road monument south of Spokane, Washington, at the intersection of E Excelsior Rd and S Monument Ln

- The road then passed into [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_\(state\) "Washington (state)") some distance south of [Spokane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spokane,_Washington "Spokane, Washington")
- From there, the Mullan Road passed through the [Palouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palouse "Palouse") country and then the [scablands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channeled_Scablands "Channeled Scablands") of [eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Washington "Eastern Washington") Washington.
- It passed through [Benge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benge,_Washington "Benge, Washington"), that section of the road was completed May 22, 1861; the wagon ruts were still visible in 2008, just northeast of town at the site of the First Benge School.
- South of Benge, there is a stretch of the former road still labeled 'Mullan Road.' This gravel section travels southwesterly until it meets [State Route 26](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_State_Route_26 "Washington State Route 26") near [Washtucna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washtucna,_Washington "Washtucna, Washington").
- The portion of Mullan Road south of SR 26 in Washington, which follows the course of the [Palouse River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palouse_River "Palouse River") as it descends to the [Snake River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snake_River "Snake River"), has been reverted to grazing and is not accessible to the public.
- After crossing the Snake River near the confluence with the Palouse River, the Mullan Road continues south along the [Fort Walla Walla Fort Colville Military Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Walla_Walla_Fort_Colville_Military_Road "Fort Walla Walla Fort Colville Military Road") [^7] to its terminus at [Fort Walla Walla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Walla_Walla "Fort Walla Walla") near [Walla Walla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walla_Walla,_Washington "Walla Walla, Washington").
