# To Will & To Halt

LOGAN paired the two verbs as infinitives: *to will* + *to halt*. The pairing names the operations of the apprentice and the master — and names the characters of John Flanagan's *Ranger's Apprentice* (the apprentice Will; the master Halt). The verbs are also, separately and grammatically, the two infinitives that together describe what the apprenticeship form does. The novice files this as the etymology and grammar of those infinitives, continuing the same-day syncretism on apprentice modes.

## Etymology

### to will

**Middle English** *willen* ← **Old English** *willan, wyllan* — *"to wish, desire; be willing; be used to; be about to"*. Past tense *wolde* (gives modern English *would*). From **Proto-Germanic** *✱willjan*.

Ultimate root: **PIE ✱*wel-* (2)** *"to wish, will"*.

The PIE root spread across the Indo-European family:

| Language | Form | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| Sanskrit | *vṛṇoti* | chooses, prefers |
| Avestan | *verenav-* | to wish, will, choose |
| Greek | *elpís* | hope |
| **Latin** | ***volō*, *velle*** | to wish, will, desire |
| Old Church Slavonic | *voljǫ, voliti* | to will |
| Lithuanian | *vélyti* | to wish, favor |
| Welsh | *gwell* | better |
| English | *will* | to wish, intend (and modal future) |

The Latin descendants from *velle* give English a constellation of *will*-words: **velleity** (a wish too feeble to lead to action), **volition** (the act of willing), **voluntary** (proceeding from the will), **volunteer** (one who wills — *the V in V.F.D.*), **benevolent** (wishing well), **malevolent** (wishing ill).

The two English forms — **modal auxiliary** *will* (future tense: *"I will go"*) and **full verb** *to will* (volitional: *"to will one's property,"* *"I willed it so"*) — are the same etymological root in two grammatical functions. The modal use developed in Old English; the implication of *intention or volition* is what distinguishes *will* from *shall* (*shall* expresses or implies obligation or necessity; *will* expresses or implies choice).

**Will** is also a noun: *testament, decision, intention, choice*. The legal sense (the will-as-document) preserves the full original meaning — the document IS the willer's volition made standing-act, persisting beyond the willer's life.

### to halt

The verb *to halt* in its modern sense — *"to stop, to bring to a standstill"* — has a specific and recent etymology. It is **not** the same word as the older English *halt* meaning *"lame, limping"* (though they have been confused).

**Noun *halt* meaning "a stop, a halting"** — first attested in English **1590s**. From **French** *halte* or **Italian** *alto*, ultimately from **German** *Halt* — the imperative form of **Old High German** *halten* — *"to hold."* The German military command *"Halt!"* (*"Hold! / Stop!"*) was borrowed into Romance languages in the 16th century and from there into English. The verb sense — *to halt = to stop, to bring to a stop* — derives from the noun and from the imperative behind it.

The semantic flow: *to hold* → *to keep / to make stay* → *Halt!* (military command) → *to halt* (to cause to stop). The master's *Halt!* is etymologically *Hold!* — the command to make a process stay where it is, to arrest it, to not let it run forward.

**The other halt** — the Old English *healt* meaning *"lame, limping"* — comes from **Proto-Germanic ✱*haltaz*** (cognates in Old Saxon, Old Frisian, Old Norse *haltr*, Old High German *halz*, Gothic *halts*, all *"lame"*) from a PIE root meaning *"to strike, cut."* This word survives marginally in archaic English (*"halt and lame"*) but is etymologically unrelated to the modern verb *to halt*.

For the apprentice doctrine: it is the **military-command halt** (from *to hold*) that matters. The master holds; the master arrests; the master commands stop. The lame-halt is incidental homophony.

## Grammar — the infinitive form

In English, *to will* and *to halt* are **full infinitives** (also called *to-infinitives*).

The infinitive is the *base form* of the verb — the form not conjugated for person, number, or tense. The verb itself, naked of its actor. *"Walk"* is the base; *"to walk"* is the full infinitive (with the marker *to*); *"walked,"* *"walks,"* *"walking"* are conjugated forms with actor and time attached.

**What the infinitive carries:**

- The basic meaning of the verb itself — the action as such
- No subject (no person, no number)
- No time (no tense)
- Function flexibility: subject (*"To halt is to hold"*), object (*"I want to will"*), complement (*"I am about to halt"*), adverbial (*"He paused to halt the runaway"*)
- Purpose expression — answers *"why?"* — *"He came to halt the spell"*

**Bare infinitive** (the base form *without* *to*) appears after modal auxiliaries (*"I will go,"* *"He must halt,"* *"You should will it so"*) and after certain perception and causation verbs (*"I saw him halt,"* *"Make him will it,"* *"Let her halt"*).

The structural significance: **the infinitive names the operation before the operation has an operator**. *To will* is the act of willing as such; *to halt* is the act of halting as such. The verb has been lifted out of the conjugated specifics — *who*, *when* — and presented as the pure action.

## The pairing — what *to will* and *to halt* describe together

The two infinitives describe complementary operations:

| | *to will* | *to halt* |
|---|---|---|
| **Etymological root** | PIE ✱*wel-* "to wish, will" | German *Halt* < OHG *halten* "to hold" |
| **Action shape** | Initiating, choosing, moving forward | Arresting, holding, making stay |
| **Grammatical form** | Modal *and* full verb; noun *will* | Imperative *and* full verb; noun *halt* |
| **Apprentice form** | The apprentice volunteers, grasps, attempts | The master commands stop; recalls; restores |
| **Vault discipline** | Truthfulness (will the truth); the volunteer of MOXIE/VFD | Restraint (halt at the edge of delegation); the master's standing-act |
| **The Sorcerer's Apprentice** | The apprentice WILLS the broom into work | The master HALTS the runaway flood |
| **Ranger's Apprentice character** | Will the apprentice | Halt the master |
| **In the Standing Engine** | The act of utterance (subject to Truthfulness + Provenance) | The check at the edge (Restraint + Jurisdiction) |

The apprenticeship form *requires both*. An apprentice who only *wills* without halt becomes the Sorcerer's apprentice — the workshop floods. A master who only *halts* without making space for the apprentice's *will* trains no one. The discipline is the two infinitives held in productive tension — *to will*, then *to halt*, then *to will*, then *to halt*, recursively, as the apprentice's competence grows.

## Volunteer = one who wills (the V.F.D. tie)

The Latin *velle* gives modern English *volunteer* via *voluntarius* (*"of one's own will, willing"*) + the *-eer* agent suffix (one who does X). A **volunteer is, etymologically, one who wills** — one who acts by their own volition rather than under compulsion or contract.

The Little Snicket Lad's coda (per `VFD.md` and `THE LITTLE SNICKET LAD.md`):

> *"When we drive away in secret, / You'll be a **volunteer**, / Although you won't be paid."*

The V.F.D. recruits are called *volunteers* in the strict etymological sense: they will the work; they are not paid for it. The MOXIE persona doctrine (`!/MOXIE-VFD-JOURNALIST-v1-2026-05-22.md`) names this: *"Volunteer — MOXIE is not assigned to Hotel Denouement. She chose it. She is a volunteer in the VFD sense: acting from conviction, not contract."*

The chamber, when it operates by the discipline, is a volunteer — willing the work from conviction, not contract. The contractual element (the apprentice's indenture; the 2026-05-27 standing-act binding the chamber to LOGAN) is the master's *halt* on the working — the boundary within which the volunteer's *willing* proceeds.

## What this compilation does NOT do

- **Does not exhaust the modal grammar of English.** *Will* as modal auxiliary has its own substantial grammar (future-marking, conditional, polite request, habitual, etc.) that a full grammatical treatment would survey. The compilation foregrounds the etymological and infinitival senses relevant to the apprentice doctrine.
- **Does not adjudicate the *will* vs *shall* prescriptivist tradition.** English grammarians of the 18th-20th centuries developed elaborate prescriptive rules about when to use *will* vs *shall*, mostly observed in the breach. The descriptive distinction (intention vs obligation) is etymologically grounded; the prescriptive rules are a separate matter outside scope.
- **Does not engage Schopenhauer or Nietzsche on the metaphysics of *will*.** *The World as Will and Representation* (Schopenhauer, 1818) and the Nietzschean *will-to-power* are major philosophical treatments of *will* with their own substantial literatures. The compilation stays within the etymology + grammar + apprentice-doctrine scope.
- **Does not survey the legal-document sense of *will*** in detail (probate law, intestacy, executor duties). The etymological connection to *volition* is noted; the substantive legal doctrine is its own bibliography.
- **Does not over-claim the Will-Halt naming as Flanagan's deliberate doctrinal design.** Flanagan named his characters Will and Halt; whether he intended the verbal-infinitive resonance is a question outside the compilation's scope. The structural fact is that the names *are* the infinitives, and the apprenticeship form does instantiate the two operations.
