A signature Idaho tradition started because Doug Copsey felt something was missing in Boise.

“I wanted to start a professional theater company in Boise, so that Idaho kids like us didn’t have to go out of state to have that kind of opportunity.”

Copsey had moved back to Boise after graduating from college and was performing with people from Boise State at a venue called “Theatre in a Trunk.”

![Baraboo - Aug/Sep - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/e83c69da-d11f-473d-80af-16d7a1b15bd4.gif)

Baraboo - Aug/Sep - 2:1

“We were just always talking about what we could do to get a venue going in a decent-sized venue to really start theater,” he said.

In the summer of 1976, Copsey said he was walking down Main Street when he saw the brand-new One Capitol Center, built by the Oppenheimer family. At street level was a restaurant called Main Street Bistro, and next to it, a grassy amphitheater.

![](https://boisedev.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/celebrating-50-years-of-the-idaho-shakespeare-festival-none-of-us-really-had-any-idea-it-would-be-that-successful-the-very-first-season-IMG-4629-Large.jpeg)

Lawn outside the former Main Street Bistro/Ray’s Oasis Restaurant (Now Art Haus bar and Kin). Photo: Dani Allsop/BoiseDev

“And I just said, you know, wow, there’s my amphitheater. Now, how do I get to be able to do plays there?”

![Idaho Lottery - 90 MM - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/35da0207-4735-4f00-a726-8f11ae69e570.gif)

Idaho Lottery - 90 MM - 2:1

Copsey reached out to Skip Oppenheimer, whom he went to school with, then his brother Doug, who said he loved the idea, as it would hopefully bring business to Main Street Bistro.

“One Capitol Center and the Oppenheimers were super, super cooperative and supportive of the whole thing,” he said.

Originally, Copsey wanted that first performance to be *Hair*, as it would have coincided with its 10th anniversary.

![Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/6f251bf1-00e6-48d7-8365-570820fe299b.gif)

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

“I thought *Hair* would be a huge hit outdoors in Boise,” he said. “The music was great. It was that time in those days; that music was really popular still, but we couldn’t afford the royalties, so we bagged it over the winter.”

They then went back to the drawing board.

“We were like, ‘What doesn’t have royalties?’ And I think it was Victoria Holloway, who was one of the group, who said, ‘Shakespeare doesn’t have royalties.’ So that became the deal. We picked *A Midsummer Night’s Dream*, and we worked our butts off and got it put up.”

![Build Idaho - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/8679b89c-de2f-4b22-948e-bd39a76c4c7f.png)

Build Idaho - 2:1

The group rehearsed during the day. In 1977, they trained in a horse pasture near an apartment complex where one of the performers lived.

“They went and talked to the owner, and he said, ‘Sure, just don’t bug the horses, and you can go ahead and rehearse.’ So we did.”

On July 27, 1977, *A Midsummer Night’s Dream* premiered at the One Capitol Center amphitheater as part of the “Main Street Bistro Summer Theatre Festival.”

![Build Idaho - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/8679b89c-de2f-4b22-948e-bd39a76c4c7f.png)

Build Idaho - 2:1

Copsey said they had no marketing budget, so they put flyers on cars and made some posters. He expected maybe 50-100 people to show up that first night.

“We were all backstage getting ready, and Victoria came running backstage… and she said, ‘My God, there’s 350 people out there!’”

The show ran for eight nights and drew over 3,500 people.

![ICCU - August 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/1baecb3d-bed1-44ed-bff7-091052e1f1a0.jpg)

ICCU - August 2026 - 2:1

“None of us really had any idea it would be that successful the very first season,” he said. “At the end of the summer, I’ll never forget, everybody’s question was, ‘What are you going to do next year?’ We were like, ‘Oh my God. Hadn’t thought about that.’ We were just too swamped, just trying to get this one pulled off.”

The next year, Main Street Bistro closed, and in came Ray’s Oasis Restaurant, which allowed the “Main Street Bistro Summer Theatre Festival,” then renamed the “Idaho Shakespeare Festival,” to stay for another two years.

“They used to set up outdoor grills and sell burgers and grilled chicken sandwiches and stuff to people who didn’t want to bring up their own picnic,” he said.

![Build Idaho - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/8679b89c-de2f-4b22-948e-bd39a76c4c7f.png)

Build Idaho - 2:1

In 1978, there were 15 performances of *Two Gentlemen of Verona*, drawing in about 4,500 people.

In 1979, the festival featured two plays: *Romeo and Juliet* and *The Merry Wives of Windsor*. In 1980, three shows: *The Comedy of Errors, The Taming of the Shrew*, and *The Merchant of Venice*.

Those two years, they practiced in a vacant lot off Third Street in downtown.

![Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/6f251bf1-00e6-48d7-8365-570820fe299b.gif)

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

“It was hot and dusty and sunny,” he said. “But we got the job done, and it was free.”

Free was a good word for the group.

“We were in the black the first four seasons,” Copsey said.

![ICCU - August 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/1baecb3d-bed1-44ed-bff7-091052e1f1a0.jpg)

ICCU - August 2026 - 2:1

In 1981, things changed. Ray’s Oasis Restaurant had been sold, and the new restaurant moving in, Angell’s Bar and Grill, wanted to renovate. In March, just months before the season was scheduled to start, they told the Idaho Shakespeare Festival they needed to find a new home.

“That cost us,” Copsey said. “That kind of put us in the hole.”

One idea was to move to the Old Idaho Penitentiary. But in May, developers and the Plantation Golf Club donated land at the golf course for free, and they stayed there for three years. Today, Plantation is now the River Club.

![Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/6f251bf1-00e6-48d7-8365-570820fe299b.gif)

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

“Every time we came up against a rock in a hard place, there were people from the community who said, ‘No, no, no, we’re not letting this die. We’re going to keep it going. What do you need?’ And they did.”

In March 1984, with a sense of deja vu, Plantation Golf Club managers told the festival they planned to develop the land they had been using, and that they would need to find a new place to perform starting that summer.

In May, the festival found a home on Parkcenter Boulevard, next to Ore-Ida Foods Corporate offices near Rossi Street along the Boise River.

![Build Idaho - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/8679b89c-de2f-4b22-948e-bd39a76c4c7f.png)

Build Idaho - 2:1

In a May 25, 1984, article from the Idaho Statesman, then-Artistic Director Mark Cuddy said, “This is our second-to-last move.”

“Once Charlie Fee and Mark Hofflund came in 1992 and 1993, respectively, their first move was to start a capital campaign to build the theater where we are now, and it took them several years to get that done. But they did, and with huge help from the community,” Copsey said.

“We looked at 40 different locations all throughout the Treasure Valley,” Hofflund said.

![Baraboo - Aug/Sep - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/e83c69da-d11f-473d-80af-16d7a1b15bd4.gif)

Baraboo - Aug/Sep - 2:1

Ultimately, they narrowed it down to three possible locations: two in Boise and one in Garden City.

In May 1998, 14 years after moving to Park Center, the Festival opened its $3.6 million permanent home at its current location on Warm Springs Avenue, behind the Idaho Department of Parks and Recreation headquarters.

“We were smart in that we had learned from the other four previous locations here in Boise that we had to put it by the river because it was going to be too hot in the middle of summer at some point,” Hofflund said. “We had to put it so that the sun would not blind half the audience while it was setting.”

![Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/6f251bf1-00e6-48d7-8365-570820fe299b.gif)

Regence - 2:1 - Business Grow

That year, they kicked off the season with the same play that started the Festival back in 1977: *A Midsummer Night’s Dream*.

A few times over the years, the land surrounding the festival grounds was considered for development, including in 2019, for a proposal to build 43 homes on 12 acres. [As BoiseDev reported](https://boisedev.com/news/2019/05/10/riparian-habitat-to-replace-boise-sewage-ponds-instead-of-homes/), the festival and neighbors challenged the project, and a deal was made to buy the land and protect it for open space.

In 50 years, they’ve produced more than [120 different productions](https://idahoshakespeare.org/plays-1977-present/). Seasons now last from late May through September, with about 95 performances each year.

![Boise Parks & Rec - Activity Guide August - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/2a472358-d841-43cb-92a9-38111f86cacf.png)

Boise Parks & Rec - Activity Guide August - 2:1

For Sara Bruner, who started her career as a performer with ISF in 1996 and is now the Producing Artistic Director, there’s nothing like performing in the outdoors.

“Starting a play in broad daylight, and you’re on stage, and you can see the audience, and they’re drinking and eating, and then the story gets more… joyful. It gets more sad. It gets more tragic. The evening progresses, and it sort of gets darker and darker, and the world sort of goes from wide and open and broad to just narrowing in and narrowing in. Like, you can’t create that. There’s no amount of lighting effect that can give you that feeling of just watching it grow dark at night with a group of strangers/your fellow audience members for the evening,” she said. “It’s amazing.”

In 2020, the season was canceled due to the COVID-19 pandemic, and had a shortened season in 2021. And in an age where digital technology is part of our everyday lives, theatre aims to bring us back to an art that’s been around for more than 2,500 years.

![Idaho Lottery - 90 MM - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/35da0207-4735-4f00-a726-8f11ae69e570.gif)

Idaho Lottery - 90 MM - 2:1

“We don’t gather as much as we used to, we self-isolate, and we stare at our phones. And a lot of the entertainment we consume is algorithms kicking things and making assumptions about us. And this is just the exact opposite experience.”

Copsey, Bruner and Hofflund all say Boise is special.

“I can’t emphasize how unique it is, having worked at a lot of other places,” Bruner said. “There’s just such a hometown, home team quality to it that makes it feel integral to summers in Treasure Valley, and you feel that when you’re on stage.”

![Idaho Lottery - 90 MM - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/35da0207-4735-4f00-a726-8f11ae69e570.gif)

Idaho Lottery - 90 MM - 2:1

“The biggest thing… is the support that this community gave us, and not by ticket sales, (but) obviously by continuing to show up and loving the plays and really wanting us to do more all the time, but also from a business community standpoint, the the donations of things like lumber and fabric for costumes and and paint and all that stuff for sets and was was phenomenal,” Copsey said.

The festival also participates in a collaboration with the [Great Lakes Theatre in Cleveland, Ohio](https://www.greatlakestheater.org/), and the [Lake Tahoe Shakespeare Festival](https://laketahoeshakespeare.com/).

“We share people, we share artists, and we give them longer contracts because their shows have longer lives,” she said. “But we also share (the) cost of materials, and we move the sets and the costumes and things like that. So it’s a less wasteful way to produce, and it creates more of a life and a career for our company members who work with us.”

![ICCU - August 2026 - 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/1baecb3d-bed1-44ed-bff7-091052e1f1a0.jpg)

ICCU - August 2026 - 2:1

“It’s a unique business model. There’s nothing else like it in the country,” Bruner said.

So far, they have collaborated and shared 50 productions.

In the off-season, ISF takes the company on the road to visit schools across Idaho, Eastern Washington, Eastern Oregon and Western Wyoming through *Shakesperience* and *Idaho Theatre for Youth.* Both give students from kindergarten through 12th grade the opportunity to see theatre without leaving their school.
