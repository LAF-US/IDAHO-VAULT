On Monday, Pope Leo XIV responded to ranting criticisms from President Donald Trump, saying in part that he had no interest in being perceived as a politician.

“The message of the church, my message, the message of the gospel: Blessed are the peacemakers,” Leo told journalists as he arrived in Algeria. “I do not look at my role as being political, a politician.”

Trump attacked Leo late Sunday night as “WEAK on Crime, and terrible for Foreign Policy,” at the beginning of a lengthy post on [Truth Social](https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/116394704213456431).

Soon after, the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops issued a [statement](https://www.usccb.org/news/2026/archbishop-coakleys-response-president-trumps-social-media-post-pope-leo-xiv) saying “Pope Leo is not \[Trump’s\] rival; nor is the Pope a politician.” Leo said on Monday: “I don't want to get into a debate with him. I don’t think that the message of the Gospel is meant to be abused in the way that some people are doing.”

But experts in Catholic theology and international politics told *Sojourners*, it would be a mistake to interpret the pope or bishops as suggesting that the gospel or his papacy are apolitical. That would contradict his clear messaging against [war](https://sojo.net/articles/news/pope-leo-jesus-rejects-prayers-leaders-who-wage-war), [poverty](https://www.npr.org/2025/10/09/nx-s1-5563956/pope-leo-xiv-is-drawing-the-worlds-attention-to-poverty), [immigration](https://sojo.net/articles/news/how-pope-leo-challenging-american-catholics-immigration), and [other issues](https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/pope-leo-xiv-very-disappointed-gov-pritzker-medical-aid-dying-bill/). Instead, they said Leo’s comments should be understood as an attempt to affect politics and foster dialogue while avoiding partisanship.

#### Catholic social teaching’s approach to politics

[Anna Rowlands](https://www.ncronline.org/opinion/ncr-voices/rowlands-new-book-brings-light-catholic-social-teaching), professor at Durham University in England and author of [*Towards a Politics of Communion: Catholic Social Teaching in Dark Times*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/towards-a-politics-of-communion-9780567219084/), said that to Pope Leo—and his predecessors Popes Francis and Benedict XVI—politics is understood as the way humans negotiate common life together. She said Francis talked about politics as “an extension of the idea of *caritas*, of charity extended to institutions,” and Benedict spoke of “political love” as the “care for institutions.”

“The Catholic Social Teaching tradition that Pope Leo belongs to would frame this as: No, he’s not a politician; yes, the gospel is political,” she told *Sojourners*. “Pope Leo will have the highest respect for the office that President Trump holds. That’s what leads him to be super critical of when the highest calling of that office appears not to be being lived out.”

“The pope has the ability to speak to the better side of people’s nature, to remind them that it's always possible to take a pathway that makes the first step towards peace, even right when you're on the brink—even the brink of potential nuclear conflict,” she said.

#### Leo as international figure

Christopher White, an associate director and senior fellow at Georgetown University and author of [***Pope Leo XIV: Inside the Conclave and the Dawn of a New Papacy***](https://store.loyolapress.com/pope-leo-xiv), did point to some differences in temperament and approach between Leo and his predecessors. Leo belongs to the [Order of St. Augustine](https://www.midwestaugustinians.org/pope-leo-xiv), which heavily influences his approach to leadership and governance.

“The Augustinian Order is run incredibly collaboratively,” White said. “No decisions are really taken alone. They’re taken after great consultation … Pope Leo really believes people should be talking to each other.”

Leo has been “deeply committed” to multilateralism and diplomacy—opposites of authoritarianism, White said. In his first year as pope, Leo has adjusted to his role as an international leader and, internally tried to “turn down the temperature on hot-button issues inside of the Catholic Church,” White said. For example, he has sought to [heal divisions](https://www.americamagazine.org/news/2026/03/26/pope-leo-latin-mass-french-bishops-inclusion/#:~:text=In%20a%20meeting%20with%20the,Joseph%20Shaw%2C%20president%20of%20FIUV.) between the Vatican and Catholics who want to celebrate Mass in Latin. While “fully on board” with Francis’ agenda, Leo has tried to strike a conciliatory tone and spend the majority of his time listening and talking to stakeholders within the church, White said.

That may explain why even some of Trump’s more vocal supporters in the church, such as Bishop Robert E. Barron of Winona-Rochester, Minn., have called Trump’s comments “entirely inappropriate and disrespectful.”

“I am very grateful for the many ways that the Trump administration has reached out to Catholics and other people of faith. It has been a high honor to serve on the Religious Liberty Commission. No President in my lifetime has shown a greater dedication to defending our first liberty,” Barron [wrote](https://x.com/BishopBarron/status/2043646792890261616) on X. “All that said, I think the President owes the Pope an apology.

#### An American pope from Latin America

While those in the U.S. know him as the pope from Chicago, Peruvians also celebrate Leo as one of their own. Leo spent nearly 30 years working in Peru, from 1985–2014. Peru’s president described Leo as Peruvian “by choice and conviction,” and the [BBC reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cewdl4e57v7o) that Leo had called Peru “ *mi segunda patria* ”—“my second homeland.”

[Peru](https://sojo.net/magazine/november-1991/perus-trial) was one of the places where liberation theology was [articulated and developed](https://sojo.net/articles/opinion/gustavo-guti-rrez-articulated-collaborative-liberating-theology) in the 1960s and ’70s before Leo arrived. The themes of poverty, anticolonialism, and anti-imperialism were richly established by the time Leo made Peru his home, Jorge Aquino, a theology and religious studies professor at the University of San Francisco, told *Sojourners*.

While in Algeria this week, Leo criticized the “neocolonial” world powers that violate international law.

“The future belongs \[to\] those who do not allow themselves to be blinded by power or wealth,” Leo said. “Africa knows all too well that people and organizations that dominate others destroy the world.”

Aquino said that by naming colonialism, Leo is standing squarely in the theology that criticizes politics that polarize wealth and poverty, creating so-called “first world” and “third world” countries—as early liberation theologians put it. It’s also a criticism of [imperialism](https://sojo.net/articles/news/after-maduro-venezuelan-christians-pray-end-us-imperialism), and the long U.S. policy of interfering in Latin American independence—a pattern [revitalized](https://sojo.net/articles/news/after-maduro-venezuelan-christians-pray-end-us-imperialism) under the Trump administration.

“The poverty of Latin America is well understood as substantially inflicted by first-world economic investment in the Global South, but also political interference,” he said.

Black Moses Rankins, the executive director of [Call to Action](https://www.cta-usa.org/staff), a progressive Catholic organizing group, told *Sojourners* he sometimes wishes the pope could more aggressively combat the president.

“I wish he could say, ‘As a U.S. citizen and pope of the church, president, you suck at what you’re doing right now. This war kills people and harms people,’” Rankins said. “\[But\] his role is bigger … He is to be like Christ. If you go in the scriptures and look at conversations Jesus had with other leaders like what we’re dealing with today … you never hear a story where Jesus went directly for someone’s head.”

Leo has to follow in a biblical tradition, Rankins said.

“Like it or not, liberal or not liberal, that’s what Christ would say. Christ wouldn’t say ‘Donald Trump, you’re doing a bad thing.’ He’d say, ‘Leaders who lead with love are more effective.’ Leo has to be Jesus, at the end of the day.”

Still, Rankins said the pope’s willingness to speak into American politics should cause an inflection point for American Catholics.

“They have to ask themselves if they’re followers of Christ or followers of Trump,” he said.
