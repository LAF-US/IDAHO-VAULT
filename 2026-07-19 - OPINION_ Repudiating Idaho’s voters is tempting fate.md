# OPINION: Repudiating Idaho’s voters is tempting fate

## Editorial: The Tribune’s Opinion

[

![Marty Trillhaase avatar](https://public-assets-prod.pubgen.ai/brand_4fc3eb37-4c8a-4951-9324-365f6e4b53fd/asset_1d1488d2-3634-415b-a713-05454f3070fe.jpg?w=3840)

Marty Trillhaase

](https://www.lmtribune.com/writers/marty-trillhaase)

It’s difficult to believe that after enduring four years of Republican extremism on the issue of women’s health, [[Idaho]] voters are about to fall for the GOP’s hysterical campaign against Proposition 1.

Prop 1 is the recently qualified ballot measure, the Reproductive Freedom and Privacy Act, that would, with voter approval in the fall, restore to Idaho women the reproductive rights Idaho’s GOP-led Legislature took from them after the U.S. Supreme Court reversed the half-century precedent of Roe v. Wade.

Today, Idaho women and their health care providers live under one of the most draconian anti-abortion bans in the United States. There is no health exception to protect a woman undergoing a crisis pregnancy from severe and possibly permanent injury.

To intervene, a physician would risk a prison sentence and the loss of professional licensing. For women, that has meant undue suffering and/or the need to find care in a more compassionate state. For the physicians, it has often meant relocating away from Idaho. A third of the state’s obstetricians and more than half of its maternal-fetal medicine specialists have left the Gem State.

The effectiveness of Idaho’s exceptions for victims of rape and incest is in doubt, since it requires women to file a police report. Many, if not most, victims do not go to the authorities.

Even with such an unpopular law — a recent Boise State University Public Policy Survey suggests Prop 1 would clear the 60% threshold — Republican lawmakers have turned a deaf ear to appeals for compromise.

So voters are about to take matters into their own hands through an initiative that would simply restore the law that was in place for 50 years:

Advertisement

Abortions would be legal until the point of fetal viability, generally about 22 to 24 weeks.

After that, abortions would be authorized only in cases of medical emergencies or a fatal fetal diagnosis.

Rather than making a late-stage pivot toward reaching a moderate compromise — such as promoting a health exception — state GOP Chairperson Dorothy Moon and her colleagues would rather engage in a campaign of histrionics and flat out distortions.

Contrary to what Moon and her colleagues say, this would not permit elective late-term abortions. It would not repeal the parental consent laws that were in place in Idaho at the time Roe was the law of the land. It certainly will not reverse Idaho’s ban on transgender care.

If any of that were true, why did Idaho Attorney General Raul Labrador — one of the most strident supporters of Idaho’s current anti-abortion law — not include those points in the ballot language his office drafted?

In any event, adding language about parental consent or transgender care would have run afoul of Idaho single-topic standards for initiatives and proposed legislation.

All of which leads to a suspicion: Moon’s claims are not meant to sway the voters as much as the members of her party who will serve in the Legislature next year.

And for them, the GOP has this message:

“The Idaho Republican convention affirms the authority of the Idaho Legislature to amend or repeal voter-approved ballot initiatives through the exercise of its legislative power, and calls upon its elected representatives to exercise such authority should the proposed reproductive rights ballot initiative be approved by the voters, in order to restore protections for the unborn and to preserve the Legislature’s proper role in setting public policy on matters of life, family, and parental rights. ...”

Already, 17 Republican legislative incumbents and candidates — including Sen. Cindy Carlson, R-Riggins, Rep. Kyle Harris, R-Lewison, and House candidate Colton Bennett, of Troy — have pledged to do just that.

Apparently, they believe themselves impervious to the will of the voters — and because they are from strong Republican districts, they might have reason to believe that.

Here’s what they may not know: Their election would not be the last word. Voters could retaliate against faithless legislators. It’s happened before. After two Idaho lawmakers — Sen. W. Fisher Ellsworth and Rep. Aden Hyde, both R-Idaho Falls — refused to respect a voter-approved initiative, eastern Idaho voters terminated their political careers in a successful 1971 recall election.

By the way, recall of elected officials is a sibling of the initiative process. Both came out of the same progressive movement of the early 1900s.

To start a recall against either Carlson or Harris, you would need to secure the signatures of 20% of the registered voters in Legislative District 7 — or about 6,962. To force a recall against Bennett, you would need the signatures of about 7,481 registered voters from District 6.

From there, it becomes more difficult. To protect themselves against recall, lawmakers have instituted this requirement: In the subsequent election — which would be held in May or November of next year — the recall movement needs to win not only a majority but also a number of votes equal to what the legislators received in their previous election. That’s a tough thing to pull off in a low-turnout special election.

So here’s where the voting gets strategic. If you plan to support Prop 1, vote for the candidates opposing Carlson, Harris and Bennett — Democrats Patrice Yeatter, of Lewiston, Larry Nostrant, of Lewiston and Trish Carter-Goodheart, of Lapwai, respectively.

Odds are they won’t prevail in what appear to solidly Republican districts. But if these opponents can whittle the Republicans’ margins low enough in November, Carlson, Harris and Bennett would be vulnerable to a well-run recall effort.

So they would have to reconsider repudiating the will of the voters next January or expose themselves to an outraged electorate that could end their political careers later in the year.

Both the voters and the candidates should think carefully about that. — M.T.
