As [[Idaho]]’s harmful algal bloom season continues, state health officials are warning people to stay out of the water at Brownlee Reservoir and Hells Canyon Reservoir after recent samples showed high amounts of toxin-producing cyanobacteria.

The advisory follows a July 7 announcement of a health advisory for Crane Creek Reservoir in Washington County south of Cambridge.

The Idaho Department of Health and Welfare works with Southwest District Health and the Idaho Department of Environmental Quality to identify, respond to and monitor cyanobacteria harmful algal blooms, also known as cyanoHABs. Officials said the toxins produced by some cyanobacteria, called cyanotoxins, can be harmful to people, pets and livestock.

While the advisories are in effect at Brownlee, Hells Canyon and Crane Creek reservoirs, officials recommend avoiding swimming, wading or other contact with the water and taking extra care to ensure children do not drink the water or get it on them. People are also urged to keep pets and livestock from drinking the water or going into it. If animals have contact with the water, officials recommend cleaning their skin, hide or fur with clean water right away.

Health officials also warned people not to drink or cook with the water, noting that boiling or filtering does not remove the toxins and can increase the risk of becoming sick. Anyone handling fish or objects from the water should wash hands thoroughly in clean water.

Officials said people who touch or swim in affected water or breathe in water droplets could experience symptoms including a rash, hives, red eyes, wheezing, coughing or shortness of breath. Swallowing the water could lead to stomach pain, diarrhea or vomiting. Other possible symptoms include headache, muscle weakness or dizziness. Officials warned that liver damage could cause yellowing skin and dark urine.

Anyone who thinks they might be sick from cyanotoxin exposure should consult a health care provider or call the poison center at 1-800-222-1222. Officials also asked people to notify algae@deq.idaho.gov and to monitor media reports and the Department of Health and Welfare website for health advisories.

Pets, livestock and wildlife can get sick or die within minutes to days after cyanotoxin exposure, officials said. Dogs may become sick before humans because they are more likely to swim in or drink contaminated water or lick bloom material off their fur. Officials recommend immediately washing animals with clean water if they have been in the water to prevent them from licking cyanobacteria off their bodies, and seeking veterinary care immediately if an animal seems sick after going in or drinking the water.
