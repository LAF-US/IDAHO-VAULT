If you’ve ever wanted to see a film more than a hundred years in the making, you’ll have a chance to do so to close out the month at the third annual Kenworthy Silent Film Festival.

Running from Sept. 25-28 at the Kenworthy Performing Arts Centre in Moscow, the event sees the organization showcasing its first major restoration project. On Friday and Saturday, the festival will be holding two screenings of a restored version of the 1919 silent film *Told in the Hills*, a Western romance featuring over 100 Nez Perce actors and creative collaborators. This version of the film is the result of multiple creative voices utilizing the surviving 25 minutes of film footage, 200 archival photos shot on the set, and a live performance of a new score by acclaimed Diné (Navajo) composer Connor Chee. However, while the images being shown are more than a century old, what audiences will experience came together in a much quicker time frame than that.

Discussing his process, Chee says he was approached about being part of the film at the end of 2024, was working from a only rough cut of the film, and had to develop new tools as an artist.

“It was a little challenging,” Chee says. “I knew the story, I knew basically what was going to be there, but I wasn’t exactly sure what the final product would be. I haven’t seen that, and I haven’t heard the music I’ve written performed live yet.”

This was quite different for the composer, as he says he usually works on film compositions from a more complete cut. Citing grant and funding restrictions, Chee says it all came together on a “really tight timeline,” with him sending the final composition to the orchestra earlier this month.

“I just finished writing the score about a week ago. They’ve only had the score since the middle of last week. I think that we’ll be in communication sometime soon before the premiere, but I tried to be really diligent in my scoring and the notes for the performers,” Chee explains. “I also left a lot of it up to the performer and the conductor. The nature of how it came together meant what I ended up doing was writing 17 different pieces that were modular, so they could be extended to several minutes or as short as 10 or 15 seconds, depending on what is needed.”

This flexibility was all part of the collaborative nature of the project, with Chee specifically expressing appreciation for its inclusion of the Nez Perce and him as an artist. On Thursday, Sept. 25, there will be a panel with Nez Perce cultural historian Nakia Williamson-Cloud, BSU archivists Gwyn Hervochon and Alessandro Meregaglia, and editor Tom Frank to set the table for the screenings over the weekend. The festival will also feature a selection of shorts directed by the late Nell Shipman with scores by composers Isabel Martin,

Mallory Hunt and Samuel Cooper screened on Sunday.

When it comes to *Told in the Hills*, Frank says that working on the film helped put things in perspective for him about history, as he’d never edited anything quite like this before. He’s still working on finalizing what the film will look like for a planned physical release as well.

“I have edited things before that relied on archival photography with the Ken Burns effects of moving photos, but this is uniquely different from anything I’ve ever worked on,” Frank says, discussing how he was constantly working to find the balance between making the film engaging to watch while maintaining a fidelity to the original footage and history. “ Now that we have so many AI tools, if I have a client that sends me a photo and says, ‘Please edit this, change that,’ the industry standard more and more is to use the AI tools that are available to do that kind of work. In this case, it is still this constant sensitive balance of wanting to make sure that we’re bringing out as much level of detail as possible, but not wanting to add any modern effects.”

Frank says that he used Adobe Photoshop for as much manual retouching as possible, but that the application does currently use “some amount of AI” with things like the repair brush tool. However, before you get your pitchforks out to prevent a nightmarish *The* *Wizard of Oz* at Sphere atrocity, Frank distinguished his tools from what is known as generative AI (i.e., the creation of new images). He emphasizes there is no generative AI used to alter the footage for its final cut. There was an initial early mockup where some generative AI was experimented with, but he says they “threw that whole approach out” due to the “weird uncanny valley thing that happens where you get melted faces and third hands,” or what has become known as “AI slop.”

This was echoed by Kenworthy Executive Director Colin Mannex, who says it was important that the integrity of the film’s original footage not be compromised.

“There’s in no way going to be any misrepresentation of the images or mangling of the film reels that survived,” Mannex says. “That’s a guiding principle because we have an obligation to the archives and the librarians who have helped us along the way to deliver the materials without any embellishment to the audience.”
