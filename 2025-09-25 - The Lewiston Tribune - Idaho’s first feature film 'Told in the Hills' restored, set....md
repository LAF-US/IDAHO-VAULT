# Idaho’s first feature film 'Told in the Hills' restored, set to premiere in Moscow

## Historic film premieres in restored format at Kenworthy’s Silent Film Festival in Moscow

Lauren Paterson for Inland 360

The first feature film made in Idaho was shot more than a century ago. But a new version of it is scheduled to premiere again, in part because of a determined professor and a team of archivists, historians and artists.

“Told in the Hills” is a Western-romance that was filmed in Kamiah, on the Nez Perce Indian Reservation.

“It was made in 1919, and it was an unusual film, even for early cinematic standards, because of the unique and unprecedented collaboration with the Nez Perce Tribe,” said Colin Mannex, executive director of the Kenworthy Performing Arts Centre in Moscow.

The restored film premieres Friday and screens again Saturday at the Kenworthy as part of the theater’s third annual Silent Film Festival, which gets underway tonight with a panel discussion featuring Nez Perce cultural historian Nakia Williamson-Cloud, Boise State University archivists Gwyn Hervochon and Alessandro Meregaglia, and film editor Tom Frank.

The silent film’s story follows Jack Stuart, played by Robert Warwick, a man from a wealthy family who tries to escape family drama by heading West. But Stuart gets himself into trouble.

More than a hundred members of the Nez Perce Tribe were invited to participate in the filming.

“ They presented a lot of authentic cultural regalia as a part of the production,” Mannex said.

The Nez Perce tribal members portray the Kootenai tribe in the film, because the story takes place in rural Montana, despite being filmed in Idaho.

After a young Kootenai chieftain is accidentally killed when he tries to bring a message of goodwill to the U.S. cavalry, Stuart is unjustly imprisoned, and the story follows his escape.

The film went missing over the decades, but at an event last year, Mannex got a tip about Tom Trusky, a BSU professor who, in the 1980s, researched early films made in Idaho.

“He was able to identify this partial print that existed in the Soviet archive,” Mannex said.

Films like “Told in the Hills” were printed on film and mailed around the world in large tins for showings in different theaters, often played again and again.

“They experienced some wear and tear,” said Hervochon, an archivist, associate professor and librarian with BSU.

The Soviet Union was one of the last stops for films that crossed the sea and circulated through Europe and beyond, she said. Some of those films ended up staying in the Soviet Union and getting locked away.

Trusky wrote to the Russians asking about the film. To sweeten the deal, he enclosed the famed 1952 portrait of Marilyn Monroe wearing an Idaho potato sack (pictured at left).

The Russians wrote back, telling him they had two reels of the “Told in the Hills” film that survived over the years.

The 35mm film print was sent back to BSU in 1987, Hervochon said.

This past spring, Mannex went to Boise and was able to find most of it in the university archives, with some help from Hervochon.

“Gwyn helped me identify everything that I needed, including the original shooting script,” he said.

Mannex had the film digitized to 4K footage, including around five minutes that weren’t part of the original movie. Then he looped in his friend Tom Frank, a producer living in Los Angeles, to edit the project.

Because only a third of the film was found, Frank had to work on how to build a narrative from the partially reconstructed video and photograph stills.

“The way that it relies on the inner titles and the music to do so much more of the lifting than what we’re used to by today’s standards of storytelling had a lot of fun challenges,” he said.

Using the original script for guidance, Frank said he combined newer footage and used a special filter to match the black and white images.

As with many silent films, music will play throughout the picture. The score for the new version was created by Diné composer Connor Chee.

It was a challenge to create a soundtrack, Chee said, because Frank was still working on the film.

“ What I ended up doing was writing about 17 different pieces, 17 cues, that had a variety of emotions that could match what I’d seen on the screen in this rough cut,” he said.

The soundtrack will be performed live during this weekend’s showings of the restored film.

Being a member of the Navajo Nation, Chee said he doesn’t have to explicitly try to impart an Indigenous feel, because it’s always woven into his work. But he said he approached the music with a historical lens.

“I wanted the melodrama to match sort of the time period in that sense, of what you’re seeing on screen,” he said.

“ This is just the first stage of the process,” Mannex said. “Ultimately, we’ll have a Blu-Ray DVD release, and we’d like to be able to tour the film throughout Idaho, wherever there’s interest in showing it.”
