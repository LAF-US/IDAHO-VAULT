## Deep Dive: Widening Idaho 55 and replacing Rainbow Bridge will cost more than $100 million. Why not bypass the area?

![](https://valleylookout.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2025/05/valleylookout.com-weekday-delays-expected-as-rainbow-bridge-work-resumes-monday-img-1149.jpeg)

The Rainbow Bridge spans the North Fork of the Payette River about 2.5 miles north of Smiths Ferry on Idaho 55. Photo: Drew Dodson/Valley Lookout

The Idaho Transportation Department — and Idaho’s drivers — have been contending with construction delays, [rockslides, and escalating costs to widen Idaho 55 in Valley County](https://boisedev.com/news/members/idaho-55-slides-itd/) for years.

The winding two-lane highway, known for its eye-catching views as it wraps through the canyon between Cougar Mountain Lodge in Smiths Ferry and Round Valley, has been a known danger zone for 50 years. The combination of a narrow roadway between the rocky canyon wall and a steep drop-off into the North Fork Payette River made it one of the most dangerous roads in the Gem State.

But, was there another way?

![Western States - Lookout creative - 4:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/115c0cfa-e547-4a5c-8f4e-7ea7e16f8bde.png)

Western States - Lookout creative - 4:1

Since 1974, ITD has earmarked this section of roadway for improvements. It languished on the state’s to-do list until 1999, when $18 million in federal funding became available to improve highway safety. That launched a multi-year effort to study whether the state should improve the existing roadway or reroute the highway entirely out of the canyon.

ITD began an analysis in 2000 that identified 25 alternatives for making the highway safer. This list was eventually narrowed to 11 ideas, including pitches to widen Highway 55 at different speeds, rebuild the historic Rainbow Bridge, and construct routes bypassing the canyon to the east or west. It was then narrowed to eight possible routes, including an option to maintain the status quo.

<iframe width="100%" height="100%" src="https://www.google.com/maps/embed/v1/place?key=AIzaSyBAM2o7PiQqwk15LC1XRH2e_KJ-jUa7KYk&amp;zoom=11&amp;maptype=roadmap&amp;q=Rainbow+Bridge%2C+Idaho" title="Google map of Rainbow Bridge, Idaho"></iframe>

ITD studied the problem for eight years before deciding to improve the existing highway rather than reroute, according to one of multiple study reports obtained by Valley Lookout. ITD arrived at this conclusion and stuck to it for a number of reasons, including the steep grades of the Forest Service roads through mountains outside of the canyon, sensitive environmental areas, and buried archeological sites from Indigenous people pre-dating western settlers arriving in the area.

![Valley Lookout house - generic](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/82689d5d-b8b7-4c7f-9d4c-b24100ffd632.gif)

Valley Lookout house - generic

ITD District 3 Engineer Jason Brinkman said building a highway through the mountains on either side of the North Fork would require intensive excavation into mountainsides and filling valleys. The project would take multiple years and likely cost several hundred million dollars, rather than the estimated $100 million to $150 million the state is looking at today.

“You move a lot more of the mountain in order to build such a route and a lot of the cost of construction has to do with that kind of earth moving,” he said. “A significant amount of additional earth moving, even if we’re only doing double, would mean double the cost. In this case, it’s probably up to several times earth-moving that would be required to build such a route. Whatever the cost of improving an existing route would be, it’s times a significant factor for the cost of a different route.”

## Steep slopes, archeological digs and wildlife all impact project

![](https://valleylookout.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/03/valleylookout.com-deep-dive-widening-idaho-55-and-replacing-the-rainbow-bridge-will-cost-more-than-100-million-why-not-bypass-the-area-image-14-large.jpeg)

It wasn’t just one factor that made bypassing the canyon difficult.

One of the alignments proposed taking cars to the east, generally following the path of an existing local gravel road. According to an analysis, that idea would entirely bury one of Round Valley’s largest and most significant archeological sites. This would require a large-scale excavation of the site, estimated to cost up to $1.25 million in 2001 dollars.

This one alternative alone, known as E2N in a 2001 draft study, would have required the construction of large embankments and extensive rock and soil cuts and fills to build the highway that would “irreversibly affect” Round Valley’s current scenic views. It would also have impacted wildlife movement through the area and local springs, some of which serve as drinking water sources for Round Valley residents.

Brinkman said all of the routes considered going east of the canyon on the existing road through the area from Smiths Ferry would have had to contend with steep slopes. The typical grade of a highway built by ITD is 4%, with rare exceptions such as Lewiston Hill, Freezeout Hill, and White Bird Grade, which reach 7%. The dirt roads bypassing the canyon have grades exceeding 10%.

What seems like a simple solution on the map, Brinkman said, is made more complicated by the steep slopes, which would require building several more miles of highway than the existing road.

“In order to build a road over the hill there, even though it seems like an easy connection because it’s an existing road, what a highway would need for alignment would need to be more gradual than that,” Brinkman said. “To make it more gradual, you have to take a longer path. You can’t cut directly to the straightest line because the grades would be too steep. You’re not going to cut a 500-foot deep trench through the mountain.”

Any reroute would also require a new bridge to cross the North Fork. Although the alternative bridges wouldn’t be as high above the river, the project would still be costly because the river is wider near Smiths Ferry. There would also be impacts on Round Valley Creek at the northern end of a new highway route. And in the meantime, ITD would need to continue maintaining the existing Idaho 55 and [replace the Rainbow Bridge anyway](https://valleylookout.com/2025/10/30/itd-growingly-concerned-about-future-of-historic-idaho-55-bridge-as-replacement-designs-narrowed/?preview=true&_thumbnail_id=5215) due the number of recreational areas and homes along the existing route that would require the canyon to remain passable.

## What about the current construction challenges?

Improving Highway 55 without realigning it hasn’t been a walk in the park either.

The project’s first phase, which began in 2020, started with widening the highway and softening sharp curves from Smiths Ferry to just south of the Rainbow Bridge. It was estimated to cost $18.9 million in September of 2020, but [multiple landslides at the project site that required redesigning the project and additional measures to stabilize the slopes](https://boisedev.com/news/members/idaho-55-slides-itd/) pushed the price tag up over [$62 million.](https://boisedev.com/news/2022/09/19/idaho55-over-budget/)

That cost doesn’t cover the state’s plans to replace the [Rainbow Bridge for another $60 million](https://valleylookout.com/2025/10/30/itd-growingly-concerned-about-future-of-historic-idaho-55-bridge-as-replacement-designs-narrowed/?preview=true&_thumbnail_id=5215) in 2028, nor a third phase of improvements on the north side of the bridge later, which doesn’t yet have a final design or cost estimate.

Valley Lookout asked Brinkman whether the challenges encountered during the first phase of the Smiths Ferry project led ITD to reconsider rerouting the highway. He said the project’s challenges with unstable slopes due to the underlying geology and the need to redesign portions of the project would multiply if the highway were rerouted. This is because the geology of the rocks with pockets of instability persists throughout the area, not just in the canyon where ITD is building on its current project.

“Those same issues would exist on the alternative route and proportionate to the amount of mountain you touch,” Brinkman said. “If we experience those problems or challenges, in a mile of route, imagine what six or eight miles of route might uncover. We would anticipate it to be a lot more of the same.”

![Highway 55 Smith's Ferry](https://valleylookout.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2024/11/Hwy55_SmithFerry_4-1500x783-1.jpg)

Photo courtesy Idaho Transportation Dept.

The initial plan for the Smiths Ferry project at the end of the 2008 study was to [flatten the](https://boisedev.com/news/2022/09/19/idaho55-over-budget/) [highway curves enough to allow speeds up to 55 miles per hour,](https://boisedev.com/news/2022/09/19/idaho55-over-budget/) consistent with other segments of the highway, requiring deeper cuts into the hillsides. This was changed in 2015 after ITD evaluated the project’s budget, and the design speed was reduced to 45 miles per hour.

![St. Luke's Health Plan - 2026 2:1](https://static-v2.boisedev.com/assets/3cffa70b-bd30-43bc-8a9e-62008bbc7dff.png)

St. Luke's Health Plan - 2026 2:1

The northernmost section of the project, where the landslides later occurred, was reduced to 40 miles per hour in 2016.

Valley Lookout asked Brinkman if ITD had factored in the delays and project costs on the existing highway in decision-making on whether the canyon should be bypassed. He said there is an assumption that building an alternative route wouldn’t affect traffic on the current highway, but that doesn’t account for the need to truck in all construction materials to the site on existing Idaho 55. It also doesn’t factor in the multiple years of closures and the costs of chip sealing, which would still impact drivers.

“We take that into account, but there’s no balance sheet because it’s hard to quantify,” he said.
