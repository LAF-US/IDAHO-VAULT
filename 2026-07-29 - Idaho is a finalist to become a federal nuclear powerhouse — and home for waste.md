Idaho is on the shortlist to become the center of America’s nuclear energy industry — and a warehouse for much of its radioactive waste.

The [Department of Energy announced Tuesday](https://www.energy.gov/articles/nuclear-lifecycle-innovation-campuses-contenders-announced) that Idaho is one of five finalists to host a proposed Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus, part of an aggressive federal push to revive domestic nuclear energy production. The hubs aim to “support activities across the entire nuclear fuel cycle,” according to a DOE press release. That means fabricating, enriching and, when possible, reprocessing nuclear fuel before permanently storing spent material on site — a decades-long hang-up for nuclear watchdogs and environmental advocates across Southern Idaho.

In return, the federal government says that the partnership could attract up to $50 billion in private capital investment and 25,000 jobs, according to the Department of Energy. The department also estimated that campuses could raise $10 billion in state and local tax revenue through the deal; Idaho as a state collected less than $10 billion total across all taxes last year, [according to data from the state tax commission.](https://tax.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/reports/EPB00033/EPB00033_07-10-2025.pdf)

Idaho, Utah, Tennessee, Oklahoma and Louisiana made the final round, beating out 21 other states that applied. [All five signed memorandums of understanding with the Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/ne/nuclear-lifecycle-innovation-campuses) “to continue exploring the opportunity to become host sites,” the department stated.

The Department of Energy is eyeing three campuses, Cally Younger, administrator of the [Idaho Office of Species, Mineral and Energy Coordination,](https://oemr.idaho.gov/) told the Idaho Statesman in a series of interviews. Each state would take one-third of the country’s stockpile of spent nuclear fuel, which is currently held on site at power plants and research facilities while the federal government searches for permanent storage.

[Yucca Mountain near Las Vegas](https://republicans-energycommerce.house.gov/yucca-mountain) was tapped to be that storage center in 1987, though it was defunded and shuttered in 2010 before ever receiving a shipment. Yucca Mountain stalled after public outrage spurred Nevada officials — including a clutch of congressmen — to oppose the project.

[Idaho’s filing for the Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus](https://nuclear.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/FINAL_DOE-NLIC-Response_2026-04-13.pdf) included a letter of support signed by three of the state’s four-member congressional delegation. Rep. Russ Fulcher, who sits on the House Committee on Energy and Commerce, did not sign. A bipartisan coalition of 20 state senators and 46 state representatives also sent a letter expressing “strong support” for the idea. So did the commissioners of Butte County, which includes the [Idaho National Laboratory](https://inl.gov/), a Department of Energy research mecca in the high desert west of Idaho Falls.

“The DOE is looking to replace Yucca Mountain,” Younger told the Statesman on Friday, but whether and how much they’re willing to pay states to take the waste “remains an open question.”

The campus does not come with a grant, and to date Congress hasn’t appropriated any public money to fund it, Younger said. Idaho was unlikely to accept a deal that only brought jobs and tax revenue if it meant storing spent fuel in perpetuity, she added.

“That’s great, and that’s what got us to apply,” she said of the economic boost on Tuesday, “but if we’re really talking about permanent storage, they’re going to have to pay.”

### ‘Home of America’s nuclear renaissance’

![The Radioactive Waste Management Complex at the Idaho National Laboratory site outside of Idaho Falls. The sprawling research facility is about the size of Los Angeles.](https://www.idahostatesman.com/public/latest-news/kfawj7/picture260040710/alternates/FREE_1200/fluor%20(2))

The Radioactive Waste Management Complex at the Idaho National Laboratory site outside of Idaho Falls. The sprawling research facility is about the size of Los Angeles. U.S. Department of Energy via AP

Tuesday’s announcement continues a hot streak for Idaho’s nuclear sector: Last week the U.S. Department of Commerce awarded a five-year $31 million grant to support [Wyoming and Idaho’s joint Intermountain-West Nuclear Energy Corridor](https://inl.gov/news-release/idaho-national-laboratory-to-play-central-role-in-new-nuclear-energy-tech-hub/), a partnership that aims to bolster nuclear infrastructure, workforce training and supply chain development. The project is separate from Idaho’s pitch for the broader nuclear campus, Younger told the Idaho Statesman, but they do “go very nicely together,” she said.

Key to both is the Idaho National Laboratory. Nuclear energy has deep roots in that part of the state: Arco, Idaho, was the first town lit by atomic power in 1955. Today, the compound spans 900 square miles in Butte County, roughly twice the size of Los Angeles. Research at “the Site,” as locals call it, remains on the cutting edge of its field, hosting government scientists and private companies working on nuclear energy.

“For more than 70 years, Idaho has been at the [forefront of nuclear innovation](https://nuclear.idaho.gov/?utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery), and we are grateful to President Trump, Secretary (of Energy Chris) Wright, and the U.S. Department of Energy for recognizing our unmatched expertise and legacy,” Idaho Gov. Brad Little said in a statement. “We look forward to working with DOE to continue advancing every stage of the nuclear lifecycle, strengthening our nation’s energy security, creating good-paying jobs, and ensuring Idaho remains the home of America’s nuclear renaissance.”

Younger hasn’t heard what steps come next, though things are moving quickly. The federal government wants to launch its Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus in 2027, according to a March letter from Little to Secretary Wright.

## Watchdogs fear waste dump

![The Idaho National Laboratory unveils a micro-reactor test bed for private experiments on April 8, 2026. The federal Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus promises billions in private investment for winning states.](https://www.idahostatesman.com/public/latest-news/z10d4q/picture315373268/alternates/FREE_1200/INL%20Dome)

The Idaho National Laboratory unveils a micro-reactor test bed for private experiments on April 8, 2026. The federal Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus promises billions in private investment for winning states. Courtesy INL

Permanent nuclear storage has been a bright line in Idaho since 1995, when then-Gov. Phil Batt negotiated a unique deal with the Department of Energy to keep the state from becoming a de facto dumping ground for nuclear waste. T [he 1995 Settlement Agreement](https://gov.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/doe-inl-1995-settlement-agreement.pdf) remains a key lever allowing Idaho to determine what projects — and radioactive material — enter the state. It also requires that byproduct to leave at some point.

The state’s March application to the Department of Energy suggests that Little is open to revisiting that deal in exchange for “significant financial consideration” and “reaffirmations of DOE’s commitment to human safety, environmental protection, and regulatory rigor.”

“Future modifications will be discussed when relevant and appropriate,” the document states.

Little has [approved waivers to the agreement](https://www.idahostatesman.com/news/local/environment/article305331811.html). A year ago, for example, he allowed a cask of spent fuel from Virginia into Idaho for research. If Idaho is tapped for the new campus, more would likely follow. Less clear is what spent fuel might look like in the years ahead. Recent research has focused on recycling nuclear fuel so that less of it needs to be landfilled and would be less radioactive. [Oklo](https://oklo.com/overview/default.aspx), a California company partnering with INL, is already working on developing those methods in Idaho.

While Idaho’s pitch centers on the INL, the lab itself is a “terrible place” for permanent storage, Younger said. The complex sits on a porous bed of basalt over the Eastern Snake River Plain Aquifer, an underground reservoir [about the size of Lake Erie](https://www.isu.edu/digitalgeologyidaho/srp-aquifer/). Its water is one of Idaho’s most precious natural resources and a major engine of the [state’s $44.5 billion agricultural economy](https://www.uidaho.edu/newsroom/idaho-agriculture-economic-impact). The risk of contaminating the aquifer is a long and sincerely held fear among critics like Leigh Ford, executive director of the Snake River Alliance, a nuclear watchdog.

“Without the aquifer, life wouldn’t be possible for us here,” Ford told the Statesman in April. “Any amount of nuclear waste is inappropriate for Idaho, and it makes no sense at the INL. … That water is precious, and we need to keep it safe for future generations.”

On that, Younger agrees. Talking to the Statesman, Younger emphasized that the plan was in preliminary stages. Idaho, she said, is willing to investigate if there are appropriate sites for deep, underground storage near the complex, but that state isn’t bound to accept a deal just yet.

“So much of this has been building the plane while we’re flying it,” she said on Friday. “There are a lot of details left to work out.”

### ‘Where’s the money?’

![The INL is a key piece of Idaho’s pitch to host a Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus.](https://www.idahostatesman.com/public/latest-news/9sisnm/picture282161938/alternates/FREE_1200/INL_Google-Sept.-2021.jpg)

The INL is a key piece of Idaho’s pitch to host a Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus. Statesman file photo

Chief among them is the money, and that depends on Congress. The federal government has a Nuclear Waste Fund designed to pay a state to take on nuclear waste. Since there’s no place in the country open and able to do that, Congress hasn’t appropriated any money into it, Younger said. That would need to change, as well as the law designating Yucca Mountain as the sole depository, Younger said.

While 26 states expressed interest hosting the nuclear campus, “I don’t think any state has been eager” to take on nuclear waste without additional compensation, Younger said.

“If we’re selected, and if you’re going to send a third of all waste to our state, where’s the money?” she said. “We won’t do it if we won’t get paid.”

Still, Little’s vision for the project emphasizes private ventures and investment to the site, rather than building more government infrastructure, Younger told the Statesman in April.

“Economic growth is not just a bunch of DOE jobs in Southeast Idaho,” she said on Tuesday. “It’s private companies coming in and offering high-paying jobs at a bunch of education levels — that is what’s really compelling for us. It could totally change that part of the state.”
