State Arms of the Union (title page, illustrated, 1876)

**Historical coats of arms of the U.S. states** date back to the admission of the first [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._state "U.S. state") to the [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"). Despite the widely accepted practice of determining early statehood from the date of [ratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratification "Ratification") of the [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution "United States Constitution"), many of the [original colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies "Thirteen Colonies") referred to themselves as states shortly after the [Declaration of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence "United States Declaration of Independence") was signed on 4 July 1776. Committees of political leaders and intellectuals were established by state legislatures to research and propose a seal and coat of arms. Many of these members were signers of the [Articles of Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articles_of_Confederation "Articles of Confederation"), Declaration of Independence, and [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution "United States Constitution"). Several of the earliest adopted state [coats of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms "Coat of arms") and [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_\(emblem\) "Seal (emblem)") were similar or identical to their colonial counterparts.

*State Arms of the Union*, illustrated by Henry Mitchell and published by [Louis Prang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Prang "Louis Prang") (known as the father of the lithographic industry), offers historically accurate renderings of the state's coats of arms as they existed in 1876. An accomplished engraver with the [Bureau of Engraving and Printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Engraving_and_Printing "Bureau of Engraving and Printing") for 40 years, Mitchell was responsible for engraving several coats of arms for official state use as well as arms for well-known educational and philanthropic organizations. The illustrations are presented alongside proof impressions from the engraved dies used to print the state arms on the first issue of United States [National Bank Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bank_Note "National Bank Note").

## Coat of arms

[Heraldic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry "Heraldry") [arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achievement_\(heraldry\) "Achievement (heraldry)") were worn ([embroidered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embroidery "Embroidery")) on a coat which [knights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight "Knight") wore over their [armor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armor#Early "Armor"), hence [*coat* of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms "Coat of arms"),[^29] a term which dates back roughly 1,000 years [^30] to [jousting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jousting "Jousting") [tournaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tournament_\(medieval\) "Tournament (medieval)").[^31]

### Arms versus seal

A state coat of arms may exist independently of the [seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_\(emblem\) "Seal (emblem)"), but the reverse is not generally the case.[^32] A seal *contains* a coat of arms or other devices [^33] [^34] whereas a state coat of arms constitutes the bulk of a seal,[^34] [^35] except for the wording identifying it as the "Great Seal of the State of..." [^36] A "seal" has been described as the design *impressed* on public or legislative official documents,[^37] whereas a coat of arms generally appears for [illustrative purposes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illustration "Illustration"). Examples include [flags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag "Flag") and [banners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banner "Banner"),[^38] and state militia uniform caps [^39] and buttons,[^40] as well as specifically designed regimental coats of arms for [U.S. Infantry Regiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infantry_Branch_\(United_States\) "Infantry Branch (United States)"), and [National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Guard_of_the_United_States "National Guard of the United States") units.[^41]

> A coat of arms of a nation or state is usually the design or device of the obverse of its seal. It is an official emblem, mark of identification, and symbol of the authority of the government of a nation or state. A nation or state's coat of arms is oftentimes referred to as the national or state arms.[^42]

### Design

The design of a state coat of arms or seal has generally been authorized by a provision in the [state constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_constitution_\(United_States\) "State constitution (United States)") or a [legislative act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_act "Legislative act"). In most instances, a committee (more often than not consisting of three members) [^43] [^44] [^45] [^46] was appointed to study the issue, seek advice from qualified artists, historians, legal scholars, etc., and report back to the authorizing legislative body with a design for their approval. Historically, this committee has consisted of notable members of society and elected officials.

The first committee to design the [Great Seal of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seal_of_the_United_States "Great Seal of the United States") was appointed on 4 July 1776 by the [Second Continental Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Continental_Congress "Second Continental Congress") and consisted of [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin "Benjamin Franklin"), [John Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Adams "John Adams"), [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson "Thomas Jefferson").[^47] Their [design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seal_of_the_United_States#First_committee "Great Seal of the United States") was rejected on 20 August 1776. The second committee ([James Lovell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lovell_\(Continental_Congress\) "James Lovell (Continental Congress)"), [John Morin Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Morin_Scott "John Morin Scott"), and [William Churchill Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Churchill_Houston "William Churchill Houston")) [design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seal_of_the_United_States#Second_committee "Great Seal of the United States") met with the same fate.[^43] It was the third committee ([Arthur Middleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Middleton "Arthur Middleton"), [Elias Boudinot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Boudinot "Elias Boudinot"), [John Rutledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rutledge "John Rutledge"), who consulted with [William Barton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Barton_\(heraldist\) "William Barton (heraldist)")) that submitted a [design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seal_of_the_United_States#Third_committee "Great Seal of the United States") which was approved on 20 July 1782.[^43]

Individual states approached their coats of arms and seals in a similar manner (i.e., seeking direction from the statesmen and scholars of their community). A few of those involved in the design of state arms and seals include (but is not limited to): [John Jay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Jay "John Jay") and [Gouverneur Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gouverneur_Morris "Gouverneur Morris") ([New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_\(state\) "New York (state)"));[^44] [Francis Hopkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Hopkinson "Francis Hopkinson") (New Jersey);[^48] [David Rittenhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rittenhouse "David Rittenhouse") and [George Clymer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Clymer "George Clymer") ([Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania "Pennsylvania"));[^46] and [George Mason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mason "George Mason"), Benjamin Franklin, [Benjamin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_West "Benjamin West"), and Thomas Jefferson ([Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia "Virginia")).[^49]

### Authority

An impression of the Great Seal of a state (or its coat of arms) has long been required on official documents ranging from deeds to legislative acts. It was the emblem that certified the authenticity of a given document [^42] [^50] or that the authority of the state was invested in the said document.[^50] Judicial decisions upheld the need for a valid seal and/or coat of arms on notarized documents.[^1]

One of the more compelling legislative actions recognizing the legal importance/authority of the state seal and arms occurred in February 1873 when a [joint session of the United States Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_session_of_the_United_States_Congress "Joint session of the United States Congress") refused to recognize Arkansas's [electoral votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Electoral_College "United States Electoral College") in the [November 1872 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1872_United_States_presidential_election "1872 United States presidential election").[^51] The official tally of the state's electoral votes was submitted with an invalid seal (bearing the coat of arms of the office of the Secretary of the State of Arkansas versus the seal of the state of Arkansas bearing the state arms).[^51] [^52] [^53]

### Regulation

Courts and state legislatures also opined on the inappropriate uses of state seals and arms. Most states barred their use for any kind of advertising.[^2] Reproduction for corporate use was similarly prohibited [^54] and such infractions were classified as offenses against public property.[^55] The 2003 [Code of Federal Regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Federal_Regulations "Code of Federal Regulations") pertaining to the [Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Alcohol,_Tobacco,_Firearms_and_Explosives "Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives") prohibits the use of state seals or coats of arms in product branding so as not to mislead the public into thinking that a commercial product has been endorsed by a government organization.[^56]

### Instances of design inaccuracies

![View of Mount Logan as depicted on the Ohio state coat of arms](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/Great_Seal_of_Ohio_actual_view.jpg/250px-Great_Seal_of_Ohio_actual_view.jpg)

Ohio's seal depicts Mount Logan (elevation 1,243 ft (379 m) 57 ) and nearby summits in Chillicothe. 58

A state coat of arms provided an opportunity to convey the natural and industrial resources available to its residents.[^59] Common themes depicted in state arms include farming, industry, transportation (e.g., boats, trains, and wagons), and nature (e.g., sunsets and mountains). The [Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio "Ohio") and [Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana "Indiana") state arms depict fairly substantial mountains in the distance. In reality, the highest points in Ohio and Indiana are [Campbell Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell_Hill_\(Ohio\) "Campbell Hill (Ohio)") (1,550 feet (470 m)) [^60] and [Hoosier Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoosier_Hill "Hoosier Hill") (1,257 feet (383 m)) [^61] respectively.

The [Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida "Florida") state arms also depicts mountains in the distance but the highest point in the state is 345 feet (105 m) feet high.[^62] In addition to the distortion of local geography,[^63] the image also contains historically inaccurate information. The period depicted in the state arms (c. 1830) was a time when the local [Seminole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole "Seminole") [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States "Native Americans in the United States") were hostile toward white settlers; the warm greeting (e.g., flower petals strewn on the ground) offered by the Seminole to the arriving steam ship would have been highly improbable.[^63] Furthermore, the Seminole woman depicted would not have worn any [headdress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_bonnet "War bonnet"), particularly one of northern and western Seminole tribes.[^63]

## State Arms of the Union

Published in 1876 by [Louis Prang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Prang "Louis Prang") and illustrated by Henry Mitchell, *State Arms of the Union* contains a [chromolithographed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromolithography "Chromolithography") title page depicting the [Great Seal of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seal_of_the_United_States "Great Seal of the United States") and seven color plates with 45 state and territorial coats of arms. The book was likely published for the [Philadelphia Centennial Exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Centennial_Exposition "Philadelphia Centennial Exposition").[^64]

### Louis Prang

Louis Prang was born 12 March 1824 in [Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw "Wrocław"). At the age of 13 he began [apprenticing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apprenticeship "Apprenticeship") for his father [^65] and learned to dye and print [calico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico_\(textile\) "Calico (textile)"), as well as [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_engraving "Wood engraving") and [metal engraving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraving "Engraving").[^66] Prang emigrated to Boston in 1850 and became an illustrator for a number of local publications.[^67] Starting a business partnership in 1856 to manufacture copper and lithographic plates, Prang became sole proprietor in 1860 and named the company L. Prang & Co.[^66] He specialized in color printing, more specifically " [chromolithography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromolithography "Chromolithography") " [^65] Prang spent over four decades studying and creating a standard of colors [^68] and engraved and printed maps, prints of contemporary celebrities, and color reproductions of famous works of art.[^67]

In 1875 Prang was responsible for introducing the [Christmas card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_card "Christmas card") to America.[^66] He created an annual design competition for his Christmas cards (run between 1880 and 1884), and judges included [John La Farge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_La_Farge "John La Farge"), [Samuel Colman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Colman "Samuel Colman"), [Stanford White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_White "Stanford White"), and [Louis Comfort Tiffany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Comfort_Tiffany "Louis Comfort Tiffany").[^65] Some of the notable winners included [Elihu Vedder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elihu_Vedder "Elihu Vedder"), [Rosina Emmet Sherwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosina_Emmet_Sherwood "Rosina Emmet Sherwood"), [Edwin Blashfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Blashfield "Edwin Blashfield"),[^65] [Thomas Moran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Moran "Thomas Moran"), and [Will Hicok Low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Hicok_Low "Will Hicok Low").[^67] Prang has become known as the "father of the American Christmas card",[^65] [^66] [^67] as well as the "father of the lithographic industry".[^68] [^69]

### Henry Mitchell

Henry Mitchell was born in New York in 1835 and went to school in Philadelphia.[^70] At the age of 10 he began working with his uncle [^71] to learn the trade of [gem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraved_gem "Engraved gem") and steel engraving.[^72] By the age of 20 (1855), Mitchell had engraved the official seals for the [Kingdom of Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hawaii "Kingdom of Hawaii").[^72] [^73]

In 1868 Mitchell joined the [Bureau of Engraving and Printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Engraving_and_Printing "Bureau of Engraving and Printing") and for 40 years engraved [stamped envelopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamped_envelope "Stamped envelope").[^74] Through his BEP work, Mitchell was also responsible for engraving the seal of the [Secretary of the Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_the_Navy "Secretary of the Navy") and the [Internal Revenue Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Service "Internal Revenue Service").[^73] He also engraved the state seals for Massachusetts,[^72] New York,[^73] New Hampshire,[^73] Vermont,[^72] Rhode Island,[^71] and Wisconsin.[^75] Outside of state and federal government engraving, Mitchell engraved the seals and coats of arms for many well-known institutions which include [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), [Society of the Cincinnati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_the_Cincinnati "Society of the Cincinnati"), and [Boston Public Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Public_Library "Boston Public Library").[^73] He engraved the Philadelphia [Centennial Exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centennial_Exhibition "Centennial Exhibition") award medal (1876) which was struck in the Philadelphia Mint.[^76] In 1891, Mitchell was invited by the [Secretary of the Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_the_Treasury "Secretary of the Treasury") to join a committee to evaluate the artistic design proposals for a new issue of U.S. coins. The two other members were [Charles E. Barber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_E._Barber "Charles E. Barber"), [Chief Engraver of the United States Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Engraver_of_the_United_States_Mint "Chief Engraver of the United States Mint"), and [Augustus Saint-Gaudens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_Saint-Gaudens "Augustus Saint-Gaudens").[^77] [^78]

In addition to being considered an expert on heraldry,[^73] Mitchell was regarded as one of the best engravers [^70] [^71] and medal designers in the United States.[^79]

## State Arms depicted on United States banknotes

The [National Bank Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bank_Act "National Bank Act") (12 [Stat.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Statutes_at_Large "United States Statutes at Large") [532](https://legislink.org/us/stat-12-532)) authorized the issue of a [national currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bank_Note "National Bank Note"). [Historical vignettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_and_engraving_on_United_States_banknotes "Art and engraving on United States banknotes") on the front and back were the same by denomination (e.g., *Landing of Columbus* was on the reverse of all $5 notes) [^80] and the state coat of arms (located on the reverse left side) was coordinated with the geographic location of the issuing bank.[^80] Records do not clearly state who bore the responsibility for the design of the state arms (i.e., the U.S. Treasury Department, or the three bank note companies contracted for engraving and printing).[^81] It appears that the first dies (for [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey"), [Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri "Missouri"), [Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota "Minnesota"), and [Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermont "Vermont")) were completed by the American Bank Note Company by 9 October 1863 based on their own drawings.[^82] State arms appeared on the reverse of the Original and 1875 Series notes (first and second issue of the first design), and the 1882 Brown Back Series (the second design) of National Bank Notes.

### Missing territorial or state coats of arms

When *State Arms of the Union* was published in 1876, some existing arms were not included (e.g., Arizona and Washington Territory). At the time, Alaska was classified as the [Department of Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Alaska "Department of Alaska") (1867–84) and became the [District of Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Alaska "District of Alaska") (1884–1912) before becoming the [Territory of Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territory_of_Alaska "Territory of Alaska") (1912–59). The Alaska territorial seal was designed in 1910 [^143] and adopted in 1913.[^144] On 3 January 1959 Alaska became the 49th U.S. State.[^145] The [Oklahoma Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Territory "Oklahoma Territory") (1890–1907) Organic Act was approved on 2 May 1890, and a territorial seal was adopted on 10 January 1893.[^146] [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii "Hawaii"), formerly the [Kingdom of Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hawaii "Kingdom of Hawaii") (1795–1893), [Republic of Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Hawaii "Republic of Hawaii") (1894–98), and then [Territory of Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territory_of_Hawaii "Territory of Hawaii") (1898–1959) became the 50th U.S. State on 21 August 1959.[^147] None of the territories or states mentioned above had a coat of arms represented on national currency.
